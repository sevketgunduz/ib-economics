    Preferential trade agreements: two, a region, or (almost) everyone

    A preferential trade agreement (PTA) gives its members better access to each other’s markets than outsiders get (Hoang pp. 469–471). Agreements are grouped by how many countries sign. Click each card for examples and the main trade-off.

    04

    Free trade area or customs union? Follow the cheapest route

    In a free trade area each member keeps its own tariff on outsiders. In a customs union all members charge one common external tariff (Hoang top tip, p. 473). An exporter outside the bloc wants to sell a $100 good in member A. Can it dodge A’s tariff by shipping through low-tariff member B first?

    Illustrative model: one unit, world price $100, constant costs. Sending goods through the lowest-tariff member to avoid a higher tariff is called trade deflection.

    05

    Trade creation or trade diversion? HL

    Country H buys a good from whoever sells it cheapest after tariffs. It then forms a customs union with a partner: tariffs on the partner disappear, but the common external tariff still applies to the rest of the world. Change the costs and the tariff and see which supplier wins, before and after (Hoang pp. 474–476).

    Illustrative model: each country produces at a constant cost, and H buys a fixed number of units. The welfare change shown is per unit and leaves out extra units bought at the lower price.

    07

    One interest rate, two economies HL

    In a monetary union one central bank (the ECB for the eurozone) sets one interest rate for every member. Set the inflation rate and output gap in a large “North” and a small “South” and compare the rate each would choose alone with the single rate (Hoang pp. 477–479).

    Illustrative model: each economy’s preferred rate follows a simple Taylor-type rule, 1% + inflation + ½(inflation − 2%) + ½(output gap). The union rate uses GDP-weighted averages. The rule shows the direction of the problem, not the ECB’s actual decisions.

    08

    The WTO: one rule for all, and agreement by everyone

    The WTO (1995, replacing GATT) works on non-discrimination: under the most favoured nation principle, a tariff cut offered to one member must be offered to all (Hoang pp. 479–480). Bilateral agreements and regional blocs are the two exceptions (p. 483). Decisions need consensus, which is why the Doha Round, launched in 2001, stalled.
