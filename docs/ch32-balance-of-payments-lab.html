    Credit or debit, and which account?

    Every transaction with the rest of the world goes somewhere. Money coming in is a credit (+); money going out is a debit (−). Click a transaction to watch it drop into the right place (Hoang Tables 32.1–32.2, pp. 507–508).

    03

    Build the current account: GIST

    Goods · Income · Services · Transfers. Income means factor income — wages, interest, rent and profit (WIRP) — not export earnings. Enter the figures and the balances build up as the examiner expects to see them.

    Enter imports as positive numbers; the calculator subtracts them. Units are whatever the question uses ($bn, $m): keep them consistent and state them in your answer.

    04

    Why the balance of payments always balances

    A country that spends more abroad than it earns has to find the money somewhere: borrow it, sell assets, or run down reserves. Every one of those is recorded too, so the accounts must sum to zero. Change any figure and watch errors and omissions absorb the difference.

    Correction to Hoang p. 514: the book writes “Current account = Capital account + Financial account”. With the sign convention used in its own exercises (debits negative), the identity is CA + KA + FA + E&O = 0. Use the sign form and EPQ 32.3 comes out right; use the book's version and you will lose the minus sign.

    06

    The accounts and the exchange rate HL

    Here is the link that ties this chapter to Chapter 31. On a currency diagram, demand is the credits and supply is the debits, so at equilibrium the balance of payments balances (Hoang Fig 32.6, p. 515). Apply a shock and see which account moved it.

    Illustrative model: linear demand and supply for the currency. Demand = all credits (exports, inward investment, transfers received); supply = all debits (imports, outward investment).

    07

    A persistent deficit: what follows, and what to do HL

    One year of deficit means little. A deficit of 5–7% of GDP for a decade is another matter (Hoang pp. 516–518). Set the size and how it is financed, then try the three families of policy.

    Policy response

    Illustrative risk model: the score weighs the deficit's size against how easily its financing could reverse. It shows the direction of the argument, not a forecast.

    08

    Marshall–Lerner and the J-curve HL

    A depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer — so why does the trade balance often get worse first? Because in the short run nobody can switch quickly. Build the diagram step by step, then move the elasticities and watch the curve change shape.

    Label checklist: y-axis “Trade balance” with a zero line · x-axis “Time” · start at A in deficit at the moment of depreciation · dip to B (Marshall–Lerner not met) · rise through zero to C (condition met) · annotate both segments.
    09

    Is a persistent surplus good news? HL

    Students assume a surplus must be a success. It depends entirely on why it exists (Hoang top tip, p. 524). Pick the causes and see what follows for the economy and for its trading partners.
