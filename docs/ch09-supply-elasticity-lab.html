    02

    Price elastic vs price inelastic supply

    Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how strongly quantity supplied responds to a change in price. Give the same price rise to a soft-drink maker and to house builders.

    03

    Calculate PES

    PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. Because of the law of supply, price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, so PES is positive.

    Working backwards: find the new quantity supplied

    Hoang practice 9.1: PES = 3.0, price falls from $13 to $11.70, and 5,000 units were supplied.

    04

    Degrees of PES

    PES runs from 0 (perfectly inelastic) to ∞ (perfectly elastic). A handy rule for straight lines: a supply curve that cuts the price axis is elastic, one that cuts the quantity axis is inelastic, and any line through the origin has PES = 1.

    05

    Build a supply curve: TICCS

    Time · Inventory (ability to store) · Costs (how fast marginal cost rises) · Capacity (spare) · Substitution (mobility) of factors. Switch the conditions on and see how elastic supply becomes, and what happens when demand rises.

    Illustrative model: each condition multiplies a base PES of 0.2.

    06

    Time: the most important determinant

    Right after a price rise, firms can hardly respond (momentary period). In the short run they can add workers and shifts. In the long run they can build factories and new firms can enter. Demand for fresh fruit rises sharply. Pick a time period.

    07

    Primary commodities vs manufactured goods HL

    Raw cocoa takes years to grow, rots if stored badly, and needs specific land and weather. Chocolate bars come off flexible factory lines and can be stockpiled. Raise demand for both by the same amount.

    Approximation: %ΔP ≈ demand shift ÷ (PES + PED), with PED = 0.5 for both goods and PES 0.1 for cocoa and 2 for chocolate bars (illustrative values).
