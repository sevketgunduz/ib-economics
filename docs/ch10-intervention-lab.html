    Why do governments intervene? FETCH PM

    Governments step into markets to support Firms, promote Equity, earn Tax revenue, influence Consumption, support low-income Households, influence Production and correct Market failure. Match each policy to its main reason.

    One market for the whole lab: Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = −20 + 2P (Q in thousands). Without intervention, equilibrium is $30 and 40,000 units, with consumer surplus $400,000 and producer surplus $400,000.

    03

    Price ceiling (maximum price)

    A legal maximum price set below equilibrium to keep a good affordable: rent controls, bread and fuel price caps. Lower the ceiling and watch the shortage grow (Hoang Fig 10.6).

    04

    Price floor (minimum price)

    A legal minimum price set above equilibrium to support producers’ incomes, such as farm price guarantees, or workers’ wages (a national minimum wage, Fig 10.12).

    05

    Indirect taxes

    A specific tax adds a fixed amount per unit, so supply shifts up in parallel (Fig 10.15). An ad valorem tax is a percentage, like VAT, so supply pivots upwards (Fig 10.16). The tax is paid by firms, but part is passed on to consumers.

    06

    Who really pays the tax? Incidence and elasticity

    The side of the market that is less price elastic pays the bigger share (Figs 10.17–10.19). Try the presets, or set the steepness of each curve yourself. The tax is $4 per unit.

    07

    Subsidies

    A payment to producers that lowers their costs, so supply shifts down (right) by the subsidy per unit (Fig 10.21). Consumers pay less, producers receive more, and taxpayers foot the bill.

    08

    Command and control, and direct provision

    Instead of changing prices, governments can make rules (command and control: emission limits, bans, age limits) or supply goods themselves (direct provision: schools, hospitals, roads). Below, a regulator orders two factories to cut pollution by the same percentage.

    09

    Consumer nudges HL

    A nudge changes how a choice is presented (defaults, framing, social norms) without banning options or changing prices much. Is each policy a nudge?

    UK pension auto-enrolment (2012) made saving the default: participation rose from about 42% to over 85% of eligible employees with almost no change in tax incentives. That is a cheap nudge, but default contribution rates may be too low.
