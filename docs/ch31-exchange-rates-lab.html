    02

    Build the currency-market diagram

    A currency is bought and sold like any other good, so it has a demand curve and a supply curve (Hoang Fig 31.3, p. 487). Examiners lose patience with vague labels, so build this one piece at a time and copy the wording exactly.

    Label checklist: y-axis “Price of A$ in £ (£ per A$)” · x-axis “Quantity of A$” · the currency named on both curve labels (DA$, SA$) · equilibrium ERe and Qe marked with dotted lines · the cause written beside any shift arrow.
    03

    What moves the rate?

    Demand for a currency is an inflow of money into the country; supply is an outflow (Hoang top tip, p. 488). Click an event to see which curve moves and which way the rate goes. The dashed grey curve is where it started.

    Illustrative model: linear demand and supply for the Australian dollar priced in pounds. Each event shifts one curve by a set amount so the direction is clear; real shifts vary in size.

    04

    Converting prices between currencies

    Two rules save you in the exam. If the rate is quoted as 1 unit of A = x units of B, then multiply to go from A to B, and divide to go from B to A. Always ask yourself first whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than what you started with.

    Cross rates

    When two currencies are both quoted against a third, you can find the rate between them (Hoang EPQ 31.3).

    05

    How far has the currency moved?

    The trap: if currency A appreciates 27.8% against B, B does not depreciate by 27.8% against A. The two percentages use different starting values. Work out both and see the gap for yourself (Hoang EPQ 31.5, p. 494).

    06

    Inflow or outflow?

    Every transaction either brings money into the country (demand for the currency) or sends it out (supply of the currency). Decide which, and the diagram follows automatically.

    07

    CUPEL: what a change in the rate does to the economy

    Current account · Unemployment · Prices · Economic growth · Living standards (Hoang Fig 31.11, p. 494). A depreciation pushes AD right as net exports rise, and pushes SRAS left as imported inputs cost more. Move the slider and watch both channels at once.

    Illustrative model: AD and SRAS shift in proportion to the size of the rate change. The Marshall–Lerner verdict uses the sum of the two elasticities, which is the correct condition (see the note below).

    Correction to Hoang p. 496: the book says the current account changes if the PED for exports and the PED for imports are each greater than one. The correct test is the Marshall–Lerner condition: the sum of the two must exceed one. So PEDx 0.8 + PEDm 0.7 = 1.5 passes, even though neither is above one on its own.
    08

    Defend the peg

    In a fixed system the central bank holds the rate at one level; in a managed system it steps in only when the rate leaves a band (Hoang Figs 31.18, 31.20 and 31.22). Both cost reserves. Apply shocks and see how long you last.

    Illustrative model: reserves start at 100 units and each unit of intervention costs one unit of reserves. A rate rise pulls demand back towards the peg but is assumed to slow the domestic economy. Real defences also use capital controls and credibility.
