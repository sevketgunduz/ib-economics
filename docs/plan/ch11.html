Unit 2 · Microeconomics · Hoang Chapter 11

Market failure – externalities and common pool (common access) resources

Syllabus 2.8 Market failure – externalities and common pool or common access resourcesTextbook pp. 140–164Sessions 7 HL · 5 SLConcepts sustainability, efficiency, intervention, interdependence, economic well-being

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? Why does the free market produce too much pollution and plastic and too little vaccination and education, and how can we use shared resources without destroying them?

Common SL/HL: socially optimum output, the four externality diagrams, merit and demerit goods, common pool resources, government responses (PIGLETS CC) and their evaluation, international cooperation. HL only: calculating welfare loss from a diagram and calculating consumer and producer surplus (pp. 142, 144–145) – Sessions 6–7.

Key terms and definitions

Market failure When the price mechanism fails to allocate scarce resources efficiently, so the market produces more or less than the socially optimum output.
Social cost The full cost to society of an activity: private costs plus external costs.
Social benefit The full benefit to society of an activity: private benefits plus external benefits.
Marginal social benefit (MSB) The extra benefit to society, including third parties, from consuming one more unit of a good.
Marginal social cost (MSC) The extra cost to society, including third parties, of producing one more unit of a good.
Socially optimum output The output where MSB = MSC. Social surplus is at its maximum and resources are allocated efficiently.
Allocative efficiency Resources are allocated to produce the combination of goods society values most, where MSB = MSC (or P = MC). No one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
Social (community) surplus The sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus. It is maximised at the allocatively efficient output.
Externality A cost or benefit to a third party who is not directly involved in producing or consuming a good, and that is not reflected in the market price.
Positive externality An external benefit to third parties. For consumption, MSB > MPB; for production, MSC < MPC. The good is underprovided.
Negative externality An external cost to third parties. For production, MSC > MPC; for consumption, MSB < MPB. The good is overprovided.
Merit good A good that creates positive externalities, so that MSB > MPB. It is under-consumed in a free market, e.g. education, vaccination, healthcare.
Demerit good A good that creates negative externalities and is over-consumed in a free market, e.g. tobacco and alcohol. What counts as a demerit good depends partly on value judgements.
Welfare loss The loss of social surplus when output is not at the social optimum. It is shown by the triangle between MSB and MSC from the market output to the optimum output.
Common pool resource A resource that is rivalrous (use by one reduces what is left for others) but non-excludable (hard to stop people using it), e.g. fish stocks, groundwater, the atmosphere.
Tragedy of the commons Hardin's (1968) idea that individuals acting in their own interest overuse a shared resource until it is degraded or destroyed, even though this is against everyone's long-run interest.
Pigouvian tax An indirect tax equal to the marginal external cost. It internalises a negative externality, shifting S = MPC up to MSC so output falls to the social optimum.
Carbon tax A tax on greenhouse gas emissions or on the carbon content of fossil fuels. It raises the costs of polluting firms and gives an incentive to switch to cleaner production.
Tradable permits (cap and trade) The government caps total emissions and issues permits that add up to the cap. Firms can buy and sell permits, so emissions are cut by the firms that can do it most cheaply.
Collective self-governance Users of a common pool resource manage it through their own rules, monitoring and sanctions, without relying on the state or the market (Ostrom).
International agreement A bilateral or multilateral treaty in which countries commit to shared targets, such as the Kyoto Protocol or the Paris Agreement.
Education (awareness creation) Government campaigns that inform consumers about the true costs and benefits of goods, shifting MPB towards MSB.
Legislation and regulation Laws and rules that limit or ban harmful activities, or require beneficial ones, enforced by monitoring and penalties.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Sustainability

Ireland's plastic bag levy

In 2002 Ireland introduced a €0.15 levy on each plastic shopping bag at the till. Before the levy, around 1.2 billion bags were used each year (about 328 per person). Within months bag use fell by about 90%, and the revenue went to an environment fund. Scotland followed with a 5p charge in 2014.

Ask the class
• What type of externality do plastic bags create?
• Why did a small levy have such a large effect?
• How should the revenue be used?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Plastic bags create a negative externality of consumption (litter, marine damage), so MSB < MPB and bags are over-consumed.
  2. The levy acts as a Pigouvian tax that raises the price from zero to €0.15.
  3. Demand is very price elastic because close substitutes exist (reusable bags), so quantity falls sharply.
  4. Output moves towards the social optimum and the welfare loss shrinks. The revenue can pay for clean-ups.
  5. Evaluate. It works because substitutes exist. Taxes on goods with inelastic demand, such as cigarettes, cut use far less but raise more revenue.
DiagramNegative consumption externality with an indirect tax (Figs 11.10 and 11.14). Axes: Price (€) and Quantity of bags. Draw MPB above MSB and S = MPC = MSC. Mark Qm at MPB = MSC. Show the levy shifting S up so that output falls towards Qopt (MSB = MSC). Shade the welfare loss removed.
Scenario 2 · Sustainability

Overfishing in the North-East Atlantic

Cod stocks off Newfoundland collapsed in 1992 after decades of heavy fishing, and about 30,000 people lost their jobs. EU fisheries now use total allowable catches, and the FAO estimates that over a third of world fish stocks are fished at unsustainable levels. Individual fishing crews earn more by catching more today, even though the stock shrinks.

Ask the class
• Why is a fishery a common pool resource?
• Why does the free market lead to overfishing?
• Which government or community responses could work?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Fish in open seas are rivalrous (a fish caught by one boat is not available to another) and non-excludable (it is hard to stop boats fishing).
  2. Each boat compares its private benefit with its private cost, ignoring the cost it imposes on others by lowering the stock. This is a negative production externality.
  3. This leads to the tragedy of the commons: individually rational choices produce a collective collapse.
  4. Responses: catch quotas (regulation), tradable quotas (like permits), international agreements for shared waters, and Ostrom-style collective self-governance in local fisheries.
  5. Evaluate. Fish stocks are hard to measure, fishing is hard to monitor at sea, and countries such as Japan refuse some protocols (p. 163), so cooperation and enforcement are hard.
DiagramNegative production externality (Fig 11.9) for the fish market. Axes: Price and Quantity of fish. Draw MSC above S = MPC, with D = MPB = MSB. Mark Qm > Qopt and shade the welfare loss. Optionally, add a vertical quota line at Qopt.
Scenario 3 · Intervention

Subsidising electric vehicles in China

China paid purchase subsidies of up to 60,000 yuan (about $9,800) per electric or hydrogen vehicle (Hoang p. 159). Sales of new-energy vehicles grew from under 100,000 in 2014 to over 1 million in 2018. Critics argued that some firms claimed subsidies for vehicles that were never sold, and the scheme cost billions of yuan a year.

Ask the class
• Why might EVs be under-consumed in a free market?
• Show the effect of the subsidy.
• What are the risks of this policy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Compared with petrol cars, EVs reduce air pollution and carbon emissions, so there is a positive externality: MSB > MPB.
  2. The market settles where MPB = MSC, at Qm, which is below Qopt.
  3. A subsidy lowers producers' costs and shifts supply down/right, so the price falls and quantity rises towards Qopt.
  4. The government cost is subsidy × new quantity. This has an opportunity cost, and some firms committed fraud (government failure).
  5. Evaluate. The subsidy helped build the industry and brought economies of scale. It was phased out as EVs became competitive, which shows subsidies work best as temporary measures.
DiagramPositive consumption externality corrected by a subsidy (Fig 11.23 adapted). Axes: Price and Quantity of EVs. Draw MSB above MPB = D. Shift S to S − subsidy so the new equilibrium on D is at Qopt. Shade the government spending rectangle.
Scenario 4 · Interdependence

The EU Emissions Trading System

The EU ETS, launched in 2005, covers more than 11,000 power stations and factories. Permit prices were below €10 per tonne for much of 2012–2017 because too many permits had been issued. After reforms that removed surplus permits, the price rose above €80 per tonne in 2022.

Ask the class
• How does cap and trade reduce emissions?
• Why was the permit price so low at first?
• What happened when the cap was tightened?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The cap fixes the total quantity of permits, so supply is vertical (Fig 11.20).
  2. Firms that can cut emissions cheaply sell permits; firms facing high abatement costs buy them. The cap is met at the lowest total cost.
  3. Too many permits plus the 2008–09 recession, which lowered demand, meant a low price and little incentive to cut emissions.
  4. Removing permits shifted the vertical supply left (Fig 11.21), which raised the price and strengthened the incentive to invest in clean technology.
  5. Evaluate. The ETS is market-based and gives certainty about the quantity of emissions, but not about price. There are risks of lobbying for free permits and of carbon leakage to countries without a cap.
DiagramTradable permits market (Figs 11.20–11.21). Axes: Price of permits (€/tonne) and Quantity of permits. Draw a vertical supply at the cap and a downward-sloping demand. Show the cap cut from S1 to S2, raising the price from P1 to P2.

Simulation and data activities

Fishing (common pool) game with sweets/counters

Put groups of four around a 'lake' of 16 counters. Each round every student secretly writes how many fish they will take, then the teacher removes them; whatever remains doubles, up to a maximum of 16. Play five rounds and record the stock. In a second game, allow the group two minutes to agree rules and a punishment for cheating (collective self-governance), then replay. Compare total catches and final stock between the two games.

Debrief
• Why did the lake collapse in the first game even though everyone lost?
• What rules did your group create, and how did you enforce them?
• Which PIGLETS CC response did the second game model?

Veconlab – Common pool resource experiment · veconlab.econ.virginia.edu

Log in as administrator and set up the 'Common Pool Resource' game with 6–8 players per group. Students join on phones, choose extraction levels each round and see their earnings. After five rounds, switch on an extraction fee (a Pigouvian tax) and play five more. Download the results chart and project it.

Debrief
• How did total extraction change when the fee was introduced?
• Did the fee move extraction close to the efficient level?
• Who gained and who lost?

Course Simulator – Externality welfare loss

Project the tool. For each of the four externality types, students predict on mini-whiteboards which curve will separate and where the welfare loss will be, then the teacher reveals it. HL students enter linear values and read off the welfare loss, then check it by hand. Finish by applying a tax or subsidy equal to the external effect and confirming that the welfare loss disappears.

Debrief
• Why does the welfare loss triangle always point towards Qopt?
• What happens to the welfare loss when the external cost doubles?
• Why is the 'right' Pigouvian tax hard to set in real life?

Cap-and-trade card game

Give six students 'firm' cards, each with emissions of 3 tonnes and a different cost of cutting one tonne ($5–$60). Distribute 12 permits (for 18 tonnes of emissions). Firms trade permits in an open market for 10 minutes; any firm with more emissions than permits must pay to cut. Record trades and the average price. Cut the cap to 9 permits and repeat.

Debrief
• Which firms sold permits, and why?
• Why did the permit price rise in round two?
• Why is cap and trade cheaper for society than ordering every firm to cut by the same amount?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. A market has MPB (demand): P = 100 − Q; MPC (supply): P = 20 + Q; the external cost is $20 per unit, so MSC: P = 40 + Q (Q in thousands). Find the market output and price, the social optimum and the welfare loss. HL

SolutionMarket: 100 − Q = 20 + Q gives Q = 40,000 and P = $60. Optimum: 100 − Q = 40 + Q gives Q = 30,000 and P = $70. At Qm the gap MSC − MSB = (40 + 40) − 60 = $20. Welfare loss = ½ × (40,000 − 30,000) × $20 = $100,000.

Problem 2. Using the same market, a Pigouvian tax of $20 per unit is imposed. Calculate the tax revenue and the burden on consumers and producers. HL

SolutionThe tax shifts MPC to MSC, so output is 30,000 and the consumer price is $70. Producers receive 70 − 20 = $50. Revenue = 20 × 30,000 = $600,000. Consumer burden = (70 − 60) × 30,000 = $300,000; producer burden = (60 − 50) × 30,000 = $300,000.

Problem 3. A merit good has MPB: P = 80 − Q; MSB: P = 100 − Q (external benefit $20 per unit); S = MPC = MSC: P = 20 + Q (Q in thousands). Calculate the welfare loss. HL

SolutionMarket: 80 − Q = 20 + Q gives Qm = 30,000, P = $50. Optimum: 100 − Q = 20 + Q gives Qopt = 40,000. At Qm, MSB = 70 and MSC = 50, so the gap is $20. Welfare loss = ½ × 10,000 × $20 = $100,000.

Problem 4. In the merit-good market above, the government pays producers a $20 subsidy per unit. Find the new price, the quantity and the cost to the government. HL

SolutionNew supply: P = Q. 80 − Q = Q gives Q = 40,000 = Qopt; consumers pay $40 and producers receive $60. Government cost = $20 × 40,000 = $800,000.

Problem 5. In the allocatively efficient market with D: P = 100 − Q and S: P = 20 + Q (no externalities), calculate consumer, producer and social surplus. HL

SolutionEquilibrium Q = 40,000, P = $60. CS = ½ × 40,000 × (100 − 60) = $800,000. PS = ½ × 40,000 × (60 − 20) = $800,000. Social surplus = $1,600,000.

Problem 6. Tradable permits: the cap is 100,000 permits and they are auctioned. Economic growth raises the permit price from €20 to €35. Calculate the change in government permit revenue. HL

SolutionRevenue before = 100,000 × €20 = €2m; after = 100,000 × €35 = €3.5m; an increase of €1.5m. Quantity is unchanged because supply is fixed by the cap (Fig 11.20).

Problem 7. Fig 11.24 (p. 159): identify the change in consumer surplus, the change in producer surplus, the government spending and the deadweight loss from the subsidy using the labelled areas. HL

SolutionConsumer surplus rises from A+B to A+B+E+F+G, a gain of E+F+G. Producer surplus rises from E+I to B+C+E+I, a gain of B+C. Government spending is B+C+D+E+F+G+H. The deadweight loss is D+H: the spending that is not received as extra surplus by anyone.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Externalities are always negative. Positive externalities (education, vaccination, beekeeping) are just as important (p. 143).
  • Merit goods are public goods. Merit goods are usually rivalrous and excludable, e.g. private clinics and selective schools (p. 145).
  • For a negative production externality, MSB shifts. It is MSC that lies above MPC. For consumption externalities the gap is on the benefit side.
  • The welfare loss is the whole area between MSC and MPC. It is only the triangle between MSC and MSB between Qopt and Qm.
  • A Pigouvian tax 'bans' pollution. It aims at the socially optimum level of pollution, which is usually above zero.
  • Common pool resources are the same as public goods. CPRs are rivalrous, while public goods are non-rivalrous (p. 150).
  • Tradable permits fix the price of pollution. They fix the quantity (the cap); the price is set by the market for permits.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Fig 11.15 caption (p. 151) dates Scotland's 5p carrier-bag charge to October 2013; the Scottish charge actually began on 20 October 2014 (Wales was 2011, Northern Ireland 2013). Also, a 5p charge is a levy retained by retailers rather than a government tax, which is worth pointing out when classifying it as a Pigouvian measure.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. State the condition for the socially optimum output.

MSB = MSC.

2. Social cost equals what?

Private cost plus external cost.

3. For a merit good, which is larger: MSB or MPB?

MSB, because consumption creates external benefits.

4. Where is the welfare loss for a negative production externality?

The triangle between MSC and MSB from Qopt to Qm.

5. Why is a fishery a common pool resource?

It is rivalrous (fish caught by one are gone for others) and non-excludable (hard to stop people fishing).

6. What should the size of a Pigouvian tax be?

Equal to the marginal external cost at the social optimum, so MPC shifts up to MSC.

7. Which curve does an education campaign on healthy eating shift?

MPB (demand) to the right, towards MSB.

8. Give one strength and one limitation of tradable permits.

Strength: emissions are cut at the lowest cost. Limitation: it is hard to set the right cap, and firms lobby for free permits.

9. What did Ostrom show about common pool resources?

Communities can manage them sustainably through their own rules, monitoring and sanctions, without privatisation or the state.

10. Give two reasons international cooperation on climate change is hard.

Free-riding by countries that do not cut emissions; difficulty in monitoring and enforcing agreements; different priorities of rich and poor countries.

11. Why are externalities hard to measure?

Third-party costs and benefits have no market price, are spread over many people and years, and depend on value judgements.

12. HL: Qm = 50,000, Qopt = 40,000, marginal external cost = $10. What is the welfare loss?

½ × 10,000 × $10 = $50,000.

13. HL: a $5 subsidy raises output to 60,000 units. What is the government cost?

$5 × 60,000 = $300,000.
