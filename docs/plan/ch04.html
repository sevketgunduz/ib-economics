Key terms and definitions

Supply The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at each possible price during a given time period, ceteris paribus.
Quantity supplied The amount producers are willing and able to sell at one particular price; a point on the supply curve.
Law of supply There is a positive relationship between the price of a good and its quantity supplied, ceteris paribus, because higher prices raise potential profit and attract new producers.
Supply curve A graph showing the relationship between price and quantity supplied; it slopes upward from left to right.
Short run HLThe period in which at least one factor of production (usually capital) is fixed.
Long run HLThe period in which all factors of production are variable.
Law of diminishing marginal returns HLIn the short run, as more units of a variable factor are added to a fixed factor, the extra output from each additional unit eventually falls.
Marginal cost (MC) HLThe extra cost of producing one more unit of output (ΔTC ÷ ΔQ).
Increasing marginal costs HLBecause of diminishing returns, each extra unit costs more to produce, so firms need a higher price to supply more.
Market supply The sum of all individual producers' supply at each price (horizontal summation).
Non-price determinants of supply Factors other than price that shift the supply curve: costs of factors, indirect taxes, subsidies, technology, expectations, related goods, number of firms (CISTERN).
Competitive supply Goods that use the same resources, so producing more of one means producing less of the other (e.g. wheat and barley on the same land).
Joint supply Goods produced together, so an increase in the output of one automatically increases the supply of the other (e.g. beef and leather).
Indirect tax A tax on spending on goods and services, paid by producers to government; it raises costs and shifts supply left.
Subsidy A payment from government to producers that lowers their costs and shifts supply right.
Movement along the supply curve A change in quantity supplied caused only by a change in the good's own price: an expansion (price rises) or a contraction (price falls).
Shift of the supply curve A change in supply at every price caused by a non-price determinant: an increase (rightward) or a decrease (leftward).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Change

Frost and Brazilian coffee

Brazil produces roughly a third of the world's coffee. In July 2021 severe frosts damaged an estimated 10% of arabica trees in Minas Gerais, and new trees take about three years to bear fruit.

Ask the class
• Which curve shifts and in which direction?
• Why can farmers not respond quickly?
• What happens to supply of coffee from Vietnam or Colombia?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Weather is not in the syllabus list, but it works through the productive capacity of land (a factor of production); fewer productive trees mean less can be supplied at every price.
  2. The Brazilian supply curve shifts left from S1 to S2.
  3. Because trees take years to mature, supply stays reduced for several seasons.
  4. Growers elsewhere face no change in costs; their supply curves do not shift, although they will expand quantity supplied if the world price rises (a movement, analysed in Chapter 5).
DiagramSupply diagram for Brazilian coffee: S1 shifts left to S2; at price P1 quantity supplied falls from Q1 to Q2.
Scenario 2 · Sustainability

Solar panel subsidies in Germany

Germany's government offers subsidies and guaranteed payments to firms installing solar panels. Meanwhile, the cost of photovoltaic cells has fallen by about 90% since 2010 due to technological progress and mass production in China.

Ask the class
• Identify two determinants of supply involved.
• Show the combined effect.
• Why might the subsidy be justified?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A subsidy lowers producers' costs, shifting supply of solar installations right.
  2. Technological progress also lowers unit costs and shifts supply right.
  3. The combined effect is a large rightward shift from S1 to S2 (or S3).
  4. Government intervenes because solar power reduces carbon emissions, linking to sustainability and to externalities in Unit 2.8.
DiagramSupply of solar installations: S1 shifts right to S2 (subsidy) and further to S3 (technology).
Scenario 3 · Interdependence

Rising beef demand and the leather market

Rising incomes in China and Indonesia increase demand for beef, and Australian cattle ranchers raise their herds. Leather tanneries report more hides available at lower cost.

Ask the class
• Explain the effect on the supply of leather.
• What type of supply relationship is this?
• How would the effect on wheat farming differ if ranchers converted wheat fields to pasture?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Higher beef prices cause an expansion of quantity supplied of beef (movement along S).
  2. Beef and leather are in joint supply; more cattle slaughtered automatically increases hide output.
  3. Supply of leather shifts right (Hoang Fig 4.8 uses beef and milk).
  4. If land is switched from wheat to cattle, wheat and beef are in competitive supply, so wheat supply shifts left (Fig 4.6).
DiagramTwo panels: beef market (movement along S from A to B after price rise); leather market (S1 → S2 right). Optional third: wheat (S1 → S3 left).
Scenario 4 · Intervention

India's cigarette tax

India levies a combination of GST and compensation cess on cigarettes that takes total taxes to around 50% of retail price (Hoang uses a 40% tax in ATL 4.5). Tobacco firms face higher costs per packet sold.

Ask the class
• Which curve does the tax shift?
• Why does it not shift the demand curve?
• What happens to quantity demanded?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. An indirect tax is paid by producers to the government, so it raises the cost of supplying each packet.
  2. Supply shifts left (vertically upward by the amount of the tax per unit).
  3. Demand does not shift because consumers' income, tastes and other determinants are unchanged.
  4. The higher price causes a contraction along the demand curve (Common mistake, p. 65); the size of the fall depends on PED (Chapter 7).
DiagramSupply and demand for cigarettes: S1 shifts to S2 (S + tax); equilibrium moves up the demand curve from P1, Q1 to P2, Q2.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Upward-sloping supply curve SL/HL

Fig 4.3, p. 58

  1. Label the vertical axis Price (P) and the horizontal axis Quantity supplied (Q) per time period.
  2. Draw a straight upward-sloping line and label it S.
  3. Mark P1, Q1 and a higher price P2 with larger Q2.
  4. Add dotted lines to both axes and an arrow along the curve.
Common errorsDrawing the curve from the origin every time (this implies unit elasticity, see Chapter 9); labelling the axis 'Supply' rather than 'Quantity'.

Simulation and data activities

Paper-plane factory (diminishing returns)

Set up a 'factory' with fixed capital: one desk, one stapler, a stack of A4. Add one worker per 60-second round and record the number of planes that pass a flight test. Students calculate total and marginal product and plot both in Google Sheets. They then assign a wage per worker and compute marginal cost per plane, showing that marginal cost rises. Discuss what would happen if a second desk were added (the long run).

Debrief
• When did marginal product start falling and why?
• How does rising marginal cost explain the upward slope of the supply curve?
• Why does this apply only in the short run?

Course Simulator – Supply & demand shifter

Show only the supply curve. Students receive CISTERN event cards and, in pairs, predict the direction of the shift before the teacher applies it on the simulator. After each event, students write the determinant and the direction in a table. Finish with two own-price changes to test whether they can recognise a movement rather than a shift.

Debrief
• Which determinants shift supply right and which left?
• Why does an indirect tax shift supply but not demand?

Our World in Data – crop yields · https://ourworldindata.org/crop-yields

Students choose one crop (wheat, rice or maize) and two countries, and chart yields per hectare over 50 years. They identify periods of sharp change and research the causes (Green Revolution technology, drought, conflict). Each pair writes a short explanation of how the change would shift the supply curve for that crop.

Debrief
• Which determinant of supply best explains long-run yield growth?
• Why do yields fall sharply in some years?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. At $10 per kg, Firm 1 supplies 200 kg and Firm 2 supplies 300 kg of organic grapes (Hoang Fig 4.4). What is market supply at $10?

SolutionMarket supply = 200 + 300 = 500 kg at $10 (horizontal summation).

Problem 2. (HL) A bakery with one oven adds workers: total output is 20, 45, 65, 80 and 90 loaves with 1 to 5 workers. Calculate marginal product and identify where diminishing returns begin. HL

SolutionMarginal products: 20, 25, 20, 15, 10. MP rises for the second worker, then falls from the third worker onwards, so diminishing marginal returns begin with the third worker.

Problem 3. (HL) Using the bakery data, each worker costs $50 per shift. Calculate the marginal cost per loaf for the 4th and 5th workers. HL

Solution4th worker: $50 ÷ 15 loaves = $3.33 per loaf. 5th worker: $50 ÷ 10 = $5.00 per loaf. Marginal cost rises, so the bakery needs a higher price to justify producing more.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A rise in demand increases supply and lowers price. Higher demand raises price and causes an expansion of quantity supplied, not a shift of supply (p. 61).
  • Any tax shifts the supply curve. Only indirect taxes shift supply; direct income taxes affect consumers' disposable income and therefore demand (p. 61).
  • A higher price increases supply. It increases quantity supplied (a movement along the curve) (p. 65).
  • A sales tax shifts the demand curve. It shifts supply and causes a movement along demand (p. 65).
  • Diminishing marginal returns means total output falls. Total output still rises, but by smaller amounts.
  • Diminishing marginal returns and diminishing marginal utility are the same idea. DMR concerns production; DMU concerns consumption (Top tip, p. 57).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 64: in-text figure references are off by one; the text cites Figs 4.13 and 4.14 for the movement and shift diagrams, which are printed as Figs 4.12 and 4.13.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define supply.

The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price in a given period, ceteris paribus.

2. Give two reasons for the law of supply.

Higher prices increase profit margins for existing firms and attract new firms into the market.

3. (HL) State the law of diminishing marginal returns.

As more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the extra output from each additional unit eventually falls.

4. (HL) Why does marginal cost rise in the short run?

Because of diminishing marginal returns, each extra unit needs more variable input, so it costs more.

5. What does CISTERN stand for?

Costs, Indirect taxes, Subsidies, Technology, Expectations, Related goods, Number of firms.

6. Distinguish joint supply from competitive supply.

Joint supply: goods produced together (beef and leather). Competitive supply: goods competing for the same resources (wheat and barley).

7. Wages in the garment industry rise. Effect on supply of shirts?

Supply shifts left because costs of production rise.

8. Government gives a subsidy to electric-car makers. Effect?

Supply of electric cars shifts right.

9. The price of smartphones rises. Movement or shift of smartphone supply?

A movement: an expansion along the supply curve.

10. Market supply at $5: Firm A 30, Firm B 50. Total?

80 units.

11. Why does an indirect tax not shift demand?

It changes producers' costs, not consumers' income or preferences; consumers respond to the higher price by moving along demand.

12. Producers expect prices to rise next month. Effect on current supply?

Current supply may shift left as firms hold back stock to sell later at the higher price.
