Key terms and definitions

Economic methodology The study of the methods economists use, including the processes, principles, models, theories and assumptions of economics.
Positive economics Statements about what is, was or will be that can be tested against evidence; they may be true or false.
Normative economics Statements about what ought to be, based on value judgements; they cannot be proved or refuted by evidence.
Logic Explaining economic events through a chain of reasoning in which each step follows from the last, rather than through emotion or belief.
Hypothesis A testable prediction about the relationship between variables, made before evidence is gathered; a null hypothesis is assumed true unless evidence rejects it in favour of the alternative.
Model A simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, used to explain and predict economic behaviour.
Theory A general explanation of economic behaviour that has been repeatedly supported by evidence and can be used to make predictions.
Ceteris paribus 'All other things being equal': the assumption that only the variable being studied changes, so that its effect can be isolated.
Empirical evidence Information gathered through observation, measurement or experiment that can be used to test hypotheses.
Refutation Showing that a hypothesis or theory is false because empirical evidence contradicts it.
Value judgement An opinion about what is right, wrong, good or bad, shaped by morals, ethics and culture.
Equity Fairness in the distribution of income, wealth or resources; a normative idea that does not necessarily mean equal shares.
Equality The state of everyone having the same shares, rights or opportunities.
Laissez-faire The belief that markets work best when government does not interfere in economic transactions.
Invisible hand Adam Smith's metaphor for how individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote the interests of society.
Marginal utility The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good.
Law of diminishing marginal utility As a person consumes more units of a good, the extra satisfaction from each additional unit eventually falls.
Say's law The classical idea that supply creates its own demand, so general overproduction cannot persist.
Marxism (labour theory of value) Marx's critique of capitalism, which holds that the value of a good depends on the labour used to make it and that capitalists exploit workers by keeping surplus value.
Keynesian economics The view that markets may not self-correct and that government should manage aggregate demand, for example by raising spending or cutting taxes in a recession.
Monetarism / new classical counter revolution Schools that emphasise control of the money supply and free markets, favouring low inflation, privatisation, deregulation and supply-side policies over demand management.
Behavioural economics A branch of economics that uses insights from psychology to explain why people often make decisions that are not fully rational.
Circular economy An economic model in which resources are kept in use through reuse, repair and recycling, as opposed to the linear 'take–make–waste' model.
Sustainability Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Change

Japan's zero interest rate policy as refutation

The Bank of Japan cut its policy rate to around 0% in 1999 and kept it at or below zero for more than 20 years (Hoang p. 34). Standard theory predicts that very low interest rates raise borrowing, investment and spending. Yet Japan's real GDP growth averaged under 1% a year and inflation stayed close to zero for much of the period.

Ask the class
• What hypothesis does the Japanese evidence test?
• Does the evidence refute the theory?
• Why is ceteris paribus crucial to the answer?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. State the hypothesis: lower interest rates increase investment and aggregate demand.
  2. Evidence: rates near zero for two decades, yet growth and inflation stayed weak, which appears to contradict the hypothesis.
  3. Ceteris paribus did not hold: an ageing population, high household saving, bank weakness and deflationary expectations all changed at the same time.
  4. Conclusion: the evidence challenges the theory, but economists disagree on whether it refutes it or shows that other factors outweighed it; this is why empirical testing in economics is difficult.

Scenario 2 · Intervention

Brexit: positive evidence and normative choices

In 2016 the UK voted 52% to 48% to leave the EU and left on 31 January 2020 (Hoang p. 35). Economists published forecasts of trade and GDP effects, while politicians argued about sovereignty, immigration and fairness.

Ask the class
• Give one positive and one normative statement about Brexit.
• Why might two economists with the same data recommend different policies?
• What role do value judgements play in referendums?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Positive: 'UK goods exports to the EU fell after new customs checks began' can be tested with trade data.
  2. Normative: 'The UK should rejoin the single market' depends on how one weighs trade against sovereignty.
  3. Economists may agree on the estimated costs yet disagree on the policy because they value outcomes differently.
  4. A referendum aggregates value judgements; positive economics can inform but not settle it.

Scenario 3 · Equity

Equity or equality? University places in Brazil

Brazil's 2012 quota law reserves 50% of places at federal universities for students from public schools, with a share of those for Black, mixed-race and Indigenous students in proportion to each state's population. Critics argue that places should go to the highest exam scores; supporters argue that exam scores reflect unequal schooling.

Ask the class
• Is the quota policy about equity or equality?
• Which statements in the debate are positive and which normative?
• What evidence could test its effects?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Equality would give every applicant the same treatment (the same cut-off score).
  2. Equity aims at fairness, accepting unequal treatment to offset unequal starting points, which is the rationale of the quotas.
  3. 'Quotas raised enrolment of low-income students' is positive and testable; 'Quotas are unfair to high scorers' is normative.
  4. Evidence on graduation rates and later earnings of quota students can inform, but not decide, the value judgement.
DiagramNo syllabus diagram required; a simple bar chart of enrolment shares before and after 2012 could be built in Google Sheets.
Scenario 4 · Change and intervention

From Keynes to COVID-19: the return of demand management

Between March and June 2020, US unemployment rose by over 40 million people and Congress injected more than $2 trillion through the CARES Act (Hoang p. 42). Forty years earlier, the USA and UK had moved away from demand management towards monetarism and supply-side reform.

Ask the class
• Which school of thought does the 2020 stimulus reflect?
• How would a new classical economist have responded?
• Why do ideas in economics change over time?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Keynesian thinking holds that markets may not self-correct quickly, so government should boost aggregate demand in a slump.
  2. The CARES Act raised government spending and transfers, a Keynesian injection into the circular flow.
  3. A new classical economist would worry about government debt, inflation and crowding out, and would prefer letting markets adjust and supply-side measures.
  4. Paradigms shift when events (the Great Depression, 1970s stagflation, the 2008 crisis, COVID-19) expose the limits of the dominant model.

Simulation and data activities

FRED hypothesis-testing lab · https://fred.stlouisfed.org

Groups choose a hypothesis from a list (e.g. 'Lower interest rates raise investment'; 'Higher unemployment lowers inflation'). They find two series in FRED, set a 20-year range and plot them on one chart. Each group writes a null hypothesis, describes the relationship seen, and lists at least three other variables that changed. Groups present in 90 seconds each.

Debrief
• Did the data support or challenge your hypothesis?
• Why can economists rarely run controlled experiments?
• What would you need to isolate cause and effect?

Diminishing marginal utility taste test

Volunteers consume up to six small units of a snack or drink and rate the satisfaction of each on a 1–10 scale. The class records the ratings in Google Sheets and charts total and marginal utility. Compare individual curves and discuss exceptions (collectors, addictive goods). Link to the free-refill and diamond–water examples on p. 39.

Debrief
• When did marginal utility start falling?
• Why is total utility still rising while marginal utility falls?
• How does this help explain the downward-sloping demand curve?

CORE Econ 'The Economy', Unit 1 · https://www.core-econ.org

Students read the CORE section on the 'hockey-stick' of long-run GDP growth and the capitalist revolution. In pairs they link each historical phase to a school of thought from Hoang pp. 37–44. They then annotate the hockey-stick chart with Smith, Marx, Keynes and Friedman at the right points in time.

Debrief
• Which events triggered changes in economic thinking?
• Which school would you expect to dominate in the next 20 years and why?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A positive statement must be true. It only needs to be testable; it can be shown to be false (Top tip, p. 30).
  • Normative statements are unscientific and useless. They are essential for policy, because choosing between outcomes always involves value judgements.
  • Equity and equality mean the same thing. Equity is fairness; equality is sameness (p. 37).
  • Ceteris paribus means economists ignore the real world. It is a tool to isolate one relationship, which is then tested against real data.
  • A theory that has been supported once is proven forever. Theories are always open to refutation by new evidence (Japan example, p. 34).
  • Adam Smith argued for no government at all. He accepted roles for government such as defence, justice and some public works.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define positive economics.

Statements about what is that can be tested against evidence.

2. Is 'A 5p plastic bag charge will cut bag use by 80%' positive or normative?

Positive: it can be tested with data, even if it turns out to be wrong.

3. Why do economists use ceteris paribus?

To isolate the effect of one variable on another by holding all other factors constant.

4. What is the difference between a hypothesis and a theory?

A hypothesis is an untested prediction; a theory is a general explanation repeatedly supported by evidence.

5. Give an example of refutation from Hoang.

Japan's near-zero interest rates for 20+ years did not produce strong growth, challenging the theory that low rates stimulate the economy.

6. Distinguish equity from equality.

Equity is fairness in distribution; equality is identical shares or treatment.

7. What is a value judgement?

An opinion about what is good or bad, shaped by morals, ethics and culture, which cannot be tested.

8. What did Smith mean by the invisible hand?

Self-interested individuals trading in free markets unintentionally benefit society.

9. State the law of diminishing marginal utility.

Each extra unit consumed eventually gives less additional satisfaction than the one before.

10. What is Say's law?

Supply creates its own demand.

11. What policy did Keynes recommend in a depression?

Higher government spending and/or lower taxes to raise aggregate demand.

12. Name two policies associated with the NCCR.

Privatisation and deregulation (also supply-side tax cuts and control of the money supply).

13. What is behavioural economics?

The use of psychology to explain why people often do not behave fully rationally.

14. How does a circular economy differ from a linear one?

It keeps resources in use through reuse and recycling rather than take–make–waste.
