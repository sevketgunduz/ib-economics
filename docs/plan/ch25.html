Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 25

Demand management: fiscal policy

Syllabus 3.6 Demand management – fiscal policyTextbook pp. 380–400Sessions 7 HL · 4 SLConcepts intervention, choice, equity, sustainability, economic well-being

How do governments manage their economy and how effective are their policies? Can a government 'spend its way out of a recession' – and at what cost to its debt, to interest rates and to the private sector?

Common SL/HL: sources of government revenue, types of expenditure, goals of fiscal policy, the government budget, expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy on AD/AS diagrams for both Keynesian and monetarist/new classical views, and constraints and strengths (political pressure, time lags, sustainable debt, targeting sectors, effectiveness in deep recession). HL only: the Keynesian multiplier and its calculations (session 3), crowding out and its diagram (session 5), and automatic stabilizers (session 6).

Key terms and definitions

Fiscal policy The use of government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand and achieve macroeconomic objectives.
Discretionary fiscal policy Deliberate changes in government spending or taxation decided by the government, as opposed to automatic changes.
Direct tax A tax on income, profits or wealth paid directly to the government, e.g. income tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax.
Indirect tax A tax on spending on goods and services, e.g. VAT/GST, excise duties, customs duties, carbon tax.
State-owned enterprise A business owned and run by the government whose sales of goods and services generate revenue for the state.
Privatization The sale or transfer of government-owned assets or enterprises to the private sector, which raises one-off revenue.
Current expenditure Government spending on goods and services consumed within the year, such as public sector wages, supplies, subsidies and debt interest.
Capital expenditure Government spending on long-lived assets such as roads, schools and hospitals, which increases productive capacity.
Transfer payment Government payments such as pensions and unemployment benefits for which no good or service is produced in return; they redistribute income but are not counted in GDP.
Government budget A plan of government revenue and expenditure over a period, usually a year; it may be in deficit (spending > revenue), balance or surplus.
Expansionary fiscal policy Increasing government spending and/or cutting taxes to raise AD and close a deflationary (recessionary) gap.
Contractionary fiscal policy Reducing government spending and/or raising taxes to reduce AD and close an inflationary gap.
Keynesian multiplier HLThe ratio of the final change in real GDP to the initial change in an injection (I, G or X): k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM).
Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) HLThe proportion of an increase in income spent on domestic consumption: ΔC ÷ ΔY.
Marginal propensity to withdraw (MPW) HLThe proportion of an increase in income that leaks from the circular flow through saving, taxes and imports: MPS + MPT + MPM = 1 − MPC.
Negative multiplier HLThe process by which a fall in an injection or rise in a leakage causes a larger final fall in real GDP.
Time lags Delays in recognising a problem (recognition lag), passing and implementing policy (administrative lag) and the policy taking effect (impact lag).
Sustainable debt A level of government debt that can be serviced and repaid without large tax rises, spending cuts or default; limits how much expansionary policy is possible.
Crowding out HLThe reduction in private investment that occurs when government borrowing to finance a deficit raises the demand for loanable funds and hence interest rates.
Loanable funds market HLThe market in which savings (supply) meet demand for borrowing from firms, households and government; its price is the interest rate.
Automatic stabilizers HLFeatures of the tax and benefit system, such as progressive taxes and unemployment benefits, that automatically reduce fluctuations in economic activity without new government decisions.
Austerity A policy of cutting government spending and/or raising taxes to reduce a budget deficit and public debt.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Intervention

Germany's 2020 stimulus

In June 2020 Germany announced a €130bn stimulus package (about 4% of GDP), including a temporary cut in VAT from 19% to 16%, a €300 payment per child and support for electric vehicles and hydrogen technology. German GDP had fallen by about 10% in Q2 2020 and unemployment was rising. Germany entered the crisis with a budget surplus and debt below 60% of GDP.

Ask the class
• Is this expansionary or contractionary?
• Which diagram fits, Keynesian or monetarist?
• Why could Germany afford it?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Tax cuts and higher spending are expansionary fiscal policy, aiming to shift AD right and close a deflationary gap.
  2. With large spare capacity, the economy was on the flat/upward section of the Keynesian AS, so output could rise with little inflation (Fig 25.8).
  3. (HL) The multiplier increases the final effect, although Germany's high MPM (it is very open to trade) reduces its size.
  4. Germany's low debt meant little risk to debt sustainability or crowding out; green investment also has supply-side effects (LRAS shifts right).
DiagramAD/AS with Keynesian AS: AD1 shifts right to AD2 in the horizontal/upward-sloping section; real GDP rises, price level barely changes.
Scenario 2 · Sustainability

Greece: austerity after 2010

By 2010 Greece's budget deficit was over 15% of GDP and debt was about 146% of GDP. In return for EU/IMF loans, the government cut public wages and pensions and raised taxes. Real GDP fell by about 25% between 2008 and 2013 and unemployment rose above 27%.

Ask the class
• Why did Greece adopt contractionary policy in a recession?
• What does the negative multiplier predict?
• Was austerity effective?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. With debt seen as unsustainable, lenders required deficit reduction; Greece could not use its own monetary policy inside the euro.
  2. Cutting G and raising T shifts AD left; (HL) the negative multiplier magnifies the fall in GDP.
  3. Falling GDP reduced tax revenue and raised benefit spending (automatic stabilizers), so the debt-to-GDP ratio rose even as the deficit was cut.
  4. Evaluation: austerity eventually restored a primary surplus but at very high social cost; the case shows that sustainable debt can constrain fiscal policy severely (p. 393).
DiagramAD/AS: AD1 shifts left to AD2, widening a deflationary gap; real GDP falls from Y1 to Y2.
Scenario 3 · Interdependence

Ireland versus the USA: multipliers in open economies (HL)

Ireland's imports are worth more than 80% of its GDP; US imports are about 15% of GDP. Suppose each government spends an extra $10bn on infrastructure. In Ireland MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.3, MPM = 0.4; in the USA MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.25, MPM = 0.15.

Ask the class
• Calculate each multiplier.
• Calculate the change in GDP in each country.
• Why is the difference so large?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Ireland: k = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.3 + 0.4) = 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25.
  2. USA: k = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.25 + 0.15) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2.
  3. ΔGDP: Ireland $12.5bn; USA $20bn.
  4. Much of Irish spending leaks abroad on imports, so later rounds of spending create income in other countries. Fiscal policy is less powerful in small open economies.
DiagramAD/AS showing AD1 → AD2 (initial injection) → AD3 (multiplier effect), with a smaller AD2→AD3 gap for Ireland.
Scenario 4 · Choice

UK mini-budget, September 2022 (HL)

The UK government announced £45bn of unfunded tax cuts, to be financed by borrowing, at a time of 10% inflation. Yields on 10-year government bonds (gilts) jumped from about 3.5% to 4.5% within days. Mortgage lenders withdrew hundreds of products and fixed mortgage rates rose above 6%. The Bank of England intervened in the bond market and most of the measures were reversed within weeks.

Ask the class
• Why did interest rates rise?
• How is this linked to crowding out?
• What does it show about fiscal policy constraints?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Extra government borrowing increases demand for loanable funds; investors also demanded a higher return because of doubts about debt sustainability.
  2. The loanable funds demand curve shifts right, raising the interest rate from r1 to r2 (Fig 25.12).
  3. Higher mortgage and business borrowing costs reduce private consumption and investment – financial crowding out – offsetting the boost from tax cuts.
  4. Evaluation: expansionary policy at full employment with high inflation is especially likely to cause crowding out; the episode also shows political pressure and credibility as constraints.
DiagramLoanable funds market: vertical or upward-sloping supply of loanable funds; demand shifts D1 to D2; interest rate rises r1 to r2; private borrowing falls.
Scenario 5 · Equity

France's fuel tax protests

In late 2018 France planned higher fuel taxes as part of its climate policy. The 'Yellow Vest' protests spread across the country, with rural and lower-income households arguing they depended on cars. The government suspended the tax rise, having earlier dropped a 75% tax on incomes above €1m in 2012 (p. 386).

Ask the class
• What goal was the fuel tax meant to serve?
• Why did it face political pressure?
• What does this show about fiscal policy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The indirect tax aimed to reduce emissions and raise revenue (sustainability and budget goals).
  2. Fuel taxes are regressive: low-income and rural households spend a larger share of income on fuel.
  3. Visible losers organised protests; the government reversed the policy – political pressure as a constraint (p. 392).
  4. Evaluation: pairing environmental taxes with compensation (rebates, public transport) may make them politically sustainable.
DiagramNo macro diagram required; optionally AD/AS with a small leftward AD shift, or a micro indirect-tax diagram.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Keynesian multiplier

Students set an injection of $10bn and adjust MPS, MPT and MPM with sliders, recording k and ΔGDP in a table. They then set values to match two real economies (a small open economy with high MPM and a large closed one). They show the round-by-round spending chart and count how many rounds it takes to reach 90% of the final effect. Finally they try a negative injection to show the negative multiplier.

Debrief
• Which leakage had the biggest effect on the multiplier?
• Why does a small open economy have a small multiplier?
• How does the time taken for the rounds relate to impact time lags?

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

In Keynesian mode, students apply the same $20bn AD increase at three starting points (deep recession, near Yf, at Yf) and record ΔY and ΔPL. They switch to LRAS (new classical) mode and repeat, noting the long-run adjustment. Groups present one slide contrasting the two schools.

Debrief
• Why does the same fiscal stimulus have different effects?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Worked example (p. 391): the government injects $400m. MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.3. Calculate the multiplier and the final change in real GDP. HL

Solutionk = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.2 + 0.3) = 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.67. ΔY = $400m × (1 ÷ 0.6) = $666.67m. Real GDP rises by $666.67m in total, of which $266.67m comes from further rounds of spending.

Problem 2. Q25.2b (p. 391): the government spends $9.5bn on infrastructure; MPC = 0.75. Calculate the change in real national output. HL

Solutionk = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4. ΔY = $9.5bn × 4 = $38bn.

Problem 3. Q25.2c (p. 391): there is a $15bn recessionary gap and MPC = 0.8. How much extra government spending is needed? HL

Solutionk = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. ΔG = gap ÷ k = $15bn ÷ 5 = $3bn.

Problem 4. Q25.2d (p. 391): the government raises tax revenue by $20bn; MPC = 0.75. Calculate the impact on real national output. HL

SolutionTreating the tax rise as a leakage with k = 4, the expected IB-style answer is ΔY = −$20bn × 4 = −$80bn. Note for stronger students: households cut spending by only MPC × $20bn = $15bn in the first round, so a more precise answer is −$15bn × 4 = −$60bn. Accept either with clear working.

Problem 5. Exports rise by $6bn. MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.25, MPM = 0.1. Calculate the change in GDP. HL

SolutionMPW = 0.5; k = 2; ΔY = $12bn.

Problem 6. Real GDP must rise by $24bn to reach Yf. MPS = 0.2, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.2. What increase in investment is required? HL

Solutionk = 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.67. ΔI = $24bn ÷ 1.67 = $14.4bn.

Problem 7. Q25.3 (p. 398): Tax A: $4,500 → $900, $10,000 → $1,800, $20,000 → $2,800. Tax B: $10,000 → $1,500, $20,000 → $3,000, $30,000 → $4,500. Tax C: $8,000 → $800, $20,000 → $3,000, $45,000 → $11,250. Calculate tax as a % of income and classify each system. HL

SolutionA: 20%, 18%, 14% – regressive. B: 15%, 15%, 15% – proportional. C: 10%, 15%, 25% – progressive. Only C acts as a strong automatic stabilizer.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Budget deficits are always bad – in a deep recession a deficit may be the right policy to support AD (p. 393).
  • Transfer payments are part of GDP – they are part of government spending but not of G in AD or GDP, since nothing is produced in return (p. 384).
  • Fiscal policy only affects AD – tax incentives and spending on education, health and infrastructure also shift LRAS (p. 398).
  • (HL) The multiplier always increases real GDP – at full employment it mainly raises the price level (p. 391).
  • (HL) A larger MPM increases the multiplier – imports are a leakage, so a higher MPM reduces it.
  • (HL) Crowding out means government spending crowds out private goods directly – IB crowding out is financial: higher interest rates reduce private investment.
  • Automatic stabilizers are decisions taken by government in a recession – they work without new decisions, through the existing tax and benefit system.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy works immediately – recognition, administrative and impact lags can mean it takes effect after the economy has already recovered.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 391: after correctly finding a final change of $666.67m, the book says real GDP rises 'by $266.67m to $666.67m', which is confusing. The total rise in real GDP is $666.67m; $266.67m is the additional amount generated beyond the initial $400m injection. Also, using the rounded k = 1.67 gives $668m; use 1 ÷ 0.6 to obtain $666.67m.
  • p. 394 (Fig 25.12): the crowding-out diagram labels its curves Sm, Dm1 and Dm2 (money-market labels) although the axis is loanable funds, and with a vertical supply the total quantity of funds cannot change even though Q2 is shown below Q1. Teach it as the demand for and supply of loanable funds, where private borrowing (not total borrowing) falls; an upward-sloping supply shows partial crowding out.
  • p. 391 (Q25.2d): the question applies the multiplier to a tax change. Strictly, a tax change has a smaller effect than an equal change in G (only MPC × ΔT is spent in the first round); see the calculation note.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define fiscal policy.

The use of government spending and taxation to influence AD and achieve macroeconomic objectives.

2. Give two sources of government revenue other than taxes.

Sales of goods and services by state-owned enterprises; sale of government assets (privatization).

3. Distinguish current and capital expenditure.

Current: spending used up within the year, e.g. wages; capital: spending on long-lived assets, e.g. roads, which raises productive capacity.

4. What does BUDGIE stand for?

Business cycle stability, Unemployment, Distribution of income, Growth, Inflation, External balance.

5. How does the monetarist view of expansionary fiscal policy differ from the Keynesian?

Monetarists say AD increases only raise the price level in the long run (vertical LRAS); Keynesians say output rises when there is spare capacity.

6. (HL) State the two multiplier formulae.

k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM).

7. (HL) MPC = 0.6. By how much does GDP rise after a $5bn rise in G?

k = 2.5, so GDP rises by $12.5bn.

8. Name the three time lags of fiscal policy.

Recognition lag, administrative (implementation) lag and impact lag.

9. Why can high government debt limit fiscal policy?

Interest costs rise and lenders may demand higher rates, so further borrowing becomes costly or impossible, forcing spending cuts.

10. (HL) What is crowding out?

Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and interest rates, which reduces private investment.

11. (HL) How does a progressive tax act as an automatic stabilizer?

In a boom, tax revenue rises faster than income, withdrawing spending; in a recession it falls faster, cushioning disposable income.

12. Give two strengths of fiscal policy.

It can target specific sectors or regions, and government spending works in a deep recession when monetary policy is ineffective.

13. Why might expansionary fiscal policy worsen the current account?

Higher incomes raise spending on imports.

14. Give one supply-side effect of fiscal policy.

Spending on education or infrastructure raises productivity and shifts LRAS right.
