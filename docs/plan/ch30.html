Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 30

Economic integration

Syllabus 4.4 Economic integrationTextbook pp. 469–484Sessions 3 HL · 3 SLConcepts interdependence, economic well-being, change, equity, intervention

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? How far should countries give up economic independence in exchange for access to bigger markets?

Common SL/HL: preferential trade agreements (bilateral, regional, multilateral), trading blocs (free trade areas, customs unions, common markets), the advantages and disadvantages of trading blocs, monetary union, and the WTO (objectives, functions and factors affecting its influence). HL only: trade creation and trade diversion (pp. 474–476) and the advantages and disadvantages of monetary union (pp. 477–479). These are marked (HL) inside common sessions 2 and 3. No diagrams or calculations are required.

Key terms and definitions

Economic integration The process by which countries become more interdependent by reducing barriers to trade and, at higher stages, harmonizing policies and currencies.
Preferential trade agreement (PTA) A treaty between two or more countries giving each other better access to their markets (e.g. lower tariffs) than they give to non-members.
Bilateral trade agreement A preferential trade agreement between two countries to reduce or remove trade barriers between them.
Regional trade agreement A reciprocal preferential agreement between two or more countries, usually in the same geographical region.
Multilateral trade agreement A legally binding agreement between many countries, usually negotiated through the WTO, to reduce trade barriers on a non-discriminatory basis.
Trading bloc A group of countries that reduce or remove trade barriers between themselves while keeping barriers against non-members.
Free trade area (FTA) A trading bloc in which members remove trade barriers between themselves but each keeps its own barriers against non-members.
Customs union A free trade area in which members also adopt a common external tariff against non-members.
Common external tariff (CET) The same tariff applied by all members of a customs union to imports from non-members.
Common market A customs union with free movement of factors of production (labour and capital) between member countries.
Monetary union A common market whose members share a common monetary policy set by a single central bank and, in a full monetary union, a single currency.
Trade creation HLWhen joining a trading bloc shifts trade from higher-cost producers (at home or outside the bloc) to lower-cost producers inside the bloc, because internal barriers are removed; it raises efficiency.
Trade diversion HLWhen joining a trading bloc shifts trade from lower-cost producers outside the bloc to higher-cost producers inside it, because of the common external tariff; it reduces efficiency.
Loss of sovereignty A reduction in a country's power to make its own economic decisions, such as setting tariffs, regulations or (in a monetary union) interest rates.
Asymmetric shock HLAn economic shock that affects members of a monetary union differently, so a single interest rate cannot suit all of them.
Changeover costs HLThe one-off costs of adopting a new common currency, such as reprinting prices, changing IT systems and replacing cash.
World Trade Organization (WTO) The international organization, set up in 1995 to replace GATT, that promotes trade liberalization, runs multilateral negotiations and settles trade disputes between members.
Most favoured nation (MFN) principle The WTO rule that a trade concession given to one member must be given to all members.
Non-discrimination The WTO principle that members must not treat trading partners' goods, services or nationals differently; bilateral agreements and regional blocs are the main exceptions.
Dispute settlement The WTO process by which members bring complaints about trade barriers, with panels ruling on whether rules have been broken and allowing authorized retaliation.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Efficiency and interdependence – regional integration is not the same as free trade.

Trade creation versus trade diversion: cars into a new customs-union member (HL)

Country H imports cars. A car costs $10,000 to produce in Germany, $9,000 in Japan and $11,000 at home. Before integration, H charges a 20% tariff on all imported cars. H then joins a customs union with Germany, which removes tariffs on German cars but keeps a 20% common external tariff on Japanese cars.

Ask the class
• Where does H buy its cars before and after joining?
• Is this trade creation or trade diversion?
• How would the answer change if H's own car industry had been the pre-union supplier?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Before: Japanese car = $9,000 + 20% = $10,800; German car = $12,000; home car = $11,000. H imports from Japan at $10,800 and the government collects $1,800 per car.
  2. After: German car = $10,000 (no tariff); Japanese car still $10,800. H switches to Germany. Consumers save $800 per car, but the government loses $1,800 of tariff revenue per car.
  3. The world now uses $10,000 of resources to make a car that Japan could make for $9,000. This is trade diversion: a lower-cost outside producer is replaced by a higher-cost inside producer. The net loss is $1,000 per car (–$1,800 + $800), before counting extra cars bought at the lower price.
  4. Alternative: suppose instead Japan's cost were also $10,000. Before the union both imports cost $12,000 with the tariff, so H buys from its own firms at $11,000. After joining, German cars cost $10,000, so supply switches from the high-cost home producer to the lower-cost partner. That is trade creation, which improves efficiency.
  5. Judgement: a customs union improves welfare only if trade creation outweighs trade diversion (p. 476); the higher the CET, the greater the risk of diversion.
DiagramNo IB diagram required; use a three-column cost table (home, partner, rest of world) before and after the union, with arrows showing the change in supplier.
Scenario 2 · Economic well-being – integration as a development strategy.

The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

AfCFTA, which began trading in 2021, aims to cut tariffs on most goods between more than 50 African countries. Intra-African trade is small compared with intra-EU trade, partly because of high tariffs, poor transport links and complex customs rules. Kenya exports tea and manufactured goods; Nigeria has a large but protected market.

Ask the class
• What type of trading bloc is AfCFTA?
• Explain two potential advantages for member countries.
• Why might the benefits be smaller than expected?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. It is a free trade area: internal tariffs are removed, but each member keeps its own external tariffs (it is not a customs union).
  2. Advantages: access to a much larger market lets firms gain economies of scale (e.g. Kenyan manufacturers selling across East and West Africa); more competition lowers prices; a united bloc may have more bargaining power with the EU or China.
  3. Limits: non-tariff barriers (border delays, standards), poor infrastructure and rules of origin may keep costs high; countries that produce similar primary products may have little to trade with each other; less competitive firms in protected markets may close, causing structural unemployment.
  4. Judgement: gains depend on complementary supply-side investment (roads, ports, digital customs); the FTA is a necessary but not sufficient condition for more trade.
DiagramOptional: free-trade imports diagram (Ch27) for a member removing its tariff on imports from other members, showing the price falling and imports rising.
Scenario 3 · Interdependence and sovereignty – the cost of giving up monetary independence.

Greece and Germany in the eurozone (HL)

In the late 2000s, Greece had high public debt and falling competitiveness, while Germany had a large current account surplus. After the 2008 global financial crisis, Greek unemployment rose above 25%, while German unemployment fell. Both used the euro and faced the same ECB interest rate. Greece could not devalue its currency (pp. 478–479).

Ask the class
• Explain why a single monetary policy caused problems for Greece.
• What adjustment options did Greece have without its own currency?
• Evaluate whether the benefits of the euro outweigh the costs for a country like Greece.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The ECB sets one interest rate for the whole eurozone, based on average conditions. Greece needed much lower rates and a weaker currency; Germany did not. This is an asymmetric shock.
  2. Without its own exchange rate, Greece could not devalue to make exports cheaper. It had to regain competitiveness through 'internal devaluation' – cutting wages and prices – which raised unemployment.
  3. Fiscal rules and bailout conditions limited expansionary fiscal policy too, so there was a loss of sovereignty over both main demand-side policies.
  4. Benefits of the euro for Greece: no transaction costs or exchange-rate risk with its main trading partners, lower borrowing costs before the crisis, price transparency and tourism.
  5. Judgement: monetary union works best when members have similar economic cycles, flexible labour markets and fiscal transfers. Greece lacked these, so the costs were very high in the crisis years, but it chose to stay in the euro, which suggests it valued the long-run benefits.
DiagramOptional (HL): AD/AS for Greece – AD falls, and without monetary or exchange-rate policy the economy remains at a low real GDP until wages and prices fall (SRAS shifts right slowly).
Scenario 4 · Equity – unequal bargaining power among WTO members.

The WTO's weakened dispute settlement system

Since December 2019, the WTO Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals because the USA blocked new appointments. Countries can still bring disputes, but a losing side can 'appeal into the void'. Meanwhile, agreement on agriculture and services in the Doha Round, launched in 2001, has not been reached (p. 483).

Ask the class
• Explain two functions of the WTO.
• Why is it difficult to reach agreement on primary products and services?
• How does unequal bargaining power affect the WTO's influence?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Functions: running negotiations to reduce barriers; settling disputes; monitoring trade policies; helping developing countries (Aid for Trade, p. 482).
  2. Agriculture is politically sensitive in high-income countries that subsidize farmers, and services involve regulation (banking, health, education) that governments want to control; all 160+ members must agree, which makes a deal very hard.
  3. Large economies have more negotiating resources and market power; ELDCs may lack the lawyers and data to use dispute settlement and may find it hard to resist pressure.
  4. The Appellate Body crisis shows that the WTO's influence depends on members, especially the largest, being willing to accept its rules.
DiagramNo diagram required; a flow chart of dispute settlement (consultation → panel → appeal → compliance or authorized retaliation) drawn in draw.io.

Simulation and data activities

Mini-Doha Round negotiation (classroom role play)

Six delegations (USA, EU, China, India, Brazil, an ELDC group) receive a one-page brief with their export interests, sensitive sectors and a 'red line'. They have 15 minutes to agree a package on farm subsidies, industrial tariffs and services, and all delegations must agree (consensus, as in the WTO). The teacher acts as Director-General and may offer an 'Aid for Trade' sweetener. Record whether a deal is reached and who conceded most.

Debrief
• Why was consensus so hard to reach?
• Which delegation had the most bargaining power and why?
• Why are agriculture and services harder to negotiate than tariffs on manufactures?
• Would a regional bloc have made agreement easier for some delegations?

Google Sheets trade creation/diversion model

The teacher shares a sheet with production costs for a good in the home country, a partner country and the rest of the world, plus a tariff rate cell and a 'customs union on/off' switch. Students change costs and the tariff and record which supplier is chosen, the consumer price, tariff revenue and the resource cost per unit. HL students classify each case as trade creation or diversion and calculate the net welfare change per unit; SL students identify the winners and losers.

Debrief
• Under what cost conditions does joining the union cause trade diversion?
• How does a higher common external tariff affect the risk of diversion?
• Why can consumers gain even in a trade-diversion case?

IMF DataMapper / Trading Economics – eurozone divergence · https://www.imf.org/external/datamapper

HL students download unemployment, inflation and real GDP growth for Germany, Greece, Spain and Ireland from 2005 to the latest year and chart them against the ECB main refinancing rate (from Trading Economics or the ECB website). They mark the 2008 crisis and the 2010–2015 debt crisis and explain what happened in each country when the ECB set a single rate.

Debrief
• Which countries needed a different interest rate from the one the ECB set?
• What adjustment tools did those countries lack?
• Has divergence decreased since 2015?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • All free trade areas have a common external tariff – only customs unions (and higher stages) do; FTA members set their own external barriers (p. 473).
  • The EU and the eurozone are the same – the EU has 27 members, but several (e.g. Denmark, Sweden, Hungary, Poland) do not use the euro.
  • Trade creation means any increase in trade within a bloc – it specifically means a switch to lower-cost suppliers; a switch to higher-cost members is trade diversion (HL).
  • Trading blocs are always a step towards global free trade – they discriminate against non-members and may slow multilateral (WTO) negotiations.
  • Loss of sovereignty only happens in a monetary union – even an FTA or customs union constrains a country's tariff and regulatory choices.
  • The WTO forces countries to trade freely – it works by consensus and depends on members accepting rulings; it has no power to impose policies directly.
  • Monetary union always lowers inflation for all members – a single interest rate may be too low for fast-growing members, fuelling inflation or asset bubbles (HL).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 474: CAFTA-DR is described as an agreement between Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua that gives them bargaining power with the USA. In fact CAFTA-DR also includes the Dominican Republic and the USA itself. The example shows small economies negotiating a deal with the USA together, not a bloc negotiating against the USA from outside.
  • p. 479: the WTO is said to have been set up in 1995 by 123 member countries. 123 countries took part in the Uruguay Round that created the WTO, but the WTO started on 1 January 1995 with about 76 members, and others joined later that year.
  • p. 475: the text links trade diversion to higher unemployment 'due to the increased intensity of competition' inside the bloc. This mixes two ideas: more competition from partners is a result of trade creation; trade diversion is about switching to higher-cost suppliers. Teach the two separately.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. What is a preferential trade agreement?

A treaty giving members better market access than non-members get, e.g. lower tariffs.

2. Give an example of a bilateral trade agreement.

China–Hong Kong CEPA (2004) (p. 470), or the UK–Australia FTA.

3. What distinguishes a customs union from a free trade area?

A customs union has a common external tariff; FTA members set their own external tariffs.

4. What does a common market add to a customs union?

Free movement of factors of production, such as labour and capital.

5. Name the four stages of integration in order.

Free trade area, customs union, common market, monetary union.

6. Give two advantages of joining a trading bloc.

Any two of: access to larger markets and economies of scale, more employment opportunities, stronger bargaining power, greater political stability and cooperation, (HL) trade creation.

7. (HL) Define trade diversion.

A switch from lower-cost producers outside a bloc to higher-cost producers inside it, because of the common external tariff.

8. (HL) When does a customs union improve welfare overall?

When trade creation outweighs trade diversion.

9. (HL) Give two disadvantages of monetary union.

Any two of: loss of independent monetary policy, no exchange-rate adjustment, asymmetric shocks, changeover costs, fiscal constraints.

10. (HL) Give two advantages of monetary union.

Any two of: no transaction costs, no exchange-rate risk within the union, price transparency, lower interest rates/credibility, more trade and FDI.

11. What is the MFN principle?

A trade concession given to one WTO member must be extended to all members.

12. State two functions of the WTO.

Any two of: running trade negotiations, settling disputes, monitoring trade policies, supporting developing countries (Aid for Trade).

13. Give two factors that limit the WTO's influence.

Difficulty reaching agreement on services and primary products, and the unequal bargaining power of members.
