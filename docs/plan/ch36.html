Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 36

Economic growth and/or economic development strategies

Syllabus 4.10 Economic growth and/or economic development strategiesTextbook pp. 590–635Sessions 7 HL · 5 SLConcepts intervention, economic well-being, equity, sustainability, interdependence, change

Why is economic development uneven – and which strategies, by governments, markets, firms, NGOs and international institutions, actually help countries to grow and develop?

All IB content in 4.10 is common SL/HL. SL classes take sessions 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7: session 4 gives an overview of aid, multilateral assistance and institutional change, and session 7 combines the strengths and limitations of strategies with progress towards selected SDGs in two or more countries. Sessions 5 and 6 are HL-only extensions: institutional change in depth (microfinance, mobile banking, women's empowerment, corruption, property and land rights) and an evaluation workshop on intervention versus market-oriented approaches. Students at both levels are expected to draw on diagrams from other units (AD–AS, PPC, market failure, poverty cycle) (p. 591).

Key terms and definitions

Import substitution An inward-looking strategy in which a country protects domestic industries with tariffs, quotas and subsidies so that domestically produced goods replace imports.
Export promotion An outward-looking strategy that encourages firms to produce for world markets, using export subsidies, competitive exchange rates and investment incentives, to achieve export-led growth.
Economic integration The process by which countries reduce trade barriers and coordinate policies, becoming more interdependent, through agreements such as FTAs, customs unions and common markets.
Diversification Broadening the range of goods and services a country produces and exports, reducing dependence on a few (often primary) products.
Social enterprise An organization that trades goods or services primarily to achieve social or environmental objectives, reinvesting surpluses rather than maximizing profit for owners.
Trade liberalization The reduction or removal of trade barriers such as tariffs and quotas to increase competition and efficiency.
Privatization The transfer of ownership of state-owned assets or firms to the private sector.
Deregulation The reduction or removal of government rules in an industry to encourage competition and new entry.
Transfer payments Payments from the government to individuals for which no good or service is provided in return, such as pensions, unemployment benefits and child allowances.
Minimum wage The lowest wage per hour that employers may legally pay workers.
Merit goods Goods and services such as education and healthcare that generate positive externalities (MSB > MPB) and are under-consumed if left to the market.
Foreign direct investment (FDI) Long-term investment by a firm (usually an MNC) in productive assets in another country, giving it a lasting controlling interest.
Foreign aid Concessional (non-commercial) flows of money, goods or services from donors to ELDCs to promote development or relieve emergencies.
Humanitarian aid Short-term emergency assistance, such as food, shelter and medical care, in response to disasters or conflict.
Development aid Longer-term assistance, such as grants, soft loans and project aid, aimed at improving infrastructure, health, education and institutions.
Tied aid Aid that must be spent on goods and services from the donor country.
Debt relief The partial or total cancellation, or rescheduling, of a country's external debt, as under the HIPC initiative.
Official Development Assistance (ODA) Government aid designed to promote the economic development and welfare of developing countries; the UN target is 0.7% of donor GNI.
Non-governmental organization (NGO) A non-profit organization independent of government, such as Oxfam or MSF, that provides aid and development services.
World Bank A multilateral institution founded in 1944 comprising the IBRD, which lends to middle-income countries, and the IDA, which offers interest-free loans and grants to the poorest countries.
International Monetary Fund (IMF) A multilateral institution founded in 1944 that oversees the international monetary system and provides short-term loans to countries with balance of payments difficulties, usually with policy conditions.
Microfinance The provision of small loans, savings and insurance to people on low incomes who lack access to conventional banking.
Mobile banking Using a mobile phone to make payments, transfers and deposits, reducing the need for bank branches.
Land rights Legal rights to acquire, use, transfer and hold land, which provide security and collateral for investment.
Dual economy An economy with two sectors at very different levels of development, such as subsistence farming alongside modern export manufacturing.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence

Ethiopia vs Vietnam: which trade strategy?

Vietnam moved from a closed economy to export promotion after the 1986 Doi Moi reforms, attracting FDI in electronics and clothing; exports now exceed 90% of GDP. Ethiopia, landlocked and mainly agricultural, tried export incentives that had limited success (Hoang p. 626) but has since built industrial parks and a railway to Djibouti.

Ask the class
• Explain how export promotion can raise growth.
• Why may it be harder for Ethiopia than for Vietnam?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Export promotion raises net exports, shifting AD right; investment and economies of scale shift LRAS right (Fig 36.2).
  2. Access to world markets allows specialization according to comparative advantage and technology transfer via FDI.
  3. Ethiopia faces higher transport costs (landlocked), weaker infrastructure and a less skilled workforce – barriers from Ch35.
  4. Evaluate: export promotion depends on market access, a competitive exchange rate and infrastructure; import substitution may protect infant industries but risks inefficiency.
DiagramAD–AS: AD1 → AD2 and LRAS1 → LRAS2, output Yf1 → Yf2, stable price level (Fig 36.2, p. 594).
Scenario 2 · Equity

Brazil's Bolsa Família

Brazil's Bolsa Família pays monthly cash transfers to poor families on condition that children attend school and receive vaccinations. It has reached more than 13 million households at a cost of about 0.5% of GDP. At the same time Brazil raised its real minimum wage substantially in the 2000s.

Ask the class
• Compare the conditional cash transfer and the minimum wage as redistribution strategies.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Transfers raise the incomes of the poorest directly; they have a high MPC, so AD rises (multiplier).
  2. Conditions build human capital (education and health), breaking the poverty cycle in the next generation.
  3. A minimum wage helps formal-sector workers but not the informal or unemployed; if set above equilibrium it can cause unemployment.
  4. Evaluate: transfers are targeted and relatively cheap but need administrative capacity; minimum wages cost the government nothing but are poorly targeted in economies with large informal sectors.
DiagramLorenz curve shifting towards the line of equality; labour market diagram with a minimum wage above equilibrium showing excess supply of labour.
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

Egypt: FDI and the Zohr gas field

Egypt is Africa's largest FDI recipient, with an FDI stock of about $116.4bn and the UK as the biggest investor (Hoang p. 611). FDI inflows rose from $6,798m to $8,107m in 2016–18 (Table 36.3), driven by energy projects such as the Zohr gas field developed by Italy's Eni.

Ask the class
• Evaluate the impact of inward FDI on Egypt's growth and development.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. FDI adds to investment (AD rises) and to capital stock and technology (LRAS shifts right).
  2. Benefits: jobs, skills transfer, tax revenue, foreign exchange and lower energy imports, improving the current account.
  3. Costs: capital-intensive gas projects create few jobs; profits are repatriated (income debit on the current account); environmental risks.
  4. Judgement: impact on development depends on whether revenues are invested in education, health and infrastructure and on local linkages.
DiagramAD–AS with AD and LRAS shifting right; poverty cycle broken at the investment link (EPQ 36.4).
Scenario 4 · Intervention

Mozambique: debt relief and hidden loans

Mozambique's GDP (PPP) grew from about $4bn in 1993 to $37bn in 2017 and it received HIPC debt relief. Around 74% of its labour force works in agriculture (Table 36.6, p. 630). In 2016 it emerged that about $2bn of state-guaranteed loans had been hidden from the IMF (2012–14), leading donors to suspend support and the currency to fall.

Ask the class
• Discuss whether debt relief and multilateral assistance can achieve development when governance is weak.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Debt relief frees revenue from interest payments for spending on health, education and infrastructure.
  2. IMF and World Bank support provide finance and credibility, attracting private investment.
  3. Weak governance undermines this: hidden debts led to suspended aid, a currency collapse and higher inflation, hurting the poorest.
  4. Evaluate: assistance is most effective when combined with institutional change (transparency, anti-corruption) – a complementary mix.
DiagramPoverty cycle showing debt servicing diverting funds from investment; currency market with a leftward shift in demand after the scandal.
Scenario 5 · Sustainability

Vietnam and Nigeria – SDG1 and SDG7

Vietnam reduced extreme poverty from over 50% in the early 1990s to under 2%, and nearly all households now have electricity. Nigeria, Africa's most populous country, has the largest number of people in extreme poverty in the world by some estimates and over 40% of its population lacks access to electricity despite large oil reserves.

Ask the class
• Compare progress towards SDG1 (no poverty) and SDG7 (affordable and clean energy) in the two countries.
• Which strategies and barriers explain the difference?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Data: Vietnam is on track on both goals; Nigeria is stagnating on SDG1 and making slow progress on SDG7.
  2. Vietnam – export promotion, FDI, rural electrification and investment in education, with stable governance.
  3. Nigeria – dependence on oil (price volatility), corruption, weak infrastructure, insecurity and rapid population growth.
  4. Evaluate: growth alone is not enough; the composition of growth and the quality of institutions determine progress on the SDGs.
DiagramPPC for each country: Vietnam moves outward with more merit goods; Nigeria operating inside its PPC; poverty cycle for Nigeria.

Micro-loan (joint liability) game

Form groups of five as joint-liability borrowers, each with a small loan of 50 units. Each round, roll a die per borrower: 1 = enterprise fails, 2–6 = success with a return. The group must cover any member's repayment. Run three rounds and record repayments and income. Then run a round with a conventional bank that requires collateral, excluding most players.

Debrief
• Why did joint liability keep repayment rates high?
• Who was excluded under the collateral rules and why does that matter for development?
• What are the risks of over-indebtedness in microfinance?

Sustainable Development Report dashboards / UN SDG database · dashboards.sdgindex.org

Pairs select two countries and two SDGs and download indicator trends since 2015. They create line charts in Google Sheets, label them with the dashboard status arrows and annotate with one strategy and one barrier that explain the trend. The final chart set is used in a gallery walk.

Debrief
• Which SDG shows the largest gap between your two countries?
• Which strategy from this chapter seems to have made the biggest difference?
• What data limitations did you encounter?

OECD ODA data explorer · data.oecd.org/oda

Students chart ODA as a percentage of GNI for DAC donors and compare with the 0.7% target. They then look up ODA received by one recipient and calculate aid per capita.

Debrief
• Why do so few donors meet 0.7%?
• Does more aid per capita correlate with faster development?

Gapminder Tools · gapminder.org/tools

Animate income per person vs life expectancy for South Korea, Ghana, Vietnam, Nigeria and Cuba from 1960. Students pause at key policy moments (e.g. Doi Moi 1986) and annotate the chart.

Debrief
• Which countries grew fastest after turning to export promotion?
• How did Cuba achieve high life expectancy with low income?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Merit goods are only provided by the government – the private sector also provides education and healthcare (CM, p. 604).
  • MNCs always exploit workers with low wages – they often pay more than local firms, although conditions vary (CM, p. 611).
  • All debt is harmful – debt is a problem only when it is unaffordable or badly managed (CM, p. 616).
  • The World Bank is one institution – it comprises the IBRD and the IDA (CM, p. 619).
  • Development requires eradicating corruption – reducing it is the realistic aim (CM, p. 624).
  • All ELDCs are the same – they differ widely in resources, institutions and history, and contain rich individuals and regions (TT, p. 628).
  • Either the market or the state must lead – evidence suggests a complementary mix of interventionist and market-based policies works best (p. 630).
  • Aid always increases development – its effect depends on type, motive, conditions and governance in the recipient country (TT, p. 617).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Rwanda inconsistency (Ch36 vs Ch34): p. 623 names Ghana and Rwanda as countries that fail to empower women and promote gender equality, whereas the Ch34 case study (p. 557, Tables 34.7–34.8) presents Rwanda as the world leader for women in parliament (61%) and female labour force participation (84.16%). Treat p. 623 with caution: Rwanda performs strongly on political representation but less well on other measures such as access to land, credit and secondary education; ask students to cite specific indicators rather than a blanket judgement.
  • IMF membership (p. 619): the book gives 188 members; the IMF had 190 members by 2020 and 191 after Liechtenstein joined in 2024. The '29 members' refers to the original signatories of 1945.
  • Data now dated: the book uses the SDG Report 2019 (pp. 631–634). Use the latest UN Sustainable Development Goals Report for the final session.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Distinguish import substitution and export promotion.

Import substitution protects domestic industry to replace imports (inward-looking); export promotion encourages production for world markets (outward-looking).

2. Which curves shift in Fig 36.2 and why?

AD shifts right from higher net exports; LRAS shifts right from investment, economies of scale and technology.

3. Why does diversification reduce risk?

It spreads export earnings over more products, so a price fall in one commodity has less effect on income and revenue.

4. Define social enterprise and give an example.

An organization trading mainly for social objectives rather than profit, e.g. Oxfam or a social-enterprise MOOC provider.

5. Name the three market-based policies in the syllabus.

Trade liberalization, privatization and deregulation.

6. How do progressive taxes and transfers promote development?

They redistribute income to the poor, who have a higher MPC and spend on basic needs, health and education.

7. Why are merit goods under-provided by the market?

Consumers ignore the external benefits (MSB > MPB) or lack information, so consumption is below the social optimum.

8. Give two benefits and one cost of inward FDI.

Benefits: jobs, skills and technology transfer, tax revenue. Cost: repatriation of profits (or environmental damage, crowding out local firms).

9. What is tied aid and why is it criticized?

Aid that must be spent on the donor's goods; it acts as a hidden subsidy to donor firms and may not suit recipient needs.

10. What is the UN target for ODA?

0.7% of donor GNI.

11. What is the difference between the IBRD and the IDA?

The IBRD lends to middle-income countries at near-market rates; the IDA gives interest-free loans and grants to the poorest countries.

12. What does the IMF do?

It oversees the international monetary system and provides short-term loans to countries with balance of payments problems, usually with conditions.

13. How has mobile banking promoted development in Kenya?

M-Pesa lets people without bank accounts send, save and receive money cheaply, widening financial inclusion and supporting small businesses.

14. Why do secure land rights encourage investment?

Owners keep the returns from improving land and can use it as collateral for loans.

15. What is the usual conclusion on intervention vs market-oriented approaches?

Neither extreme works; a complementary mix suited to the country's context is most effective.
