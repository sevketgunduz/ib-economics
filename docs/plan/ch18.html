Macroeconomic objectives: economic growth

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Is faster economic growth always good for a country's people and its environment?

Session plans

Session 1: Actual and potential growth: diagrams and measurement SL/HL pp. 264–270

Objectives
  • Students will be able to define economic growth and distinguish actual (short-term) from potential (long-term) growth.
  • Students will be able to show actual growth with the PPC and AD/AS models, and potential growth with a PPC shift and an LRAS shift.
  • Students will be able to calculate real GDP and the real growth rate from nominal GDP and deflator data.

Session 2: Consequences of economic growth SL/HL pp. 271–274

Objectives
  • Students will be able to evaluate the effects of growth on living standards, including absolute poverty, health and education.
  • Students will be able to explain the environmental costs of growth and link them to sustainability and intergenerational equity.
  • Students will be able to explain why growth may widen income inequality and assess whether the benefits 'trickle down'.

Key terms and definitions

Economic growth An increase in real GDP (real output) over time, usually measured as the annual percentage change in real GDP.
Negative economic growth A fall in real GDP; two consecutive quarters of negative growth is a recession.
Actual (short-term) growth An increase in real output from using existing resources more fully or more efficiently; a movement from inside towards the PPC, shown by a rightward AD shift.
Potential (long-term) growth An increase in an economy's productive capacity, shown by an outward shift of the PPC or a rightward shift of LRAS (Yf1 to Yf2).
Actual output The real GDP an economy is currently producing; a point on or inside the PPC.
Potential output The real GDP an economy could produce if all its resources were fully and efficiently employed.
Factor endowments The quantity and quality of the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) a country has.
Labour force People of working age who are employed (including the self-employed) or unemployed and actively seeking work.
Labour productivity Output per worker (or per hour worked) in a given period; raised by education, training, capital and technology.
Occupational and geographical mobility The ease with which workers can change jobs (occupational) or move to where jobs are (geographical).
Nominal economic growth The annual percentage change in GDP measured at current prices, so it includes the effect of inflation.
Real economic growth The annual percentage change in GDP measured at constant prices, i.e. after removing the effect of inflation.
GDP deflator A price index for all domestically produced output, used to convert nominal GDP into real GDP.
Real GDP per capita Real GDP divided by the population; a measure of average real income per person.
Absolute poverty A situation in which income is too low to meet basic needs such as food, shelter and clean water.
Green GDP GDP minus the estimated costs of environmental degradation and resource depletion caused by production.
Intergenerational equity Fairness between present and future generations; unsustainable growth that depletes resources passes costs on to the future.
Human Development Index (HDI) A UNDP composite measure of development combining life expectancy, years of schooling and GNI per capita (PPP).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Change

Macao: casino-led growth

Macao's real GDP grew by 20.7% in 2011, 9.8% in 2012 and 14.3% in 2013, driven by gambling revenue of about $38bn, larger than Las Vegas (Hoang p. 273). Firms such as Sands and MGM invested heavily in resorts. COVID-19 then closed borders to visitors.

Ask the class
• Show how investment in new resorts leads to growth.
• Was 2012 a recession?
• Who gains and who loses from this growth?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Resort building raises I, so AD shifts right in the short run (AD1 → AD2): real GDP rises from Y1 to Y2 and the price level rises – actual growth.
  2. Once completed, the resorts add to productive capacity, shifting LRAS right – potential growth.
  3. 2012 was not a recession: growth fell from 20.7% to 9.8% but was still positive.
  4. Winners: casino owners, construction firms, government tax revenue, workers in tourism. Losers: residents facing higher property prices, problem gamblers, and an economy very exposed to one industry (shown by the collapse during COVID-19).
DiagramAD/AS diagram: AD1 shifts right to AD2 (Y1 → Y2), then LRAS1 shifts right to LRAS2 (Yf1 → Yf2); label the average price level and real GDP axes.
Scenario 2 · Sustainability

Vietnam: growth, poverty and pollution

Vietnam's GDP rose from $186.2bn to $245.2bn over five years (Q18.1, p. 270), and from $6.7bn in 1990 to over $260bn by 2020 (Hoang p. 235). Extreme poverty fell sharply, but air pollution in Hanoi and plastic waste in rivers have become serious problems.

Ask the class
• Which diagram shows Vietnam's long-term growth?
• How has growth affected living standards?
• Is this growth sustainable?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. FDI in factories, better education and new infrastructure raised productive capacity: PPC shifts outward and LRAS shifts right.
  2. Higher real incomes cut absolute poverty and raised spending on health and schooling.
  3. However, rapid industrial growth created negative externalities (air and water pollution) that GDP does not subtract; green GDP would be lower.
  4. Judgement: growth has been positive for living standards overall, but long-term sustainability requires cleaner energy and environmental regulation, or future generations bear the cost.
DiagramPPC diagram with PPC1 shifting out to PPC2 (point B to C), and an AD/AS diagram with LRAS1 → LRAS2.
Scenario 3 · Equity

Does growth trickle down? The USA

US real GDP per capita has roughly doubled since 1980, but the share of income going to the top 1% has risen strongly and median real wages have grown slowly. Forbes (2017) reported that the ten richest Americans held more wealth than the poorest 3.6 billion people in the world (Hoang p. 273).

Ask the class
• Why might growth increase inequality?
• Does it matter for living standards if the poor are still better off in absolute terms?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Growth driven by technology and capital raises returns to capital owners and highly skilled workers faster than to less-skilled workers.
  2. So income can grow for everyone while relative inequality rises; the Gini coefficient increases.
  3. Rising inequality can reduce well-being through relative poverty, weaker social mobility and social tension, and may reduce future growth if low-income families cannot invest in education.
  4. Judgement depends on policy: progressive taxes and spending on education can spread the gains (link to Ch 23).
DiagramNo AD/AS diagram needed; a Lorenz curve moving further from the line of equality (Ch 23) illustrates the change.

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Tables 18.1–18.3 (p. 268): nominal GDP $110.0bn, $115.5bn, $121.0bn (2019–21); deflator 100.0, 103.5, 105.0. Calculate real GDP and real growth.

SolutionReal GDP = nominal ÷ deflator × 100: 110.00, 111.59, 115.24 ($bn). Real growth 2020 = (111.59 – 110.00) ÷ 110.00 × 100 = 1.45%; 2021 = (115.24 – 111.59) ÷ 111.59 × 100 = 3.27%.

Problem 2. Worked example (p. 269), base year 2018: nominal GDP 120.0, 126.5, 136.2 ($bn); deflator 100, 102.8, 106.4. Compare nominal and real growth.

SolutionReal GDP: 120.00, 123.05, 128.01. Real growth: 2.54% then 4.02%. Nominal growth: 5.4% then 7.67%. The gap between them is due to rising prices.

Problem 3. Q18.1 (p. 270): Vietnam nominal GDP 186.2, 193.24, 205.28, 223.78, 245.21 ($bn) for five consecutive years (2014–18). (a) Year of highest nominal gain; (b) year of highest growth rate (2 d.p.).

SolutionGains: +7.04, +12.04, +18.50, +21.43, so the largest is in the final year (2018). Growth rates: 3.78%, 6.23%, 9.01%, 9.58%, so the highest is also 2018 at 9.58%. Note these are nominal figures.

Problem 4. Q18.2 (p. 270): nominal GDP ($bn) / population (m): USA 20,544 / 327.0; China 13,608 / 1,400; Japan 4,971 / 126; UK 2,855 / 66.19. Which has the highest and lowest GDP per capita?

SolutionUSA ≈ $62,826; China ≈ $9,720; Japan ≈ $39,452; UK ≈ $43,133. Highest: USA; lowest: China, even though China's total GDP is second largest.

Problem 5. Approximation (p. 268): nominal GDP rises by 5% and inflation is 3%. Estimate real growth.

SolutionReal growth ≈ 5% – 3% = 2% (exactly 1.05 ÷ 1.03 – 1 = 1.94%).

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A fall in the growth rate (e.g. from 3% to 1%) is a recession – output is still rising; a recession requires negative growth.
  • Economic growth means an increase in nominal GDP – growth refers to real GDP.
  • Actual and potential growth are shown the same way – actual growth is a movement to the PPC or an AD shift; potential growth is an outward PPC or rightward LRAS shift.
  • Growth benefits everyone equally – gains are often unevenly distributed and can widen inequality.
  • Higher total GDP means higher living standards – population growth and distribution matter, so use real GDP per capita and other indicators.
  • An increase in AD always gives sustainable growth – without an increase in LRAS it leads to inflation once the economy nears full capacity.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 270, Fig 18.6: the chart is titled 'Vietnam's GDP $bn (2014–20)' but shows only five annual values (186.2 to 245.21), which match 2014–2018. Tell students the years are 2014–18 before they attempt Q18.1.
  • p. 269 vs p. 270: Table 18.4 gives GDP per capita of $54,541.70 for the USA and $7,755 for China, but the Q18.2 data give about $62,826 and $9,720. The figures come from different years or measures; point out the mismatch so students do not think they have made an error.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define economic growth.

An increase in real GDP over time, usually measured as the annual percentage change in real GDP.

2. How is actual growth shown on a PPC?

By a movement from a point inside the PPC towards (or onto) the curve.

3. How is potential growth shown in the AD/AS model?

By a rightward shift of LRAS (Yf1 to Yf2), or of the vertical section of the Keynesian AS.

4. Nominal GDP is $150bn and the deflator is 120. What is real GDP?

150 ÷ 120 × 100 = $125bn.

5. Real GDP rises from $400bn to $412bn. What is the growth rate?

(412 – 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = 3%.

6. Growth falls from 4% to 2%. Is this a recession?

No – real GDP is still rising, just more slowly.

7. Give two reasons why growth rates differ between countries.

Differences in factor endowments and in the size, skills and productivity of the labour force (also investment and technology).

8. State one way growth improves living standards.

Higher real incomes reduce absolute poverty and allow more spending on health and education.

9. State one environmental cost of growth.

Increased pollution and carbon emissions (also resource depletion, deforestation, waste).

10. Why might growth widen income inequality?

The gains may go mainly to owners of capital and highly skilled workers.

11. What happens to the price level when LRAS shifts right with AD unchanged?

It falls, while real GDP rises.

12. What is intergenerational equity?

Fairness between present and future generations, e.g. not using up resources that future generations need.
