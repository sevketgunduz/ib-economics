Syllabus 2.7 Role of government in microeconomicsTextbook pp. 121–139Sessions 5 HL · 4 SLConcepts intervention, equity, efficiency, choice, economic well-being

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? Who gains and who loses when a government fixes prices, taxes a product or subsidizes it?

Common SL/HL: reasons for intervention, price controls, indirect taxes, subsidies, direct provision, command and control regulation, and all four intervention diagrams. HL only: consumer nudges (p. 136) and calculating the effects of price ceilings, floors, indirect taxes and subsidies on markets and stakeholders.

Key terms and definitions

Indirect tax A tax on spending on goods and services. It is paid to the government by the firm, which passes part of the burden on to consumers through a higher price.
Specific (per-unit) tax A fixed amount of tax on each unit sold. It shifts the supply curve vertically upwards by the amount of the tax, so the new curve is parallel to the old one.
Ad valorem tax A tax set as a percentage of the price, such as VAT. The supply curve pivots upwards because the tax per unit grows as the price rises.
Direct tax A tax on income and wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm that owes it, e.g. income tax or corporation tax.
Price control A government rule that sets a legal maximum or minimum price for a good, service or factor.
Price ceiling (maximum price) A legal maximum price set below equilibrium so that a good stays affordable. It causes excess demand (a shortage).
Price floor (minimum price) A legal minimum price set above equilibrium to raise producers' incomes. It causes excess supply (a surplus).
National minimum wage A price floor in the labour market. Firms may not pay less than this hourly wage. In the simple model it causes excess supply of labour, or unemployment.
Parallel (underground) market An illegal market that grows when a price ceiling causes a shortage. Goods are resold above the legal maximum.
Tax incidence How the burden of a tax is split between consumers and producers. The side whose demand or supply is less price elastic pays the larger share.
Subsidy A payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs. The supply curve shifts down (right), which lowers the price and raises output.
Transfer payments Payments from tax revenue to households, such as unemployment benefit or state pensions, with no good or service given in return.
Direct provision When the government supplies a good or service itself, such as schools, hospitals or roads, paid for from taxes. It is not necessarily free at the point of use.
Command and control (CAC) regulation Laws and rules that set what firms or consumers must or must not do, enforced by penalties. Examples are emission limits and bans on gambling with credit cards.
Community (social) surplus Consumer surplus plus producer surplus. It measures the total net benefit that a market gives society.
Deadweight (welfare) loss The loss of community surplus when output moves away from the allocatively efficient level. It is shown as a triangle on the diagram.
Government failure When government intervention makes the allocation of resources less efficient, or costs more than the market failure it was meant to correct.
Law of unintended consequences The idea that intervention often has effects that were not planned, and that these may harm some stakeholders.
Nudge theory HLThe idea from behavioural economics (Thaler) that small changes in how choices are presented can steer behaviour in a predictable way without banning options or changing incentives much.
Choice architect HLThe person or organisation, often the government, that designs the setting in which people make choices, e.g. by setting defaults.
Default (opt-out) option HLThe outcome that applies if a person takes no action, such as automatic enrolment in a pension. Defaults are powerful nudges because people tend to stick with them.
Sovereign wealth fund A state-owned fund that invests in shares, bonds, property or gold to earn income for the government.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Equity

Rent ceilings in a booming city

A city government caps monthly rents for two-bedroom flats at $1,800, while the market rent is $2,400. At the capped rent, 90,000 households want flats but landlords offer only 60,000. Some landlords turn flats into short-term holiday lets, and some tenants sublet illegally for $2,600.

Ask the class
• What is the size of the shortage?
• Who gains and who loses from the ceiling?
• Why might the quality of rented housing fall over time?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The ceiling ($1,800) is below equilibrium ($2,400), so it binds.
  2. Quantity demanded (90,000) is greater than quantity supplied (60,000), so there is excess demand of 30,000 flats.
  3. Tenants who find a flat gain because they pay less. Those who cannot find one lose, and so do landlords, whose revenue and producer surplus fall.
  4. The shortage creates a parallel market (illegal sublets at $2,600) and pushes landlords into unregulated uses such as holiday lets. Landlords have little reason to maintain their properties.
  5. Evaluate. Rent control helps sitting tenants in the short run. In the long run supply becomes more elastic, so the shortage grows. Direct provision of social housing or housing subsidies may work better (Fig 10.6 diagram).
DiagramPrice ceiling (Fig 10.6). Axes: Monthly rent ($) and Quantity of flats. Draw D and S crossing at $2,400. Draw a horizontal line at $1,800 below equilibrium, mark Qs = 60,000 and Qd = 90,000, and bracket the shortage. HL: shade the transfer from producer to consumer surplus and the deadweight loss (Fig 10.7).
Scenario 2 · Economic well-being

Mexico's tax on sugary drinks

Mexico introduced a tax of 1 peso per litre on sugar-sweetened drinks in 2014, roughly a 10% price rise. Studies found purchases fell by around 6–8% in the first two years, and the tax raised over 18 billion pesos a year. Health campaigners wanted a 2-peso tax.

Ask the class
• Is demand for sugary drinks price elastic or inelastic?
• Who bears most of the tax?
• Why might a larger tax be needed to cut obesity?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A specific tax shifts supply vertically up by 1 peso (a parallel shift).
  2. A 10% price rise cut quantity by about 7%, so PED is about 0.7, which is inelastic.
  3. Because demand is less elastic than supply, consumers pay most of the tax. The consumer price rises by nearly the full amount of the tax.
  4. Revenue is large because quantity falls only a little, but the health effect is modest.
  5. Evaluate. A larger tax, or combining it with education and nudges such as front-of-pack labels, may change behaviour more (Figs 10.15 and 10.17).
DiagramSpecific indirect tax with price-inelastic demand (Fig 10.17). Axes: Price (pesos per litre) and Quantity (litres). Show S shifting up to S + tax. Label P0, the higher consumer price Pc and the lower producer price Pp. Shade the consumer burden (large) and producer burden (small), and mark the deadweight loss.
Scenario 3 · Intervention

Guaranteed price for rice farmers

The government of a rice-growing country sets a minimum price of $400 per tonne, compared with an equilibrium price of $320. At $400 farmers supply 25 million tonnes, but domestic buyers want only 20 million tonnes. The government buys the surplus and stores it, then sells some abroad at $250 per tonne.

Ask the class
• How large is the surplus and what does it cost to buy?
• What is the net cost to taxpayers if the surplus is exported at $250?
• Is this a good use of public money?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The floor is above equilibrium, so it binds and causes excess supply of 5 million tonnes.
  2. Buying the surplus costs $400 × 5m = $2 billion.
  3. Exporting it at $250 brings back $1.25 billion, so taxpayers lose $0.75 billion, plus storage costs.
  4. Farmers gain higher, more stable incomes. Domestic consumers pay more and buy less, and taxpayers pay for the scheme. Foreign farmers are hurt by the cheap exports (dumping).
  5. Evaluate. Income support gives food security and rural equity, but it causes allocative inefficiency and government failure (Fig 10.10).
DiagramPrice floor with government purchase (Fig 10.10). Axes: Price ($ per tonne) and Quantity (m tonnes). Draw a horizontal Pmin line above Pe. Mark Qd = 20 and Qs = 25 and shade the rectangle Pmin × (Qs − Qd) as government spending.
Scenario 4 · Choice

Nudging pension saving (HL)

Before 2012, only about 42% of eligible UK private-sector employees saved in a workplace pension. The UK then introduced automatic enrolment: workers are enrolled by default and must actively opt out. Participation rose to over 85% within a few years, with little change to the tax incentives on offer.

Ask the class
• Why did a change in default have such a large effect?
• How does this differ from a subsidy for pension saving?
• What are the limitations of nudges?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Behavioural economics suggests people have status quo bias and put off decisions (bounded rationality).
  2. As choice architect, the government made saving the default without removing freedom of choice.
  3. A subsidy changes relative prices and costs the government money. A nudge costs very little but depends on people's inertia.
  4. Limitations: default contribution rates may be too low for a good retirement, some people cannot afford to save, and critics call nudges paternalistic.
  5. Conclusion: nudges work best alongside other policies, not instead of them.
DiagramNo compulsory diagram. Optionally draw a supply and demand diagram for 'pension saving' in which demand shifts right after the nudge. Label it clearly as an illustration, not an IB-required diagram.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Price ceiling (maximum price) with excess demand SL/HL

Fig 10.6, p. 126

  1. Label axes: Price (P) and Quantity (Q); title the market.
  2. Draw D and S crossing at Pe, Qe.
  3. Draw a horizontal line Pmax BELOW Pe, stopping at the demand curve.
  4. Read across to S for Qs and to D for Qd; bracket Qd − Qs as excess demand.
  5. Optional: mark the price a parallel market might charge, where the vertical line from Qs meets D.
Common errorsDrawing the ceiling above equilibrium, which does not bind; labelling the vertical gap as the shortage when it is the horizontal gap; forgetting that the quantity actually traded is Qs, not Qd.
Graphing toolCourse Simulator – Price ceiling/floor; EconGraphs

Welfare effects of a price ceiling HL

Fig 10.7, p. 126; Fig 10.8, p. 127

  1. Start from the ceiling diagram.
  2. Consumer surplus before = the triangle above Pe under D; after = the area under D above Pmax, up to Qs.
  3. Producer surplus after = the triangle below Pmax above S, up to Qs.
  4. Shade the deadweight loss triangle between D and S from Qs to Qe.
Common errorsAssuming consumers as a whole always gain: they gain the rectangle transferred from producers but lose part of the triangle. Placing the deadweight loss to the right of Qe.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Price ceiling/floor

Project the tool and set a ceiling below equilibrium. Students predict the shortage on mini-whiteboards before you reveal it. They then take turns moving the ceiling up past equilibrium to see it stop binding. Switch to floor mode and record surplus and government cost for three floors. HL students copy the consumer surplus, producer surplus and welfare-loss values into a table and check them by hand.

Debrief
• Why does the shortage grow when supply is more elastic?
• At which price level does the ceiling stop having any effect?
• Which stakeholder bears the cost of a floor if the government buys the surplus?

Course Simulator – Tax/subsidy incidence

In pairs, students impose a $2 per-unit tax, record the consumer and producer price, and then make demand steeper step by step. They plot the consumer share of the tax against the slope in Google Sheets. They repeat with a subsidy and compare the government cost for elastic and inelastic demand. The class agrees on a rule for incidence.

Debrief
• Why would a government pick price-inelastic goods to tax if the aim is revenue?
• Why is a subsidy more expensive when demand is elastic?
• Where does the deadweight loss come from?

Classroom pit market with a price ceiling

Hand out buyer cards (maximum willingness to pay) and seller cards (minimum cost). Run one trading round with no rules and record trade prices. In round two, announce a legal maximum price below the observed average and watch the queues and side-deals. Allow a 'black market' corner in round three where trades above the ceiling happen quietly. Record the number of trades each round.

Debrief
• How many trades took place with and without the ceiling?
• Who was left out and how did they react?
• How does this reflect Hoang's parallel-market argument (p. 126)?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Market: Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = −20 + 2P (Q in thousands, P in $). Find the equilibrium, consumer surplus and producer surplus. HL

Solution100 − 2P = −20 + 2P gives P = $30 and Q = 40,000. The demand intercept is P = 50 and the supply intercept is P = 10. CS = ½ × 40,000 × (50 − 30) = $400,000. PS = ½ × 40,000 × (30 − 10) = $400,000. Community surplus = $800,000.

Problem 2. In the same market the government sets a price ceiling of $25. Calculate the shortage, the new consumer and producer surplus, and the deadweight loss. HL

SolutionQd = 100 − 50 = 50,000; Qs = −20 + 50 = 30,000, so the shortage is 20,000 units. At Q = 30,000, demand price = 35. CS = rectangle (35 − 25) × 30,000 + triangle ½ × 30,000 × (50 − 35) = 300,000 + 225,000 = $525,000 (up $125,000). PS = ½ × 30,000 × (25 − 10) = $225,000 (down $175,000). DWL = ½ × (40,000 − 30,000) × (35 − 25) = $50,000. Check: 800,000 − 525,000 − 225,000 = 50,000.

Problem 3. In the same market the government sets a price floor of $35 and buys any surplus. Calculate the surplus, the government cost, consumer spending and producer revenue. HL

SolutionQd = 100 − 70 = 30,000; Qs = −20 + 70 = 50,000, so the surplus is 20,000 units. Government cost = 35 × 20,000 = $700,000. Consumer spending = 35 × 30,000 = $1,050,000 (down from $1,200,000). Producer revenue = 35 × 50,000 = $1,750,000 (up $550,000).

Problem 4. In the same market a specific tax of $10 per unit is imposed. Calculate the new price and quantity, tax revenue, the consumer and producer burdens, the change in consumer spending, and the deadweight loss. HL

SolutionNew supply: Qs = −20 + 2(P − 10) = −40 + 2P. 100 − 2P = −40 + 2P gives Pc = $35, Q = 30,000, and producers keep Pp = $25. Tax revenue = 10 × 30,000 = $300,000. Consumer burden = (35 − 30) × 30,000 = $150,000; producer burden = (30 − 25) × 30,000 = $150,000. Consumer spending changes from $1,200,000 to $1,050,000, a fall of $150,000. Producer revenue after tax = 25 × 30,000 = $750,000. DWL = ½ × 10,000 × 10 = $50,000. CS falls to ½ × 30,000 × 15 = $225,000; PS falls to ½ × 30,000 × 15 = $225,000.

Problem 5. In the same market a subsidy of $6 per unit is paid to producers. Calculate the new price and quantity, the government cost, and the shares of the subsidy received by consumers and producers. HL

SolutionNew supply: Qs = −20 + 2(P + 6) = −8 + 2P. 100 − 2P = −8 + 2P gives Pc = $27 and Q = 46,000; producers receive 27 + 6 = $33. Government cost = 6 × 46,000 = $276,000. Consumer share = (30 − 27) × 46,000 = $138,000; producer share = (33 − 30) × 46,000 = $138,000. DWL = ½ × 6,000 × 6 = $18,000.

Problem 6. Hoang Q10.2 (p. 127): good Y has equilibrium $20 and 20,000 units. At a $25 floor, Qs = 24,000 and Qd = 15,000. Find the excess supply and the cost to the government of buying it. HL

SolutionExcess supply = 24,000 − 15,000 = 9,000 units. Cost = $25 × 9,000 = $225,000.

Problem 7. Hoang Q10.6 (p. 134): good Z has equilibrium $6.00 at 60,000 units. After a $1 subsidy, the market price is $5.50 at 70,000 units. Calculate the government cost and the consumer share. HL

SolutionGovernment cost = $1 × 70,000 = $70,000. Consumer share = (6.00 − 5.50) × 70,000 = $35,000. Producer share = $35,000 (producers receive $6.50).

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • An indirect tax shifts the demand curve. It shifts supply; quantity demanded falls along the demand curve because the price rises (p. 131).
  • A price ceiling above equilibrium causes a surplus. It has no effect, because the market price is already below the ceiling.
  • Consumers always pay the full tax. The burden depends on the relative PED and PES.
  • A subsidy's government cost is subsidy × original quantity. It is subsidy × the NEW, larger quantity.
  • A price ceiling helps all consumers. Consumers who cannot buy because of the shortage lose, and some pay parallel-market prices.
  • Government intervention always fixes market failure. It can cause government failure and unintended consequences.
  • Nudges are just advertising. They change the choice setting (defaults, framing, social norms) without banning options or changing prices much (HL).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Command and control worked example (p. 136): the target is stated as a 60% cut in pollution, but the solution uses 70%, and the printed costs ($210,000 for Firm A and $108,000 for Firm B, $318,000 in total) are really 30% of each firm's emissions × unit cost, which is neither a 60% nor a 70% cut. For a 60% cut: A cuts 420 units × $1,000 = $420,000, B cuts 360 units × $600 = $216,000, total $636,000. Correct this before using the example in class.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Give four of the seven reasons for intervention in FETCH PM.

Any four of: support Firms, promote Equity, earn Tax revenue, influence Consumption, support low-income Households, influence Production, correct Market failure.

2. Where must a price ceiling be set to have an effect?

Below the equilibrium price.

3. What does a binding price floor create?

Excess supply (a surplus), because Qs > Qd at the minimum price.

4. What happens to the supply curve with an ad valorem tax?

It pivots upwards, because the tax per unit rises with price.

5. If demand is very price inelastic, who bears most of an indirect tax?

Consumers, because they reduce quantity very little when the price rises.

6. Give the formula for tax revenue on a diagram.

Tax per unit × quantity sold after the tax, i.e. (Pc − Pp) × Qt.

7. Why can a price ceiling lead to a parallel market?

The shortage leaves buyers willing to pay more than the legal price, so illegal resale above the ceiling becomes profitable.

8. State two disadvantages of subsidies.

Opportunity cost of public money; firms may become inefficient and dependent; they may cause overproduction and harm foreign producers.

9. What is government failure?

When intervention causes a less efficient allocation of resources than before, or costs more than the failure it tries to fix.

10. Give one example of command and control regulation.

The UK 2020 ban on gambling with credit cards, or emission limits set by an environmental agency.

11. HL: a $4 tax lowers output from 50,000 to 42,000. What is the deadweight loss?

½ × 8,000 × $4 = $16,000.

12. HL: what is a default option, and why is it a nudge?

The outcome if you do nothing, e.g. being enrolled in a pension. People tend to stay with defaults, so changing the default changes behaviour without removing choice.

13. HL: a $0.50 subsidy raises output to 80,000 units. What does it cost the government?

$0.50 × 80,000 = $40,000.
