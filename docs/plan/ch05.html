Key terms and definitions

Market equilibrium The situation where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change.
Equilibrium (market-clearing) price The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, leaving no shortage or surplus.
Disequilibrium A situation where quantity demanded does not equal quantity supplied at the current price.
Excess supply (surplus) When price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded; downward pressure on price follows.
Excess demand (shortage) When price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied; upward pressure on price follows.
Price mechanism The system in which the interaction of demand and supply determines prices, which in turn allocate resources and answer the basic economic questions.
Signalling function Price changes convey information to buyers and sellers about where resources are wanted (rising prices) or not wanted (falling prices).
Incentive function Price changes motivate producers and consumers to change their behaviour, e.g. higher prices encourage firms to expand quantity supplied.
Rationing function Rising prices reduce quantity demanded so that scarce goods go to those willing and able to pay the most.
Resource allocation The assignment of scarce factors of production to the production of particular goods and services.
Consumer surplus The difference between the maximum price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay; the area below the demand curve and above the price.
Producer surplus The difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they are willing to accept; the area above the supply curve and below the price.
Social (community) surplus The sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus; a measure of the total welfare gained from a market.
Marginal benefit (MB) The extra benefit from consuming one more unit; represented by the demand curve.
Marginal cost (MC) The extra cost of producing one more unit; represented by the supply curve.
Allocative efficiency Producing the combination of goods most wanted by society, where MB = MC (P = MC) and social surplus is maximised.
Welfare loss (deadweight loss) The loss of social surplus that occurs when output is above or below the allocatively efficient level.
Market failure A situation in which the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so social surplus is not maximised.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Scarcity

Avocados: 'green gold' in 2017

Mexico supplies about 45% of the world's avocados. In 2017 poor harvests in Mexico, Peru and California coincided with booming demand in the USA, Europe and China, pushing prices to a record high (Hoang p. 71).

Ask the class
• Show and explain the record price.
• Can we be sure what happens to quantity?
• Which functions of price are at work?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Poor harvests reduce supply: S1 shifts left to S2.
  2. Rising popularity (tastes) and incomes in China increase demand: D1 shifts right to D2.
  3. Both shifts raise price unambiguously; the effect on quantity depends on the relative size of the shifts.
  4. The high price signals scarcity, gives farmers an incentive to plant more trees (long-run expansion), and rations avocados to buyers who value them most.
DiagramSupply and demand for avocados: S1 → S2 left, D1 → D2 right; equilibrium moves from P1, Q1 to a much higher P2, with Q2 close to Q1.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Concert tickets: Taylor Swift's Eras Tour

In 2022 Ticketmaster's presale for Taylor Swift's US tour received demand from about 14 million users for roughly 2.4 million tickets. Face-value tickets of $49–$449 were resold for thousands of dollars.

Ask the class
• Why did a shortage occur?
• What function would a higher price perform?
• Is price rationing fair?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Stadium capacity is fixed, so supply is vertical at Qx (Hoang Fig 5.13).
  2. Face-value prices were set below the market-clearing price, creating excess demand.
  3. In the resale market, price rises until quantity demanded falls to Qx: the rationing function.
  4. Price rationing is efficient in the sense that tickets go to those willing to pay most, but it may be seen as inequitable because willingness to pay depends on income.
DiagramVertical supply S at Qx; demand D; face-value price Pf below Pe showing excess demand (Qd − Qx); resale price at Pe where D meets S.
Scenario 3 · Change

Salmonella and the egg market

A Salmonella outbreak linked to eggs is reported in the UK press (Hoang p. 68). Supermarket egg sales fall by about 20% within weeks, and egg producers are left with unsold stock.

Ask the class
• Show the effect on equilibrium.
• Explain what happens at the original price.
• How does the market return to equilibrium?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A health scare changes tastes and preferences, so demand shifts left from D1 to D2.
  2. At the original price P1, quantity supplied exceeds the new quantity demanded: excess supply.
  3. Producers cut prices to clear stock; as price falls, quantity demanded expands and quantity supplied contracts.
  4. A new equilibrium is reached at a lower price and quantity (P2, Q2), without government action.
DiagramEgg market (Fig 5.2): D1 shifts left to D2; excess supply shown at P1; new equilibrium at P2, Q2.
Scenario 4 · Efficiency

Uber surge pricing on New Year's Eve in Sydney

On New Year's Eve demand for rides in Sydney spikes after the midnight fireworks. Uber's algorithm raises fares by up to 3–4 times. Drivers who would otherwise stay home log on to the app.

Ask the class
• Explain surge pricing using the functions of price.
• Show the effect on a diagram.
• What happens to consumer and producer surplus?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A sharp increase in demand shifts D right; at the normal fare there would be a shortage of rides.
  2. The higher fare signals where drivers are needed.
  3. It gives drivers an incentive to supply more rides (expansion along S) and rations rides to passengers with the highest willingness to pay.
  4. At the new equilibrium, producer surplus rises; consumer surplus may fall for some passengers, but more trips take place than under a fixed fare, so social surplus is higher than with a shortage.
DiagramRide market: D1 → D2; normal fare P1 with shortage; surge fare P2 at new equilibrium; shade CS and PS at P2.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Market equilibrium SL/HL

Fig 5.1, p. 67

  1. Label axes Price (P) and Quantity (Q).
  2. Draw D sloping down and S sloping up.
  3. Mark the intersection and label Pe and Qe with dotted lines to both axes.
Common errorsLeaving off Pe and Qe; drawing curves that do not cross within the axes.
Graphing toolCourse Simulator – Supply & demand shifter

Changes in equilibrium: four shift cases SL/HL

Figs 5.2–5.5, p. 68

  1. For each case draw D and S with the original equilibrium P1, Q1.
  2. Shift one curve (label D2 or S2) and mark the new equilibrium P2, Q2.
  3. Add arrows on the axes showing the direction of change in P and Q.
  4. Name the cause (e.g. health scare, marketing, flood, technology).
Common errorsShifting both curves in response to one event; forgetting that a shift of one curve causes a movement along the other.
Graphing toolCourse Simulator – Supply & demand shifter / EconGraphs

Excess supply and excess demand SL/HL

Fig 5.6 and Fig 5.8, p. 69

  1. Draw D and S with Pe.
  2. For excess supply draw a price line above Pe; mark Qd and Qs where it meets each curve and bracket the gap as 'excess supply'.
  3. For excess demand draw a price line below Pe and bracket Qd − Qs as 'excess demand'.
  4. Add arrows showing price moving back towards Pe.
Common errorsMeasuring the gap vertically rather than horizontally; confusing which curve gives Qd and Qs.

Simulation and data activities

Classroom pit market (double auction)

Give buyers cards with private maximum values and sellers cards with private minimum costs. For five minutes they walk around and negotiate trades; each completed trade is reported and the price written on the board. After two rounds, prices converge towards the theoretical equilibrium. In round three, introduce a shock (e.g. a new batch of buyers) and watch the price rise. Finish by calculating each trader's surplus.

Debrief
• Why did prices converge without anyone setting them?
• What happened to price after the shock and why?
• Who gained the most surplus and why?

Veconlab – Market experiment · https://veconlab.econ.virginia.edu

Set up the double-auction market in Veconlab with students on laptops or phones. Run three trading periods and let the software plot trade prices against the theoretical equilibrium. Introduce a demand or supply shift in period four. Display the efficiency percentage Veconlab calculates and compare it with the classroom pit market.

Debrief
• How close did the market get to 100% efficiency?
• What does 'efficiency' mean in terms of surplus?
• Why might real markets be less efficient?

Course Simulator – Supply & demand shifter (surplus view)

Students set linear demand and supply, then switch on consumer and producer surplus shading. They shift each curve and record how CS, PS and the total change. Next they fix output below equilibrium and see the welfare loss triangle appear. (HL) Students verify the simulator's numbers with ½ × base × height.

Debrief
• When does social surplus reach its maximum?
• What happens to CS when supply increases?
• Why is output below Qe inefficient?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. At $3 a loaf, bakers supply 200 loaves and consumers demand 150 (Hoang p. 69). Identify the disequilibrium.

SolutionQs − Qd = 200 − 150 = 50 loaves of excess supply. Price will tend to fall.

Problem 2. At $4 a slice, consumers demand 50 pieces of carrot cake and the café supplies 30 (p. 69). Identify the disequilibrium.

SolutionQd − Qs = 50 − 30 = 20 pieces of excess demand. Price will tend to rise.

Problem 3. Demand for grass seed shifts so that price rises from $3 to $4 and quantity from 30,000 to 40,000 kg (practice 5.2, p. 70). Calculate the change in total revenue.

SolutionOriginal TR = $3 × 30,000 = $90,000. New TR = $4 × 40,000 = $160,000. TR rises by $70,000.

Problem 4. Schedule (practice 5.3/5.6): P = $0, 10, 20, 30, 40; Qd = 80, 60, 40, 20, 0; Qs = 0, 20, 40, 60, 80 (thousand units). Find equilibrium and the disequilibrium at $10 and $30.

SolutionEquilibrium: P = $20, Q = 40,000. At $10: Qd 60,000 − Qs 20,000 = 40,000 excess demand. At $30: Qs 60,000 − Qd 20,000 = 40,000 excess supply.

Problem 5. (HL) Using the same schedule, calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus at equilibrium. HL

SolutionDemand meets the price axis at $40, supply at $0. CS = ½ × 40,000 × ($40 − $20) = $400,000. PS = ½ × 40,000 × ($20 − $0) = $400,000. Social surplus = $800,000.

Problem 6. (HL) If the price were $10 (with sales limited by supply), calculate producer surplus. HL

SolutionAt $10, Qs = 20,000. PS = ½ × 20,000 × ($10 − $0) = $100,000.

Problem 7. A consumer is willing to pay $800 for a laptop priced at $750; a farmer is willing to sell potatoes at $3/kg but receives $3.80/kg (p. 74). Calculate CS and PS.

SolutionCS = $800 − $750 = $50. PS = $3.80 − $3.00 = $0.80 per kg.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A surplus just means overproduction. It means the price is above equilibrium, so Qs > Qd (p. 70).
  • Government is needed to restore equilibrium. Price adjusts by itself through the price mechanism (p. 73).
  • When demand rises, supply rises too. Supply does not shift; quantity supplied expands along the curve as price rises.
  • Consumer surplus is the money consumers save. It is the difference between willingness to pay and price, a measure of welfare rather than cash.
  • Maximum social surplus means the outcome is fair. Allocative efficiency says nothing about equity; a very unequal outcome can still be efficient.
  • Signalling and rationing are the same thing. Signalling relates to shifts that change price; rationing and incentives relate to movements along the curves (Top tips, pp. 72, 74).

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define equilibrium price.

The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.

2. What happens when price is above equilibrium?

Excess supply (Qs > Qd) puts downward pressure on price until equilibrium is restored.

3. A new technology cuts production costs. Effect on P and Q?

Supply shifts right: price falls and quantity rises.

4. Demand rises and supply falls. What is certain?

Price rises; the change in quantity is indeterminate.

5. Name the three functions of price.

Signalling, incentive and rationing.

6. Is the incentive function linked to a shift or a movement?

A movement along the curve (e.g. expansion of quantity supplied).

7. How does price ration a fixed supply of tickets?

Price rises until quantity demanded falls to the fixed quantity available.

8. Define consumer surplus.

The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.

9. Define producer surplus.

The difference between the price received and the minimum price producers would accept.

10. Where is social surplus maximised?

At the competitive equilibrium, where MB = MC.

11. What condition defines allocative efficiency?

MB = MC (P = MC): the last unit's value to consumers equals its cost to society.

12. (HL) D intercept $40, Pe $20, Qe 40,000. Calculate CS.

½ × 40,000 × 20 = $400,000.

13. Schedule: at $10 Qd = 60k, Qs = 20k. Identify the disequilibrium.

Excess demand of 40,000 units.
