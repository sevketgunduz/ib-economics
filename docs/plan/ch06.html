Critique of the maximizing behaviour of consumers and producers

Syllabus 2.4 Critique of the maximizing behaviour of consumers and producersTextbook pp. 77–91Sessions 4 HL · 0 SLConcepts choice, economic well-being, intervention, equity

Do consumers and firms really behave as the models assume, and can governments and businesses use behavioural insights to steer choices for better or worse?

Entire chapter HL only (AO3). SL students do not study this chapter; no IB-required AO4 diagrams.

Session plans

Session 1: Rational consumer choice and cognitive biases HL pp. 77–80

Objectives
  • Students will be able to state the assumptions of rational consumer choice.
  • Students will be able to explain rule of thumb, anchoring, framing and availability biases with examples.
  • Students will be able to evaluate how far biases undermine the rational choice model.
TimePhaseActivity
10 minHookAnchoring experiment: half the class write the last two digits of a phone number, the other half write '10'. Everyone then estimates the price of a used bicycle. Compare the averages of the two groups.

Biases (pp. 79–80): rule of thumb (defaults and habits), anchoring (NYC taxi tips 30/25/20%, averages previously 8–10%, tips rose 22%), framing ('99% fat free' vs '1% fat'), availability (shark attacks, air crashes).

Groups rotate, record their choices, and at each station name the bias and the rational alternative.
20 minScenario applicationPairs analyse Apple's iPhone launch at $600 and cut to $400 (p. 82) and a supermarket 'was $10, now $6' offer: which biases are exploited, and would a fully rational consumer respond differently?

5 minHomework setSet the bias-spotting diary.

Key terms and definitions

Rational consumer choice HLThe assumption that consumers make decisions that give them the greatest possible utility, given their income and prices.
Utility maximisation HLChoosing the combination of goods and services that gives the highest total satisfaction from a given budget.
Perfect information HLThe assumption that all buyers and sellers have full, accurate and freely available information about prices, quality and alternatives.
Behavioural economics HLA branch of economics that combines psychology and economics to explain how people actually make decisions, which often departs from rational choice.
Rule of thumb HLA mental shortcut or habit used to make decisions quickly, such as always buying the same brand, which may not give the optimal outcome.
Anchoring HLA bias in which people rely too heavily on an initial reference point (e.g. an original price) when making a judgement.
Framing HLA bias in which choices are affected by how information is presented rather than by its content (e.g. '90% survival' vs '10% mortality').
Availability bias HLJudging the likelihood of an event by how easily examples come to mind, leading people to overestimate vivid or recent risks.
Bounded rationality HLThe idea that rationality is limited by the information available, the time to decide and cognitive capacity, so people aim for satisfactory rather than optimal outcomes.
Bounded self-control HLThe limited ability of individuals to act in their own long-term interest, e.g. overeating or failing to save.
Bounded selfishness HLThe observation that people are not purely self-interested but often act fairly, reciprocally or altruistically.
Imperfect information HLA situation where buyers or sellers lack full or accurate information; when one side knows more than the other it is asymmetric information.
Choice architecture HLThe deliberate design of the way choices are presented in order to influence decisions.
Default choice HLThe option that applies automatically if a person takes no action, e.g. automatic enrolment in a workplace pension.
Restricted choice HLLimiting the number of options available to make decisions easier or steer behaviour, e.g. only healthy drinks in school vending machines.
Mandated choice HLRequiring people to state a decision in advance, e.g. declaring organ-donation preference when applying for a driving licence.
Nudge HLA low-cost change to the choice environment that alters behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives.
Profit maximisation HLThe objective of producing the output where marginal cost equals marginal revenue (MC = MR), so that the gap between total revenue and total cost is greatest.
Marginal revenue (MR) HLThe extra revenue from selling one more unit of output (ΔTR ÷ ΔQ).
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) HLA business objective in which a firm takes responsibility for its impact on society and the environment, going beyond legal requirements.
Market share HLA firm's sales as a percentage of total industry sales: (firm's sales revenue ÷ industry sales revenue) × 100.
Satisficing HLAiming for a satisfactory level of profit rather than the maximum, often to balance the interests of different stakeholders.
Growth (business objective) HLIncreasing the size of the firm, e.g. by sales revenue, output or market reach, through internal expansion or mergers and acquisitions.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Intervention

Opt-out organ donation in Wales and England

Wales introduced an opt-out ('deemed consent') organ-donation system in December 2015, and England followed in May 2020. Before the change, surveys showed that over 80% of people supported donation, yet only about 40% had registered as donors.

Ask the class
• Which type of choice architecture is this?
• Why might the default change behaviour?
• Evaluate the policy.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The switch from opt-in to opt-out changes the default choice: people are donors unless they register otherwise.
  2. Bounded rationality and inertia (rule of thumb) mean many people never act on their stated preference; the default aligns outcomes with preferences.
  3. Freedom of choice is preserved because anyone can opt out, so it is a nudge rather than a mandate.
  4. Evaluation: consent rates rose in Wales, but actual donations also depend on families, hospital capacity and trust; critics raise ethical concerns about presumed consent.
DiagramNo syllabus diagram; a bar chart of registration or consent rates before and after the change could be built from NHS Blood and Transplant data.
Scenario 2 · Choice

NYC taxi tips and anchoring

In 2009 New York taxis installed card-payment screens with suggested tips of 30%, 25% and 20%. Before this, the average tip was 8–10%; afterwards average tips rose by about 22% (Hoang p. 80).

Ask the class
• Which bias explains the change?
• Is the passenger worse off?
• Who is the choice architect?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The suggested percentages act as an anchor, a reference point that passengers adjust from.
  2. Framing also matters: pressing a preset button is easier than calculating a tip (a rule of thumb).
  3. The taxi companies and screen designers are the choice architects.
  4. A rational consumer would tip based on service quality; the anchor transfers surplus from passengers to drivers, raising questions about the ethics of commercial nudges.
DiagramNo syllabus diagram; a simple before/after bar chart of average tip percentages.
Scenario 3 · Choice

Profit maximisation vs satisficing at a Kenyan coffee roaster

A coffee roaster in Nairobi could charge $110 per kg and sell 10,000 kg, or charge $90 and sell 13,000 kg. Average cost is $70 per kg in both cases (figures from Hoang Fig 6.10, p. 89). The owner is considering the lower price to keep local cafés loyal and pay farmers a premium.

Ask the class
• Calculate profit in each case.
• Which is profit maximising?
• Why might the owner choose the lower profit?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Profit = (P − AC) × Q. At $110: (110 − 70) × 10,000 = $400,000.
  2. At $90: (90 − 70) × 13,000 = $260,000.
  3. $110 is the MC = MR output, so it maximises profit; $90 is satisficing.
  4. The owner may satisfice to build market share, reward stakeholders (CSR) and secure long-run survival; business objectives are not mutually exclusive (Top tip, p. 91).
DiagramFig 6.10-style diagram: AR = D and MR sloping down, MC and ATC; profit-maximising Q where MC = MR at 10,000 and price $110; satisficing at 13,000 and $90; shade the two profit rectangles.
Scenario 4 · Economic well-being

Sugar, calories and restricted choice in Singapore

From 2022 Singapore required 'Nutri-Grade' labels on pre-packed drinks, graded A to D by sugar content, and banned advertising for the least healthy D-grade drinks. The Health Promotion Board also works with hawker centres to offer lower-sugar options as the default.

Ask the class
• Identify the forms of choice architecture used.
• Which behavioural limitations are targeted?
• Evaluate effectiveness.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Labels address imperfect information and make the healthy choice easy (EAST).
  2. The advertising ban restricts the choice environment, while lower-sugar defaults use default choice.
  3. The target is bounded self-control and availability bias (tasty drinks are salient; long-term health risks are not).
  4. Evaluation: early evidence shows reformulation by producers, but effects depend on enforcement, habits and whether consumers substitute towards other sugary foods.
DiagramNo syllabus diagram; optionally a demand shift for D-grade drinks (tastes and information).

Simulation and data activities

The Evolution of Trust · https://ncase.me/trust

Students play the interactive game individually for about 15 minutes, working through the repeated prisoner's dilemma rounds. They note which strategy wins in each environment (e.g. Copycat, Always Cheat). In pairs they discuss what the game shows about self-interest and reciprocity. Link the results to bounded selfishness and to why cooperation emerges in repeated interactions.

Debrief
• Did pure self-interest win in the long run?
• What does the game suggest about the assumption of selfish, rational agents?
• Where do we see reciprocity in real markets?

Classroom ultimatum and dictator games

Pair students anonymously. Proposers receive 10 sweets and offer a share; responders accept (both keep their shares) or reject (both get nothing). Record all offers and decisions on the board. Repeat as a dictator game where responders cannot reject. Compare the class's average offers with the rational prediction of offering one sweet.

Debrief
• Why did responders reject positive offers?
• Why did dictators still give sweets away?
• What do the results show about bounded selfishness?

Veconlab – Behavioural experiments (e.g. bargaining or anchoring) · https://veconlab.econ.virginia.edu

Run a Veconlab bargaining or lottery-choice experiment with students on laptops. The software records choices and displays class distributions. Compare the distribution with the rational-choice prediction. Discuss framing by re-running one question with a different wording.

Debrief
• How far did class choices depart from the model?
• Did the wording change behaviour?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. A firm sells 10,000 units at $110 with average cost $70 (profit maximising) or 13,000 units at $90 with average cost $70 (satisficing) (Hoang p. 89). Calculate profit in each case. HL

SolutionProfit = (P − AC) × Q. Maximiser: (110 − 70) × 10,000 = $400,000. Satisficer: (90 − 70) × 13,000 = $260,000. The satisficing firm gives up $140,000 profit to sell 3,000 more units.

Problem 2. Heritage Pots has a 16.4% share of a market worth $14m a year (practice 6.1, p. 88). Calculate its annual sales revenue. HL

SolutionSales = 16.4% × $14m = 0.164 × 14,000,000 = $2,296,000 (about $2.3m).

Problem 3. Output 4 units: TR = $100, TC = $70. Output 5 units: TR = $120, TC = $85. Output 6 units: TR = $136, TC = $105. Find MR and MC for the 5th and 6th units and the profit-maximising output. HL

Solution5th unit: MR = 20, MC = 15 (MR > MC, expand). 6th unit: MR = 16, MC = 20 (MC > MR, contract). Profit is $30 at 4, $35 at 5 and $31 at 6 units, so profit is maximised at 5 units.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Behavioural economics proves people are irrational. It shows that rationality is bounded and that choices are predictably influenced by context, not that people act randomly.
  • A nudge is the same as a tax or ban. Nudges keep all options available and do not significantly change incentives; a tax or ban does.
  • Default, restricted and mandated choices are always easy to tell apart. Some nudges, such as cafés defaulting to skimmed milk, fit more than one category (Top tip, p. 84).
  • All firms aim only to maximise profit. Firms may pursue CSR, market share, satisficing or growth, and these objectives often overlap (Top tip, p. 91).
  • CSR always reduces profit. CSR can raise brand loyalty, market share and long-run profit.
  • Only governments use choice architecture. Firms use it constantly, e.g. supermarket layouts, decoy pricing and Starbucks cup sizes.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. State the three assumptions of rational consumer choice.

Consumer rationality, utility maximisation and perfect information.

2. Define anchoring.

Relying too heavily on an initial reference point, such as an original price, when making a judgement.

3. '95% fat free' vs '5% fat': which bias?

Framing: the same information presented differently changes choices.

4. Why do people overestimate the risk of air crashes?

Availability bias: vivid, widely reported events come easily to mind.

5. Define bounded rationality.

Rationality limited by information, time and cognitive capacity, so people choose satisfactory rather than optimal options.

6. Give an example of bounded selfishness.

Volunteering for a beach clean-up or giving to charity.

7. Why is a used car an example of imperfect information?

The seller knows the car's condition but the buyer does not (asymmetric information).

8. Pension auto-enrolment is which type of choice?

A default choice.

9. What is a nudge?

A low-cost change to how choices are presented that steers behaviour without removing options or changing incentives significantly.

10. What does EAST stand for?

Easy, Attractive, Social, Timely.

11. State the profit-maximising rule.

Produce where MC = MR.

12. Define satisficing.

Aiming for a satisfactory rather than maximum profit, balancing stakeholder interests.

13. Firm sales $3m, industry sales $12m. Market share?

25%.

14. Give one reason a firm might pursue CSR.

To build brand loyalty and reputation, which can raise long-run profit and market share.
