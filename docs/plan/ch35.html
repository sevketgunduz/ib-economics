Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 35

Barriers to economic growth and/or economic development

Syllabus 4.9 Barriers to economic growth and/or economic developmentTextbook pp. 564–589Sessions 4 HL · 3 SLConcepts economic well-being, equity, scarcity, intervention, sustainability

Why is economic development uneven? Why do some countries with abundant resources remain poor, while others with few resources prosper?

All IB content in 4.9 is common SL/HL. Sessions 1–3 cover the poverty cycle, all ten economic barriers and all political and social barriers, with the significance of barriers evaluated at the end of session 3. Session 4 is marked HL as an HL-only extension: an in-depth case-study workshop on the relative significance of barriers (DR Congo, Venezuela, Somalia).

Key terms and definitions

Poverty trap (poverty cycle) A self-reinforcing cycle in which low income leads to low savings, low investment and low productivity, which keep income low and pass poverty from one generation to the next.
Savings ratio The proportion of disposable income that households save.
Human capital The skills, knowledge, health and experience of the labour force, which determine its productivity.
Infrastructure The physical structures and facilities a country needs to function efficiently, such as roads, ports, power, telecommunications, water and sanitation.
Appropriate technology Technology suited to a country's resources, skills and needs – often labour-intensive and affordable – that raises productivity.
Economic inequality An unequal distribution of income or wealth among the population, measured by the Gini coefficient or the IHDI loss.
Primary sector dependence Reliance on agriculture, fishing, forestry or mining for a large share of output, employment and exports, exposing the economy to price volatility and low value added.
Market access The ability of a country's producers to sell goods and services in foreign markets, limited by trade barriers, standards and transport costs.
Informal economy Economic activity that is not officially registered or recorded in GDP and is outside tax and regulation; it is not necessarily illegal.
Capital flight The large-scale movement of money and assets out of a country because of economic or political uncertainty; it can be legal or illicit.
Indebtedness The total debt owed by a country's households, firms and government to creditors; high external debt diverts revenue to interest payments.
Debt-to-GDP ratio National (government) debt expressed as a percentage of GDP, used to assess the sustainability of debt.
Landlocked country A country with no coastline, which faces higher transport costs and depends on neighbours for access to ports.
Endemic disease A disease, such as malaria, that is constantly present in a region, reducing labour productivity and life expectancy.
Institutional framework The legal, financial, political and cultural systems and rules that shape economic behaviour, including courts, tax systems, banks and property rights.
Property rights Legally enforceable rights to own, use and transfer tangible and intangible assets such as land, buildings and intellectual property.
Micro-credit Very small loans to people on low incomes, often without collateral, to fund self-employment (pioneered by Muhammad Yunus).
Gender inequality Unequal treatment and opportunities for women and men in education, health, work, law and politics.
Corruption The abuse of entrusted power for private gain, such as bribery, embezzlement and fraud.
Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) Transparency International's annual ranking of countries by perceived public-sector corruption, from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean).
Good governance Accountable, transparent, effective and lawful exercise of power, measured for example by the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators.
Unequal political power A situation in which a small elite holds most political influence, allowing it to shape policy in its own interest rather than for broad-based development.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being

Niger: breaking the human capital link

Niger has the lowest Education Index in the world (0.198, Table 35.1, p. 571) and adult literacy around 31% (p. 547). Around 80% of the labour force works in subsistence agriculture, and fertility rates are among the highest in the world, so the population doubles roughly every 20 years.

Ask the class
• Explain how low human capital keeps Niger in a poverty cycle.
• Which link in the cycle would you target first?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Low education → low productivity and low wages; farmers cannot adopt new techniques.
  2. Low income → low savings → little investment in capital or schooling for the next generation.
  3. Rapid population growth reduces capital per person (Sachs, p. 565), deepening the trap.
  4. Target: girls' education and health – educated women have fewer children and invest more in each child (UNICEF, p. 570); evaluate cost, time lag and reliance on aid.
DiagramPoverty cycle diagram (Fig 35.1) with 'low education' and 'high population growth' boxes; PPC shifting outward (Fig 35.8) if human capital improves.
Scenario 2 · Interdependence

Thailand 1997: capital flight

In 1997 foreign investors pulled money out of Thailand after doubts about its fixed exchange rate and bank lending. The baht lost about half its value against the dollar, firms with dollar debts went bankrupt, and around 600,000 foreign workers left the country (Hoang case study, p. 575). The crisis spread to Indonesia and South Korea.

Ask the class
• Explain how capital flight acts as a barrier to growth and development.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Capital flight is a financial account outflow; supply of baht rises and demand falls, so the currency depreciates sharply.
  2. Firms with foreign-currency debt see repayments soar in baht; bankruptcies and falling investment shift AD left.
  3. Lower investment reduces future capital stock, shifting LRAS growth down; unemployment and poverty rise.
  4. The tax base falls and governments may need IMF loans with conditions, limiting social spending.
  5. Evaluate: the long-run damage depends on the strength of institutions and how quickly confidence returns.
DiagramForeign exchange market (S of baht shifts right, D shifts left, ER falls) plus AD–AS with AD shifting left.
Scenario 3 · Sustainability

Venezuela: oil dependence

Oil accounted for about 95% of Venezuela's exports and 25% of GDP (Hoang case study, p. 587). When the oil price fell from over $100 a barrel in 2014 to below $50, government revenue collapsed. The government printed money, price controls caused shortages, and millions emigrated.

Ask the class
• Explain why dependence on primary production is a barrier.
• How did political factors worsen the impact?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Over-specialization in one commodity exposes the economy to price volatility – a fall in world demand or a rise in supply cuts export revenue.
  2. The current account deteriorates and foreign exchange for imports of food and medicines runs out.
  3. Falling government revenue led to money creation and hyperinflation, eroding real incomes.
  4. Weak institutions, corruption and unequal political power prevented saving the oil windfall in a stabilization fund or diversifying.
  5. Capital flight and emigration of skilled workers (brain drain) deepened the fall in LRAS.
DiagramOil market: D shifts left, price falls; AD–AS: AD shifts left and SRAS shifts left (import shortages).
Scenario 4 · Equity

India's narrow tax base

Fewer than 10% of Indians pay income tax (Hoang p. 579), and much employment is informal. The government relies heavily on indirect taxes such as the Goods and Services Tax (GST), which take a larger share of the income of poorer households.

Ask the class
• Explain how an ineffective taxation structure acts as a barrier to development.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A narrow base limits revenue, so spending on education, health and infrastructure per person is low.
  2. Heavy reliance on indirect taxes is regressive, increasing inequality.
  3. The informal economy avoids tax, so public goods are under-provided and the formal sector is overburdened.
  4. Evaluate: digital payments and simplifying tax rules can widen the base, but enforcement requires strong institutions.
DiagramNo formal diagram required; optionally a Lorenz curve showing the impact of regressive taxation.
Scenario 5 · Equity

DR Congo: the paradox of plenty (HL extension)

DR Congo holds large reserves of cobalt, coltan, copper and diamonds (Hoang case study, p. 587) and supplies most of the world's cobalt used in batteries. Yet decades of conflict, weak institutions and corruption keep it among the poorest countries, with low HDI and large informal mining.

Ask the class
• Evaluate which barrier is most significant for DR Congo.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Natural resources are not the barrier – their mismanagement is. Revenues are captured by elites and armed groups (unequal political power, corruption).
  2. Conflict destroys infrastructure and human capital and drives capital flight.
  3. Weak property rights and legal systems deter formal FDI; informal artisanal mining escapes tax.
  4. Judgement: conflict and governance failures are most significant because they block the removal of every other barrier; economic barriers such as infrastructure are consequences rather than causes.
DiagramPoverty cycle diagram with 'conflict and corruption' as the entry point; a PPC inside its potential due to unemployed resources.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Poverty cycle (poverty trap) SL/HL

Fig 35.1, p. 564

  1. Draw at least four boxes in a circle: Low income → Low savings → Low investment → Low productivity → back to Low income.
  2. Add linked factors where relevant (e.g. low education, poor health, high population growth).
  3. Use clear arrows showing direction of causation.
  4. Add a policy 'break point' when answering strategy questions (e.g. micro-credit between low income and low investment).

Simulation and data activities

Bribery / licence office role-play

Assign one student as a licensing clerk and five as entrepreneurs with project cards worth 10–40 points that only pay out if licensed within two minutes. The clerk may demand side payments. Run round 1 with no oversight, then round 2 with an auditor who fines corrupt clerks. Record total points produced and who received them in each round.

Debrief
• How did corruption change which projects went ahead?
• Why does corruption act like a regressive tax?
• What institutional reforms are equivalent to the auditor?

Gapminder Tools · gapminder.org/tools

Plot income per person against child mortality, then colour bubbles by landlocked status or by region. Students identify three landlocked countries and compare them with coastal neighbours. Then switch to malaria deaths vs income.

Debrief
• Do landlocked countries systematically lag behind?
• Are there exceptions (e.g. Switzerland, Botswana), and what explains them?

Transparency International CPI and World Bank WGI · transparency.org/cpi

Pairs download CPI scores and one WGI dimension for 15 countries and plot them against GDP per capita (PPP) in Google Sheets. They fit a trendline and identify outliers.

Debrief
• Does corruption cause poverty or does poverty cause corruption?
• What does the scatter suggest about the significance of governance as a barrier?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • All informal economic activity is illegal – much of it is legal but unregistered, such as street vending, barter or payment in kind (CM, p. 574).
  • Micro-credit automatically lifts people out of poverty – lenders still assess creditworthiness, so the poorest may not qualify, and loans can cause debt stress (CM, p. 581).
  • All growth leads to development – technological unemployment and inequality can accompany growth (TT, p. 570).
  • Natural resource wealth guarantees development – DR Congo and Venezuela show the resource curse (p. 587).
  • All debt is a barrier – debt used for productive investment can raise growth; problems arise when it is unaffordable or badly managed.
  • The same barrier matters equally in every country – significance depends on context: resources, history, politics, stability, climate and population (pp. 586–587).
  • Geography is destiny – landlocked Switzerland and tropical Singapore show institutions and policy can overcome geographical barriers.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Data caveat: the World Bank discontinued the Ease of Doing Business rankings (Table 35.6, p. 579) in 2021 after data irregularities were found; use the 2019/2020 figures as historical examples only, or refer students to the World Bank's replacement Business Ready (B-READY) reports.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Draw a poverty cycle with four links.

Low income → low savings → low investment → low productivity → low income.

2. What are Sachs's six types of capital?

Physical, human, natural, business, public institutional and knowledge capital.

3. Why can rising inequality be a barrier to growth?

It limits the poor's access to education, health and credit, reduces aggregate demand and can cause social and political instability.

4. Define appropriate technology.

Technology suited to a country's resources and skills, often labour-intensive and affordable, that raises productivity.

5. Give two reasons why dependence on primary products is a barrier.

Price volatility and low (often falling) terms of trade; also low value added and low income elasticity of demand.

6. What share of the world's workers are informal (ILO 2018)?

About 61% (2 billion people), rising to about 90% in developing countries.

7. Define capital flight.

The large-scale outflow of money and assets from a country due to economic or political uncertainty.

8. Why does being landlocked slow development?

Higher transport costs, dependence on neighbours' ports and infrastructure, and reduced competitiveness of exports.

9. How do tropical diseases act as a barrier?

They lower life expectancy and productivity, raise health spending and deter investment and tourism.

10. Name the four parts of a weak institutional framework in the syllabus.

Legal system, ineffective taxation structures, banking system and property rights.

11. Why do insecure property rights deter investment?

Owners cannot be sure they will keep the returns and cannot use land as collateral for loans.

12. What does the CPI measure?

Perceived levels of public-sector corruption from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean).

13. How does gender inequality slow growth?

It wastes the potential of about half the population, lowering labour force participation, productivity and investment in children.

14. Why is conflict often the most significant barrier?

It destroys infrastructure and human capital, causes capital flight and prevents other barriers from being addressed.

IA angle

Theory of knowledge

Economic development draws from a set of values set out by Denis Goulet in 1971 (life sustenance, self-esteem and freedom). Does this make the pursuit of economic development unscientific?
