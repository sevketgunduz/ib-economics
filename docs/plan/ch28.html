Types of trade protection

Syllabus 4.2 Types of trade protectionTextbook pp. 436–454Sessions 5 HL · 4 SLConcepts intervention, efficiency, interdependence, equity, choice

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? When a government protects a domestic industry, who pays, who gains and how much is lost overall?

Key terms and definitions

Trade protection (protectionism) Government use of barriers to trade to shield domestic producers from foreign competition.
Barriers to trade Government-imposed measures, such as tariffs, quotas, subsidies and administrative rules, that make foreign goods less competitive in the domestic market.
Tariff A tax on imported goods and services. It raises the domestic price, increases domestic production, reduces imports and generates government revenue.
World supply (Sworld) The supply of a good from the rest of the world to a small economy, drawn as perfectly price elastic at the world price.
Consumer surplus The difference between the highest price consumers are willing and able to pay and the price they actually pay; the area below demand and above price.
Producer surplus The difference between the price producers receive and the lowest price at which they would be willing to supply; the area above supply and below price.
Community (social) surplus The sum of consumer and producer surplus, used to judge allocative efficiency and welfare.
Welfare (deadweight) loss The loss of community surplus that no stakeholder gains, caused by inefficient domestic production and reduced consumption under protection.
Non-tariff barriers Restrictions on international trade that are not taxes, such as quotas, subsidies and administrative barriers.
Quota A legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported in a given period.
Import licence A permit to import a set quantity under a quota. If the government sells licences it earns the quota rent; if they are given away, importers or foreign exporters keep it.
Quota rent HLThe gain from selling quota-limited imports at the higher domestic price: (Pquota – Pw) × quota.
Subsidy (trade) Payment from the government to domestic producers that lowers their costs so they can compete with imports or sell more abroad.
Production subsidy A per-unit payment to all domestic producers of a good, which shifts domestic supply down by the value of the subsidy.
Export subsidy A payment to domestic firms for each unit they export, making exports cheaper for foreign buyers.
Administrative barriers Bureaucratic rules such as product standards, health and safety regulations, labelling and customs procedures that raise costs for foreign firms or delay imports.
Embargo A complete ban on trade with a particular country, or in a particular good, usually for political or security reasons.
Exchange controls Government limits on the amount of foreign currency residents can buy or sell, which restrict spending on imports.
Retaliation When a country responds to another's trade barriers by imposing barriers of its own, which can lead to a trade war.
Tariff revenue HLGovernment income from a tariff: the tariff per unit multiplied by the quantity of imports after the tariff.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Intervention – protection creates winners (domestic firms, government) and losers (consumers, foreign firms).

India's tariff on imported smartphones

India has raised import duties on smartphones and components to encourage domestic assembly. Suppose the world price of a mid-range phone is ₹15,000 and a 20% tariff raises the domestic price to ₹18,000. Indian demand falls from 100m to 92m phones a year, while domestic output rises from 40m to 55m.

Ask the class
• Calculate imports before and after the tariff.
• Calculate government tariff revenue.
• Explain the effects on Indian consumers, Indian producers and foreign producers.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Before the tariff, imports = Qd – Qs = 100m – 40m = 60m phones.
  2. After the tariff, imports = 92m – 55m = 37m phones.
  3. Tariff revenue = ₹3,000 × 37m = ₹111bn.
  4. Consumers pay 20% more and buy 8m fewer phones, so consumer surplus falls. Domestic assemblers sell 15m more at a higher price, so producer surplus and employment rise. Foreign producers sell 23m fewer phones at the unchanged world price, so their revenue falls from ₹900bn to ₹555bn.
  5. The welfare loss triangles represent inefficient domestic production (units costing more than ₹15,000 to make) and lost consumption. If Indian assembly becomes efficient over time, there may be a long-run gain (infant industry, Ch29).
  6. Diagram: tariff diagram (Fig 28.2).
DiagramTariff diagram (Hoang Fig 28.2): Price (₹) vs Quantity (m phones); Dd, Sd; Sworld at ₹15,000; Sworld+tariff at ₹18,000; Q1 = 40, Q2 = 55, Q3 = 92, Q4 = 100; label areas c (PS gain), d and f (welfare loss), e (tariff revenue).
Scenario 2 · Equity – the burden falls on consumers and on exporters in a lower-income country.

Quota on garment imports

A country limits imports of cotton T-shirts from Bangladesh to 20 million a year. Before the quota, it imported 50 million at the world price of $4. After the quota, the domestic price rises to $5.50. Import licences are given free to established importers.

Ask the class
• Who receives the gain from the higher price on the 20 million imported T-shirts?
• How would the outcome differ if the government auctioned the licences?
• Why might Bangladeshi workers be harmed?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The quota shifts supply to Sdomestic+quota; the price rises until domestic demand equals domestic supply plus 20m.
  2. On the 20m imported units, the gap between the domestic price and the world price is ($5.50 – $4) × 20m = $30m. This is quota rent. With free licences it goes to the licence holders (importers or foreign exporters), not the government.
  3. If licences were auctioned, the government could capture up to $30m, which makes the quota's effect on stakeholders closer to that of a tariff.
  4. Bangladeshi exporters sell 30m fewer shirts, which reduces output and employment in an ELDC whose comparative advantage is garments.
  5. Society loses the welfare triangles of inefficient domestic production and lost consumption.
DiagramQuota diagram (Hoang Fig 28.7): Dd, Sd, Sworld at Pw; Sdomestic+quota drawn parallel to Sd, shifted right by 20m above Pw; new price Pquota; label the quota rent rectangle and welfare loss triangles.
Scenario 3 · Efficiency – resources are pulled into an activity where the country lacks a comparative advantage.

Subsidizing domestic rice farmers

Rice farmers in a country face competition from imports at a world price of $400 per tonne. The government pays domestic farmers a subsidy of $50 per tonne. Domestic output rises from 2m to 2.5m tonnes, while consumption stays at 5m tonnes.

Ask the class
• What happens to the price paid by consumers and to imports?
• Calculate the cost of the subsidy to the government.
• Why is there a welfare loss even though consumers are unaffected?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The subsidy shifts domestic supply down by $50. Consumers still pay the world price of $400 and buy 5m tonnes.
  2. Imports fall from 3m to 2.5m tonnes, so foreign producers lose 0.5m tonnes of sales ($200m of revenue).
  3. Subsidy cost = $50 × 2.5m = $125m, paid by taxpayers; this has an opportunity cost (e.g. spending on schools).
  4. Domestic farmers receive $450 per tonne and produce 2.5m tonnes; their revenue rises from $800m to $1,125m.
  5. Welfare loss = ½ × 0.5m × $50 = $12.5m: the extra 0.5m tonnes cost more to produce at home than to import.
  6. Diagram: subsidy diagram (Fig 28.10).
DiagramSubsidy diagram (Hoang Fig 28.10): Sworld at Pw ($400); Sdomestic and Ssubsidy $50 lower; Q1 = 2m, Q2 = 2.5m, Q3 = 5m; Ps = $450 on Sdomestic; shade the subsidy cost rectangle (a+b+c) and the welfare loss triangle (c).
Scenario 4 · Choice – regulation can protect consumers or restrict their choice.

Oktoberfest and the beer purity law

Only beer brewed within Munich's city limits and following the 1516 Reinheitsgebot may be served at Oktoberfest (p. 453). Foreign brewers, and even German brewers from other cities, cannot sell there. Supporters argue it protects quality and tradition.

Ask the class
• What type of trade protection is this?
• Who gains and who loses?
• Is this protection economically justified?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. It is an administrative barrier: a standard or regulation, not a tax or a quantity limit, that effectively excludes foreign firms.
  2. Munich brewers gain a protected market, higher prices and a guaranteed volume. Visitors lose choice and may pay higher prices.
  3. Unlike a tariff, the government receives no revenue, so the higher price is either a gain to protected producers or a pure cost.
  4. Evaluation: if the standard reflects real consumer preferences (quality or cultural value), part of the 'cost' is a genuine benefit. If it is simply keeping competitors out, it is disguised protectionism.
DiagramOptional: treat the barrier as raising the effective price of foreign supply (Sworld shifts up), as in a tariff diagram but with no revenue rectangle.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Effect of a tariff on price, production, consumption, expenditure, revenue and welfare SL/HL

Fig 28.2, p. 438; Fig 28.4, p. 440; Fig 28.5, p. 444

  1. Label axes: Price and Quantity; draw Ddomestic and Sdomestic.
  2. Draw Sworld as a horizontal line at Pw below the domestic equilibrium; mark Q1 (domestic Qs) and Q4 (Qd); imports = Q4 – Q1.
  3. Draw Sworld+tariff as a horizontal line above Sworld by the amount of the tariff; the new price is Pw+t.
  4. Mark Q2 (new domestic Qs) and Q3 (new Qd); new imports = Q3 – Q2.
  5. Label the areas: c = gain in domestic producer surplus; e = tariff revenue (tariff × (Q3 – Q2)); d and f = welfare loss; c+d+e+f = lost consumer surplus.
  6. HL: label foreign producer revenue as Pw × (Q3 – Q2), below the revenue rectangle e.
Common errorsDrawing the tariff as a shift of domestic supply; putting the revenue rectangle between Q1 and Q4; labelling foreign producers as receiving the tariff-inclusive price; forgetting that the welfare loss is two triangles, not one.

Effect of a quota on price, production, consumption, expenditure, revenue and welfare SL/HL

Fig 28.7, p. 445; Fig 28.8, p. 446; Fig 28.9, p. 447

  1. Draw Dd, Sd and Sworld at Pw; mark free-trade Qs (Q1) and Qd (Q4).
  2. Draw Sdomestic+quota: from the point where Sd meets Pw, follow Pw rightwards by the amount of the quota, then draw a line parallel to Sd.
  3. The new price Pquota is where Sdomestic+quota crosses Dd; mark the new Qd (Q3).
  4. At Pquota, domestic firms supply Q3 – quota (Q1 + (Q3 – Q2) in Hoang's notation); imports = the quota (Q2 – Q1).
  5. Label the quota rent rectangle (Pquota – Pw) × quota and the welfare-loss triangles.
  6. State who receives the quota rent (government if licences are sold; licence holders or foreign exporters otherwise).
Common errorsDrawing Sdomestic+quota starting at the origin or below Pw; treating the rent rectangle as government revenue automatically; not showing that domestic output rises.

Effect of a (production or export) subsidy on price, production, consumption, expenditure, revenue and welfare SL/HL

Fig 28.10, p. 448; Fig 28.12, p. 450

  1. Draw D, Sdomestic and Sworld at Pw; mark Q1 (domestic Qs) and Q3 (Qd).
  2. Shift domestic supply down by the subsidy to Ssubsidy; the new domestic output Q2 is where Ssubsidy meets Pw.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Tariff/quota welfare

Students set up a market with a world price below the domestic equilibrium. In tariff mode they increase the tariff step by step and log price, domestic output, imports, tariff revenue and welfare loss. They find the 'prohibitive' tariff at which imports fall to zero and note that revenue then disappears. In quota mode they find the quota that gives the same price as a chosen tariff and compare the two tables.

Debrief
• Why does tariff revenue first rise and then fall as the tariff increases?
• Why does welfare loss grow faster than the tariff?
• Under what licensing rule are a tariff and a quota equivalent?

Classroom pit market / double auction with imports

Give domestic seller cards with costs from £4 to £12, buyer cards with values from £4 to £20, and an 'importer' who sells unlimited units at £8. Trade for 5 minutes and record prices. Round 2: add a £2 tariff (the importer must pay £2 to the 'government' tin for each sale). Round 3: replace the tariff with a quota of 3 import units. Compare prices, quantities and who earned what.

Debrief
• What happened to the market price in each round?
• Which domestic sellers entered only because of protection?
• Where did the tariff money go, and who got the extra money under the quota?

World Bank Open Data – tariff rates · https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/TM.TAX.MRCH.WM.AR.ZS

Students download the weighted mean applied tariff for five countries at different income levels and chart them in Google Sheets. They compare with Hoang's Table 29.1 (the highest-tariff countries, p. 460). Each group then researches one non-tariff barrier used by a low-tariff country and discusses whether tariff data understate protection.

Debrief
• Why do small island economies often have high tariffs?
• Why might a country with low tariffs still be highly protected?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Hoang's UK wine tariff (Fig 28.4, pp. 440–444): Pw = £8, tariff = £2; at £8, Qd = 90m and domestic Qs = 50m; at £10, Qd = 80m and domestic Qs = 60m. Calculate the change in consumer expenditure, domestic producer revenue, foreign producer revenue and government revenue. HL

SolutionConsumer expenditure: £8 × 90m = £720m → £10 × 80m = £800m (+£80m). Domestic producer revenue: £8 × 50m = £400m → £10 × 60m = £600m (+£200m). Imports: 40m → 20m. Foreign producer revenue: £8 × 40m = £320m → £8 × 20m = £160m (–£160m); foreign firms receive the world price, and the £2 per bottle goes to the government. Government revenue = £2 × 20m = £40m. Check: £600m + £160m + £40m = £800m. (The textbook gives £200m for post-tariff foreign revenue; see errata.)

Problem 2. Tariff with consistent linear curves: Qd = 120 – 5P and Qs = 5P – 20 (Q in million units, P in $). World price $8; a $2 tariff is imposed. Calculate CS, PS, imports, tariff revenue and welfare loss before and after. HL

SolutionBefore: Qd = 80, Qs = 20, imports 60. CS = ½ × 80 × (24 – 8) = $640m; PS = ½ × 20 × (8 – 4) = $40m. After (P = $10): Qd = 70, Qs = 30, imports 40. CS = ½ × 70 × 14 = $490m (–$150m); PS = ½ × 30 × 6 = $90m (+$50m); tariff revenue = $2 × 40 = $80m. Welfare loss = 150 – 50 – 80 = $20m = ½ × 10 × 2 (production) + ½ × 10 × 2 (consumption). Consumer expenditure $640m → $700m; domestic revenue $160m → $300m; foreign revenue $480m → $320m.

Problem 3. Quota with the same curves (Qd = 120 – 5P, Qs = 5P – 20, Pw = $8): the government sets an import quota of 40m units. Find the new price, domestic output, quota rent and welfare loss. HL

SolutionSolve Qd – Qs = 40: (120 – 5P) – (5P – 20) = 40 → 140 – 10P = 40 → P = $10. Qd = 70m, domestic Qs = 30m, imports = 40m. Quota rent = ($10 – $8) × 40m = $80m, which goes to the government only if licences are auctioned. Welfare loss = $20m (as for the equivalent tariff). Foreign revenue = $10 × 40m = $400m if foreign exporters keep the rent, or $8 × 40m = $320m if the government captures it.

Problem 4. Hoang's cheese quota (Fig 28.9, pp. 447–448): 10m-unit quota; price rises from £8 to £10; Qd falls from 90m to 80m; domestic supply rises from 50m to 70m. Calculate the changes in consumer expenditure, domestic producer revenue and foreign producer revenue. HL

SolutionConsumer expenditure: £720m → £800m (+£80m). Domestic revenue: £8 × 50m = £400m → £10 × 70m = £700m (+£300m). Foreign revenue: £8 × 40m = £320m → £10 × 10m = £100m (–£220m), assuming foreign exporters keep the quota rent. Government revenue from licences (fee × number of licences) cannot be calculated from the diagram (p. 448).

Problem 5. Hoang's baby milk powder subsidy (Fig 28.12, pp. 450–451): Pw = $8, subsidy $2; Qd = 90,000; domestic output rises from 50,000 to 60,000. Calculate consumer expenditure, domestic and foreign revenue, government cost and welfare loss. HL

SolutionConsumer expenditure unchanged: $8 × 90,000 = $720,000. Domestic producer revenue: $8 × 50,000 = $400,000 → $10 × 60,000 = $600,000 (including $120,000 of subsidy). Imports: 40,000 → 30,000; foreign revenue $320,000 → $8 × 30,000 = $240,000. Government cost = $2 × 60,000 = $120,000. Welfare loss (area c) = ½ × 10,000 × $2 = $10,000.

Problem 6. Subsidy with consistent curves (Qd = 120 – 5P, Qs = 5P – 20, Pw = $8): a $2 per-unit production subsidy. Calculate the change in domestic output, imports, government cost, producer surplus and welfare loss. HL

SolutionProducers now receive $10, so Qs = 30m (from 20m); consumers still pay $8 and buy 80m; imports fall from 60m to 50m. Government cost = $2 × 30m = $60m. PS rises from $40m to ½ × 30 × 6 = $90m (+$50m). Welfare loss = $60m – $50m = $10m = ½ × 10m × $2. Consumer surplus unchanged at $640m.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Foreign producers receive the tariff-inclusive price – they receive the world price; the tariff per unit goes to the government.
  • A quota always raises government revenue – only if import licences are sold; otherwise importers or foreign exporters keep the quota rent.
  • A production subsidy lowers the price for consumers – in a small open economy, consumers still pay the world price; only the mix of domestic and imported supply changes.
  • The welfare loss is simply 'the area' – students must explain that it represents inefficient domestic production (resources that could be used elsewhere) and lost consumption (p. 450).
  • A tariff shifts the domestic supply curve – it shifts the world supply line upwards; domestic supply does not move.
  • Protection always saves jobs overall – jobs saved in the protected industry may be offset by job losses in industries that use the protected good as an input, or in export industries hit by retaliation.
  • Administrative barriers are harmless because they are not taxes – they can cause welfare losses as large as tariffs and raise no government revenue.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • pp. 442–443 (UK wine tariff): the book calculates post-tariff foreign producer revenue as £10 × 20m = £200m, using the tariff-inclusive price. Foreign producers receive the world price, so foreign revenue = Pw × imports = £8 × 20m = £160m (a fall of £160m, not £120m); the £2 per bottle (£40m) is tariff revenue for the government. Check: consumer expenditure £800m = domestic revenue £600m + foreign revenue £160m + tariff revenue £40m.
  • p. 443: the text says that after the tariff the price rises 'to £8'; it should say £10 (the calculation below it correctly uses £10).
  • Figs 28.4–28.5: the quantities plotted (Qd = 90m at £8 and 80m at £10; Qs = 50m at £8 and 60m at £10) do not fit straight lines with the stated price intercepts (£20 for demand, £4 for supply). As a result, the triangle-formula results (ΔCS = –£140m, ΔPS = +£80m) do not match the area method (c+d+e+f = £170m; c = £110m). The welfare loss d+f = £20m is the same both ways. Use the internally consistent data set in the calculations section for practice.
  • p. 450: the cross-reference to the 'law of comparative advantage (see Chapter 30)' should point to Chapter 27.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define a tariff.

A tax on imported goods and services.

2. On the tariff diagram, which area is government revenue?

The rectangle between the world price and the tariff-inclusive price, over the post-tariff imports (Q3 – Q2).

3. Which areas make up the welfare loss of a tariff and what do they represent?

Triangles d and f: inefficient domestic production (d) and lost consumption (f).

4. What happens to imports under a tariff?

They fall, because domestic output rises and domestic consumption falls.

5. What is an import licence?

A permit to import part of a quota; selling licences gives the government the quota rent.

6. How is Sdomestic+quota drawn?

Parallel to Sdomestic, shifted right by the size of the quota, starting at the world price.

7. Why does a production subsidy not change the price consumers pay?

The country is a price-taker at the world price; the subsidy only lets domestic firms supply more at that price.

8. What is the welfare loss of a subsidy?

Triangle c: the extra domestic units whose true cost is above the world price.

9. Give two examples of administrative barriers.

Any two of: product standards, health and safety rules, labelling requirements, slow customs checks, embargoes, exchange controls.

10. (HL) Pw = $5, tariff = $1, post-tariff imports = 30,000. Calculate tariff revenue.

$1 × 30,000 = $30,000.

11. (HL) After a tariff, imports are 20m and Pw = £8. What is foreign producer revenue?

£8 × 20m = £160m (foreign firms receive the world price).

12. (HL) Subsidy $3 per unit; domestic output after the subsidy is 40,000. What is the cost to the government?

$3 × 40,000 = $120,000.

13. (HL) Why can't quota revenue be calculated from the quota diagram?

It depends on the licence fee and number of licences sold, which the diagram does not show (p. 448).

Theory of knowledge

Is it ethically sound for economically developed countries to demand that less developed countries remove their trade barriers in the interests of free trade when they continue to provide income support to their farmers? Can one country know what is right for another to do?
