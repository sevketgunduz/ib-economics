Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 24

Demand management (demand-side policies): monetary policy

Syllabus 3.5 Demand management (demand-side policies) – monetary policyTextbook pp. 364–379Sessions 7 HL · 3 SLConcepts intervention, choice, change, economic well-being, interdependence

How do governments manage their economy and how effective are their policies? Can a central bank steer an entire economy just by changing the price of money – and what happens when interest rates are already close to zero?

Common SL/HL: central banks and the goals of monetary policy, real versus nominal interest rates (with calculation), expansionary and contractionary monetary policy on AD/AS diagrams, and the effectiveness of monetary policy. HL only: money creation by commercial banks (money multiplier), the tools of monetary policy (OMO, minimum reserve requirements, minimum lending rate, QE), and the money market diagram showing the equilibrium interest rate (sessions 2, 3, 4 and 7).

Key terms and definitions

Monetary policy The use of interest rates and the money supply by the central bank to influence aggregate demand and achieve macroeconomic objectives.
Central bank The monetary authority of a country that issues the currency, implements monetary policy, acts as banker to the government and to commercial banks, and is lender of last resort.
Interest rate The price of money: the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, expressed as a percentage per year.
Money supply The total quantity of money in circulation at a point in time, including notes, coins and bank deposits.
Inflation targeting A monetary policy framework in which the central bank uses interest rates to achieve a publicly announced inflation rate or range, e.g. 2%.
Credit creation HLThe process by which commercial banks create new money by lending out a proportion of their deposits, which are re-deposited and lent again.
Minimum reserve ratio (MRR) HLThe minimum percentage of deposits that commercial banks must hold as reserves at the central bank, which limits how much they can lend.
Money multiplier HLThe ratio showing the maximum increase in deposits created from an initial deposit: 1 ÷ reserve ratio.
Open market operations (OMO) HLThe buying and selling of government securities by the central bank to change the money supply and interest rates; buying injects money, selling withdraws it.
Minimum lending rate HLThe official interest rate the central bank charges on loans to commercial banks; also called the base, discount or refinancing rate.
Quantitative easing (QE) HLA policy in which the central bank creates new money electronically to buy bonds from financial institutions, increasing liquidity and lowering long-term interest rates.
Liquidity trap A situation in which interest rates are at or near zero, so cutting them further cannot stimulate borrowing and spending.
Demand for money (Dm) HLThe desire to hold wealth in the form of money for spending; it is inversely related to the interest rate, the opportunity cost of holding money.
Supply of money (Sm) HLThe quantity of money in the economy, shown as vertical because it is set by the central bank at a point in time.
Nominal interest rate The interest rate actually stated or agreed, not adjusted for inflation.
Real interest rate The nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation; it measures the true return to savers and cost to borrowers.
Negative real interest rate A situation in which inflation is higher than the nominal interest rate, so savings lose purchasing power.
Expansionary (loose) monetary policy Lowering interest rates and/or increasing the money supply to raise AD and close a deflationary (recessionary) gap.
Contractionary (tight) monetary policy Raising interest rates and/or reducing the money supply to reduce AD and close an inflationary gap.
Deflationary (recessionary) gap The situation in which real GDP is below potential (full-employment) output because AD is too low.
Inflationary gap The situation in which real GDP is above potential output, causing upward pressure on the price level.
Bank run A situation in which many customers try to withdraw their deposits at the same time because they fear the bank will fail.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Choice

Brazil tightens hard, 2021–22

Brazil's inflation rose above 10% in 2021 after food and energy price rises and a weaker real. The Banco Central do Brasil raised its Selic rate from 2% in March 2021 to 13.75% by August 2022. Inflation fell to about 6% by the end of 2022, but credit growth slowed and business investment weakened.

Ask the class
• Why did the central bank raise rates so far?
• What happened to real interest rates?
• Who lost from the policy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. With inflation above target, contractionary policy aims to reduce AD: higher borrowing costs cut consumption on credit and investment.
  2. Higher rates also attract capital inflows, strengthening the real and lowering import prices.
  3. Early in 2021 the real rate was deeply negative (2% − 6% ≈ −4%); by late 2022 it was strongly positive (13.75% − 6% ≈ 7.75%), which is when the policy bit hardest.
  4. Losers: indebted households and firms, and workers in interest-sensitive sectors; gainers: savers. Evaluation: part of the inflation was cost-push, which monetary policy tackles only by cutting demand.
DiagramAD/AS with SRAS and LRAS: AD1 shifts left to AD2, closing an inflationary gap (Fig 24.10).
Scenario 2 · Change

Japan's long battle with deflation

The Bank of Japan's policy rate has been close to zero since the late 1990s, and it introduced a negative rate of −0.1% in 2016. Despite this, inflation stayed below its 2% target for most of the period. The BoJ used large-scale QE, buying government bonds and even equity funds, until its holdings of government bonds exceeded half of all bonds issued.

Ask the class
• Why were rate cuts not enough?
• How is QE meant to work?
• What limits its effectiveness?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. With the rate at zero, the economy is in a liquidity trap: rates cannot be cut further and conventional policy is exhausted (p. 370).
  2. (HL) QE shifts the money supply curve right: the BoJ buys bonds, bank reserves rise, bond yields fall, and banks should lend more (Fig 24.6).
  3. Low confidence, an ageing population and firms' reluctance to invest meant households and firms did not borrow much more, so AD rose only slowly.
  4. Evaluation: monetary policy is weak when confidence is low (p. 376); supply-side reform and fiscal policy were also needed.
DiagramHL: money market with Sm1 shifting right to Sm2 and the rate falling towards zero; AD/AS with only a small AD shift.
Scenario 3 · Intervention

Covid-19 emergency cuts in the UK

In March 2020 the Bank of England cut Bank Rate twice within a week, from 0.75% to 0.1% (p. 378), and restarted QE, eventually buying £895bn of bonds. Lockdowns meant many shops, restaurants and airlines could not open. UK GDP fell by about 11% in 2020.

Ask the class
• What was the aim of the cuts?
• Why might they have had limited effect on spending in 2020?
• What strength of monetary policy does this show?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The aim was to reduce borrowing costs, support cash flow and prevent a financial crisis, shifting AD right or at least limiting its fall.
  2. In lockdown, households could not spend and confidence was very low, so lower rates did little to raise C and I in the short run.
  3. The speed of the response shows the strength of short time lags and flexibility (p. 377).
  4. Evaluation: fiscal policy (the furlough scheme) was more important in 2020; loose policy then contributed to inflation in 2021–22 when demand recovered.
DiagramAD/AS with AD shifting left sharply (shock), and a smaller rightward shift from monetary policy.
Scenario 4 · Intervention

Credit creation in Country M (HL)

In Country M the reserve ratio is 10%. A tourist deposits $50,000 in a local bank. Inflation is rising and the central bank is considering raising the reserve ratio to 20%.

Ask the class
• Calculate the maximum new money created at 10%.
• What happens if the ratio rises to 20%?
• Why might actual credit creation be smaller?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Money multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10; total deposits = $500,000; new money = $500,000 − $50,000 = $450,000.
  2. At 20%, multiplier = 5; total = $250,000; new money = $200,000, so credit growth halves.
  3. Actual creation is smaller if banks keep excess reserves, borrowers do not want loans, or people hold cash instead of depositing it.
  4. The higher ratio shifts the money supply curve left, raising interest rates and reducing AD.
DiagramMoney market: Sm1 shifts left to Sm3 and the interest rate rises (Fig 24.8).

Simulation and data activities

ECB 'Inflation Island' / €conomia games · https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb-and-you/explainers/html/index.en.html

Students play in pairs for 15 minutes. Each round they set the policy interest rate in response to news about demand and supply shocks and watch inflation respond with a lag. They record their decisions and outcomes in a table. After the game, pairs compare scores and explain one mistake they made.

Debrief
• Why did inflation respond only after a delay?
• Which shocks were hardest to deal with with interest rates alone?
• What does the game suggest about central bank credibility?

Banking chain classroom game (HL)

Six to eight students act as banks. The first receives a $1,000 deposit, keeps the required reserve and passes the rest as a loan to the next 'bank' on a sticky note. After six rounds, the class adds up total deposits and compares with the formula. Repeat with a higher reserve ratio and with one bank holding excess reserves.

Debrief
• How close did you get to the formula value after six rounds?
• What happened when one bank kept extra reserves?
• Why does the central bank care about the reserve ratio?

FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) · https://fred.stlouisfed.org

Students plot the US federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS), CPI inflation and the Fed's total assets (WALCL) on one dashboard from 2006 to today. They identify the zero lower bound periods, the QE rounds and the 2022–23 tightening. They calculate the real interest rate at three dates and annotate the chart.

Debrief
• When was the real interest rate most negative, and why?
• What did the Fed do when rates reached zero?
• How long did it take for inflation to fall after rates rose in 2022?

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

Project the tool with an economy in a deflationary gap. Students propose an interest-rate cut and predict the AD shift; the teacher moves AD. Repeat for an inflationary gap. Then place the economy on the flat section of the Keynesian AS and show that even a large AD shift is needed when confidence is low.

Debrief
• Why does the same AD shift have different effects on different sections of AS?
• What limits how far AD shifts when rates are cut?
• Why is closing an inflationary gap usually easier than closing a deep recessionary gap?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. A saver deposits $1,000 and the reserve ratio is 10%. Calculate the money multiplier and the new money created. Repeat for a 20% ratio. HL

Solution10%: multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10; $1,000 × 10 = $10,000; new money = $10,000 − $1,000 = $9,000. 20%: multiplier = 5; $5,000 − $1,000 = $4,000 (p. 368).

Problem 2. Show the first three loans in the credit-creation chain from a $1,000 deposit at a 10% reserve ratio. HL

SolutionLoan 1 = $900 (reserve $100); deposit $900 → loan 2 = $810; deposit $810 → loan 3 = $729. Each round is 90% of the previous one (p. 368).

Problem 3. A bank pays a nominal rate of 2% and inflation is 1.5%. Calculate the real interest rate.

SolutionReal rate = 2% − 1.5% = 0.5% (p. 373).

Problem 4. Worked example (p. 373): $10,000 is saved for a year at 2.5% nominal interest; inflation is 2%. Calculate the nominal and real return.

SolutionNominal return = $10,000 × 2.5% = $250. Real rate = 2.5% − 2% = 0.5%; real return = $10,000 × 0.5% = $50.

Problem 5. Nominal rate 3%, inflation 4.2%. Calculate the real rate and explain the effect on a saver with $1,000.

SolutionReal rate = 3% − 4.2% = −1.2%. The saver receives $30 interest ($1,030 in total), but goods costing $1,000 now cost $1,042, so purchasing power falls by about $12.

Problem 6. Q24.1 (p. 373): CPI 2017–20 = 100, 110, 120, 115; nominal rates 5.0%, 5.5%, 5.0%, 4.5%. Calculate the real interest rate in 2018, 2019 and 2020.

SolutionInflation 2018 = (110 − 100) ÷ 100 = 10% → real = 5.5 − 10 = −4.5%. 2019 = (120 − 110) ÷ 110 = 9.09% → real = 5.0 − 9.09 = −4.09%. 2020 = (115 − 120) ÷ 120 = −4.17% (deflation) → real = 4.5 − (−4.17) = 8.67%.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • The central bank sets inflation directly – it influences AD and expectations, which in turn affect inflation (p. 366).
  • Higher interest rates reduce AD mainly because people save more – the main channel is higher borrowing costs (mortgages, loans), since small rate changes barely affect saving (p. 375).
  • The real interest rate is the nominal rate plus inflation – it is the nominal rate minus inflation.
  • Monetary policy is the same as fiscal policy – both are demand-side policies, but monetary policy uses interest rates and the money supply, not taxes and government spending (p. 375).
  • (HL) The money supply curve slopes upward like a normal supply curve – in the IB model it is vertical, set by the central bank.
  • (HL) QE is just 'printing notes' – the central bank creates reserves electronically to buy bonds; its effect depends on banks lending and households/firms borrowing.
  • Lower interest rates always boost the economy – when rates are near zero or confidence is low, borrowing may not rise (liquidity trap).
  • (HL) A $1,000 deposit with a multiplier of 10 creates $10,000 of new money – total deposits reach $10,000, but new money created is $9,000 (p. 368).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 373: the negative real interest rate example uses a nominal rate of 3% and inflation of 4.2%, but then says $1,000 earns $50 (total $1,050) while prices rise to $1,060. These figures correspond to 5% and 6%, not 3% and 4.2%. Correct figures: $30 interest ($1,030) against prices rising to $1,042.
  • p. 370: QE is described as the central bank buying 'corporate bonds' and the next sentence calls bonds 'a form of government debt security'. QE mainly involves buying government bonds (some programmes also bought corporate bonds).
  • p. 370: the text says lower rates 'reduce the value of real debts'. Lower rates reduce the cost of servicing debts; they do not directly reduce the real value of the debt. Also 'important expenditure' should read 'import expenditure'.
  • p. 374: the text suggests government expenditure (G) rises because borrowing is cheaper. In the IB model G is set by government policy; C, I and net exports are the interest-sensitive components.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Name four functions of a central bank.

Sole issuer of currency, banker to the government, bankers' bank/lender of last resort, and conductor of monetary policy.

2. What is inflation targeting?

The central bank uses interest rates to keep inflation at an announced target, such as 2%.

3. Explain one way a fall in interest rates increases AD.

Borrowing becomes cheaper, so households spend more on credit and firms invest more, raising C and I.

4. (HL) State the money multiplier formula.

Money multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio.

5. (HL) Reserve ratio 5%, deposit $1,000. How much new money is created?

1 ÷ 0.05 = 20; $20,000 − $1,000 = $19,000.

6. (HL) What does a central bank do in an expansionary open market operation?

It buys government securities, injecting money and lowering interest rates.

7. (HL) What is QE and when is it used?

Creating money to buy bonds from financial institutions; used when interest rates are near zero.

8. (HL) Why is the money supply curve vertical?

Because the quantity of money is set by the central bank at a point in time and does not depend on the interest rate.

9. (HL) What happens to the interest rate if the central bank raises the reserve requirement?

Money supply shifts left and the equilibrium interest rate rises.

10. Nominal rate 4%, inflation 6%: real rate?

−2%.

11. Draw (in words) expansionary monetary policy closing a deflationary gap.

AD shifts right from AD1 to AD2 until it meets SRAS at the LRAS, raising real GDP to Yf and the price level.

12. Give two constraints on monetary policy.

Rates cannot be cut much below zero (liquidity trap), and low confidence means households and firms may not borrow.

13. Give two strengths of monetary policy.

Changes can be small, flexible and reversed quickly; decisions are made quickly by an independent central bank (short time lags).

14. Why might contractionary monetary policy harm growth?

Higher rates reduce investment, which lowers AD now and future productive capacity.
