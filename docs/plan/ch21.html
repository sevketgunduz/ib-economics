Macroeconomic objectives: sustainable level of government (national) debt

Syllabus 3.3 Macroeconomic objectives – sustainable level of government (national) debt (HL only)Textbook pp. 310–315Sessions 2 HL · 0 SLConcepts sustainability, intervention, choice, equity

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? When does government borrowing stop being a useful tool in a recession and start being a burden on future generations?

The whole chapter is HL only. SL students do not study it (0 SL sessions), though SL teachers may mention budget deficits when teaching fiscal policy in Chapter 25.

Objectives

Key terms and definitions

Budget deficit HLWhen government spending exceeds government revenue (mainly taxes) in a given period, usually a year (G > T).
Budget surplus HLWhen government revenue exceeds government spending in a given period (T > G); a surplus can be used to repay debt.
Balanced budget HLWhen government revenue equals government spending in a given period.
Government (national / public) debt HLThe total amount a government owes to domestic and foreign lenders; the accumulation of past budget deficits minus past surpluses.
Debt-to-GDP ratio HLGovernment debt expressed as a percentage of nominal GDP: (debt ÷ GDP) × 100; the standard measure of how affordable a country's debt is.
Sustainable (affordable) debt HLA level of government debt that can be serviced from future income without needing large tax rises, spending cuts, default or ever-greater borrowing.
Debt servicing costs HLThe cost of financing government debt: interest payments plus repayment of the principal as loans fall due.
Credit rating HLAn assessment by an agency (e.g. Moody's, S&P, Fitch) of a borrower's ability and willingness to repay; lower ratings mean higher interest rates on borrowing.
Default HLFailure by a borrower, including a government, to make interest or principal repayments on time.
Government bond HLA financial security sold by the government to borrow money, promising regular interest and repayment of the principal at maturity.
Bond yield HLThe effective annual return an investor receives on a bond; for the government it is the cost of new borrowing.
Austerity measures HLGovernment spending cuts and/or tax increases designed to reduce a budget deficit and the growth of government debt.
Opportunity cost of debt servicing HLThe alternative spending (e.g. on health, education or infrastructure) given up because revenue is used to pay interest on debt.
Crowding out HLWhen heavy government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private sector investment and consumption.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Sustainability

Spain and Brazil: same debt, different burden

In March 2020 Spain's government debt was about $1.33tn and Brazil's about $1.11tn (Hoang Table 21.1, p. 311). Spain's GDP was about $1.46tn and Brazil's about $2.02tn.

Ask the class
• Calculate each debt-to-GDP ratio.
• Whose debt is more affordable?
• Why are absolute figures misleading?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Spain = 1.3297 ÷ 1.46 × 100 ≈ 91.1%; Brazil = 1.1092 ÷ 2.02 × 100 ≈ 54.9%.
  2. Relative to the size of its economy (and so its tax base), Brazil's debt is smaller, so it is more affordable on this measure.
  3. Absolute figures ignore the income available to service the debt; a large economy can carry a larger debt.
  4. Evaluate: affordability also depends on interest rates (Brazil pays much higher yields than Spain), the currency the debt is in and who holds it, so the ratio alone is not enough.
DiagramNo theory diagram; a bar chart comparing absolute debt and debt-to-GDP for the two countries.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Greece: a debt crisis and austerity

Greece's debt ratio rose above 150% after 2008. Credit agencies downgraded Greek bonds to junk, yields rose above 20% in 2012, and Greece needed bailouts of about $159bn (Hoang p. 235). In return it cut public spending and raised taxes; unemployment rose to 26.8% in 2013.

Ask the class
• How did the downgrade affect Greece's debt?
• Show the effect of austerity on the economy.
• Did austerity reduce the debt ratio?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A lower credit rating means lenders see more risk of default, so they demand higher yields: debt servicing costs rise, making deficits bigger.
  2. Austerity cuts G and raises T, so AD shifts left; through the multiplier, real GDP falls by more than the initial cut and cyclical unemployment rises.
  3. Falling nominal GDP raised the debt ratio (the denominator shrank) even as borrowing slowed – Greek debt passed 180% of GDP.
  4. Judgement: austerity restored some confidence but at a large social cost; many economists argue slower fiscal adjustment would have been less damaging.
DiagramAD/AS diagram: AD1 shifts left to AD2 (austerity), real GDP falls from Y1 to Y2 below Yf, creating a deflationary gap.
Scenario 3 · Sustainability

Japan: 238% and no crisis?

Japan's government debt was about 238% of GDP in 2020, the highest in the world (Hoang p. 312), yet its 10-year bond yields were close to zero and it has never defaulted. Most Japanese government bonds are held by Japanese banks, pension funds and the Bank of Japan.

Ask the class
• Why can Japan carry such a high ratio?
• What are the risks?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Very low interest rates keep debt servicing costs low even on a large stock of debt.
  2. Borrowing in its own currency from domestic lenders reduces the risk of a sudden stop in lending and of default.
  3. Risks: if interest rates rise, servicing costs rise quickly; an ageing population raises pension and health spending and shrinks the tax base.
  4. Conclusion: sustainability depends on interest rates, growth and who holds the debt, not on a single ratio threshold.
DiagramNo theory diagram; a chart of Japan's debt ratio vs 10-year bond yield (Trading Economics).
Scenario 4 · Intervention

COVID-19: borrowing to protect the economy

In 2020 many governments ran record deficits. The UK announced a £350bn support package (Hoang p. 248) and its debt passed 100% of GDP. The USA's debt rose above $27tn by the end of 2020.

Ask the class
• Why was borrowing justified?
• What future costs may result?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Lockdowns cut C, I and X; without government support AD would have fallen much further (deeper deflationary gap, more bankruptcies and unemployment).
  2. Borrowing at very low interest rates kept debt servicing costs manageable in 2020–21.
  3. The deficits added to the stock of debt; when interest rates rose in 2022–23, servicing costs rose sharply, forcing trade-offs between tax rises and spending cuts.
  4. Evaluate: a deficit in a crisis can protect potential output (fewer firms fail, less long-term unemployment), which helps future tax revenue and debt sustainability.
DiagramAD/AS diagram showing AD falling (AD1 → AD2) during lockdowns and fiscal support shifting it back towards AD3, reducing the deflationary gap.

Each pair chooses two countries with different debt histories (e.g. Germany and Italy, or Chile and Argentina). They export the data to Google Sheets and chart both series. They then write three sentences explaining how deficits and GDP growth drove changes in the debt ratio.

Project the site for two minutes and let students watch the numbers change. Ask them to record total debt, debt per citizen and interest paid. Worked calculations

Problem 1. Table 21.2 (p. 311): Spain debt $1.3297tn, GDP $1.46tn; Brazil debt $1.1092tn, GDP $2.02tn. Calculate the debt-to-GDP ratios. HL

SolutionSpain = 1.3297 ÷ 1.46 × 100 ≈ 91.07%; Brazil = 1.1092 ÷ 2.02 × 100 ≈ 54.91%. Brazil's debt is more affordable relative to its economy despite a similar absolute amount.

Problem 2. Interpretation (p. 312): Japan's debt-to-GDP ratio is 238%. What does this mean? HL

SolutionThe government owes $2.38 for every $1 of annual GDP (e.g. $2.38m per $1m of GDP); even if all output were used, it would take about 2.4 years of total GDP to repay.

Problem 3. Debt servicing (p. 313): US debt $24tn. Calculate annual and daily interest at 0.5% and at 4.75%. HL

SolutionAt 0.5%: 24tn × 0.005 = $120bn a year; ÷ 365 ≈ $328.8 million a day. At 4.75%: 24tn × 0.0475 = $1.14tn a year; ÷ 365 ≈ $3.12bn a day.

Problem 4. Deficit and debt: a country starts with debt of $500bn and GDP of $1,000bn. It runs deficits of $40bn, $50bn and $30bn in the next three years while nominal GDP grows to $1,040bn, $1,060bn and $1,110bn. Calculate debt and the debt ratio each year. HL

SolutionYear 1: debt 540, ratio 540 ÷ 1,040 = 51.9%. Year 2: debt 590, ratio 590 ÷ 1,060 = 55.7%. Year 3: debt 620, ratio 620 ÷ 1,110 = 55.9%. Each deficit adds to the stock of debt; in Year 3 the ratio barely rises because nominal GDP grew by 4.7% while debt grew by 5.1%.

Problem 5. Exit ticket (Session 1): debt $900bn, GDP $1,200bn; then a $60bn deficit with GDP rising to $1,260bn. Calculate both ratios. HL

SolutionInitial: 900 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 75%. New debt = $960bn; ratio = 960 ÷ 1,260 × 100 ≈ 76.2%.

Problem 6. Credit downgrade: debt $800bn with an average interest rate of 3%; after a downgrade the rate on the debt rises to 5%. Tax revenue is $400bn. Calculate interest as a percentage of tax revenue before and after. HL

SolutionBefore: 800 × 0.03 = $24bn = 6% of revenue. After: 800 × 0.05 = $40bn = 10% of revenue. An extra $16bn a year must come from higher taxes, spending cuts or more borrowing.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A budget deficit and national debt are the same thing – the deficit is an annual flow; the debt is the accumulated stock.
  • A country with a larger absolute debt is always in more trouble – affordability depends on the debt relative to GDP, interest rates and who holds the debt.
  • Deficits are always harmful – deficits in a recession can support AD, jobs and future tax revenue.
  • Budget surpluses are always good – they may mean taxes are unnecessarily high or public investment is too low.
  • Austerity always reduces the debt-to-GDP ratio – if it causes GDP to fall, the ratio can rise.
  • A budget surplus means the government has no debt – a surplus only reduces existing debt if it is used for repayments.
  • Paying off debt quickly has no costs – spending cuts and tax rises have opportunity costs and often hit low-income households hardest.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 313: the book states that US interest of $120bn a year is '$328,767 per day'. The correct figure is about $328.8 million per day ($120bn ÷ 365). The book's later figure for 4.75% (more than $3.1bn per day) is correct, so the first figure is out by a factor of 1,000.
  • p. 313: the 4.75% example uses the US commercial bank lending rate. The US government actually borrows at Treasury bond yields, which were well below 2% in 2020, so the $1.14tn figure shows what could happen, not what the US actually paid. Point out this difference when you use the example.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define a budget deficit.

When government spending is greater than government revenue in a given year.

2. What is government (national) debt?

The total amount the government owes: the accumulation of past deficits minus past surpluses.

3. How does a budget deficit affect national debt?

It adds to it, because the government borrows to cover the gap.

4. Debt = $300bn, GDP = $500bn. What is the debt-to-GDP ratio?

60%.

5. Why is the debt-to-GDP ratio better than absolute debt for comparisons?

It relates debt to the size of the economy, and so to the income available to service it.

6. Name the three costs of high government debt in the IB guide.

Debt servicing costs, credit ratings, and impacts on future taxation and government spending.

7. What are debt servicing costs?

Interest payments plus repayment of the principal on government debt.

8. How does a credit downgrade affect a government?

Lenders see higher risk, so interest rates on new borrowing rise, increasing servicing costs.

9. Give one opportunity cost of high interest payments.

Less money for public services such as health, education or infrastructure.

10. Why might austerity fail to cut the debt ratio?

Spending cuts reduce AD and GDP through the multiplier, so the denominator falls.

11. Can the debt ratio fall while the government runs a deficit?

Yes, if nominal GDP grows faster than the debt.

12. Why can Japan sustain a debt ratio above 200%?

Very low interest rates, debt in its own currency, and mostly domestic creditors.
