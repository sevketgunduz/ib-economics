Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 26

Supply-side policies

Syllabus 3.7 Supply-side policiesTextbook pp. 401–418Sessions 4 HL · 3 SLConcepts efficiency, intervention, equity, sustainability, change

How do governments manage their economy and how effective are their policies? Can governments raise an economy's long-run growth rate – by freeing markets, or by investing directly in people, ideas and infrastructure?

All content is common SL/HL; there is no HL-only content in this chapter. Session 4 is an HL-only extension session (synthesis of demand-side and supply-side policy and extended evaluation practice) because HL has one more session.

Key terms and definitions

Supply-side policies Long-term government policies aimed at increasing the economy's productive capacity by improving the quantity and/or quality of factors of production, shifting LRAS to the right.
i-CLIP Hoang's mnemonic for the goals of supply-side policies: Incentives, Competition, Labour market flexibility, International competitiveness (low inflation) and Productive capacity.
Market-based supply-side policies Policies that reduce government intervention and free up markets to improve competition, flexibility and incentives, e.g. deregulation, privatization, tax cuts.
Interventionist supply-side policies Policies in which the government acts directly to raise productive capacity, e.g. spending on education, healthcare, R&D, infrastructure and industrial policy.
Deregulation The removal or reduction of government rules and barriers to entry in an industry to increase competition and efficiency.
Privatization The sale or transfer of state-owned enterprises or assets to the private sector.
Nationalization The purchase or takeover of private-sector assets or firms by the government.
Trade liberalization The reduction or removal of barriers to international trade such as tariffs, quotas and subsidies to domestic producers.
Anti-monopoly regulation Laws and regulators that limit restrictive practices, block anti-competitive mergers and control the market power of dominant firms.
Labour market flexibility The ability of firms to adjust wages, hours and the number of workers in response to changing economic conditions.
Labour union An organisation of workers that bargains collectively with employers over pay and working conditions.
Unemployment benefits Transfer payments to people who are able and willing to work but cannot find a job.
Minimum wage A legal lowest wage that employers may pay per time period; if set above equilibrium it creates a surplus of labour.
Incentive-related policies Cuts in personal income tax, business (corporation) tax and capital gains tax designed to encourage work, enterprise and investment.
Human capital The skills, knowledge, experience and health of the workforce that determine labour productivity.
Research and development (R&D) Activities undertaken to invent, improve and innovate products and production processes.
Infrastructure The physical and organisational facilities an economy needs to function, such as transport, telecommunications, power and sewage systems.
Industrial policy Targeted government support (subsidies, tax breaks, public investment) for industries considered important for growth and employment.
Vested interest A personal stake that an individual or group has in a policy outcome, which may lead them to resist reforms.
EPIC Hoang's mnemonic for the effects of supply-side policies: Efficiency, Productivity, Incentives, Competitiveness.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Efficiency

Indian telecoms: deregulation and competition

India opened its telecoms sector to private firms in the 1990s. After Reliance Jio entered in 2016 with free calls and very cheap data, mobile data prices fell by over 90% and India became one of the world's largest users of mobile data. Several competitors merged or left the market, and by 2023 three firms held most of the market.

Ask the class
• How did deregulation affect the supply side?
• What were the benefits to consumers and firms?
• What are the risks?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Removing barriers to entry increased competition, forcing firms to cut costs and innovate.
  2. Cheaper data lowered costs for businesses (e-commerce, digital payments) and improved access to information, raising productivity – LRAS shifts right.
  3. The price level can fall while real GDP rises, improving international competitiveness (Fig 26.2).
  4. Evaluation: consolidation into a few firms creates oligopoly power, so anti-monopoly regulation is still needed; rural coverage raises equity concerns.
DiagramAD/AS with LRAS1 shifting right to LRAS2; Yf1 to Yf2; price level falls from PL1 to PL2.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Germany's minimum wage

Germany introduced a national minimum wage of €8.50 an hour in 2015 and raised it to €12 in 2022. The textbook model predicts unemployment among low-skilled workers. Studies found little overall job loss, though some mini-jobs were converted or lost, and wage inequality at the bottom fell.

Ask the class
• What does the minimum-wage diagram predict?
• Why might the prediction not hold?
• Would abolishing it be a good supply-side policy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. With Wm above We, firms demand Nd while Ns want to work, so unemployment equals Ns − Nd (Fig 26.6).
  2. If demand for labour is inelastic, or firms have monopsony power, employment may fall little; higher pay may raise productivity and reduce staff turnover.
  3. Abolishing the minimum wage would lower labour costs and could raise employment of the low-skilled (a market-based supply-side policy).
  4. Evaluation: the equity cost may be high and evidence suggests limited employment gains, so abolition conflicts with the objective of reducing poverty.
DiagramLabour market: axes Wage and Quantity of labour; D and S of labour; We, Ne; Wm above We; Nd and Ns with the gap labelled unemployment.
Scenario 3 · Intervention

The US CHIPS and Science Act

In 2022 the USA passed the CHIPS and Science Act, providing about $52bn of subsidies for semiconductor manufacturing and research plus tax credits for investment. Firms including TSMC, Intel and Samsung announced new US factories. Critics argued that subsidies might be wasted, and that allies such as the EU and South Korea would respond with their own subsidies.

Ask the class
• What type of supply-side policy is this?
• What are the short-run and long-run effects?
• What are the constraints?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. It is an interventionist industrial policy combined with R&D support, targeting a sector seen as vital for growth and security.
  2. Short run: construction spending raises investment and AD (Fig 26.11). Long run: new capacity and technology raise productivity and shift LRAS right (Fig 26.12).
  3. Constraints: high budget cost (opportunity cost), long time lags before factories produce, risk of government failure in picking winners, and subsidy races with other countries.
  4. Evaluation: direct support of a strategic sector is a key strength of interventionist policy (p. 417), but success depends on skilled labour supply.
DiagramTwo-stage AD/AS: AD1 to AD2 in the short run; LRAS1 to LRAS2 in the long run.
Scenario 4 · Sustainability

UK privatization: the 1980s and after

The UK government sold British Telecom (1984), British Gas (1986), British Airways (1987) and British Steel (1988), raising more than £29bn (p. 405). Telephone waiting lists disappeared and prices fell in real terms in several sectors. However, water privatization in England has been criticised for high dividends, rising debt and sewage discharges into rivers.

Ask the class
• How is privatization meant to improve efficiency?
• Why were outcomes different across industries?
• What role does regulation play?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Private owners have a profit incentive to cut costs and innovate, improving productive efficiency.
  2. Where privatization was combined with competition (telecoms, airlines), consumers gained; where a natural monopoly remained (water), benefits depended on regulation.
  3. Weak regulation can lead to under-investment and negative externalities, an environmental constraint on market-based policy (p. 415).
  4. Evaluation: privatization raised one-off revenue and put no ongoing burden on the budget (a strength), but equity and environmental outcomes vary.
DiagramAD/AS with LRAS shifting right where efficiency improves; optionally a negative externality diagram for sewage discharges.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

Students start with an economy at full employment with inflation rising. Group A applies a supply-side policy (shift LRAS right); Group B applies contractionary demand-side policy (shift AD left). They record real GDP and the price level for each. Then they combine an AD increase with an LRAS increase of the same size and observe growth without inflation.

Debrief
• Which policy reduced inflation without reducing output?
• Why do supply-side policies take longer to work in reality than in the simulator?
• Why does the economy need enough AD to use new capacity?

Course Simulator – Price ceiling/floor (minimum wage mode)

Relabel the axes as wage and quantity of labour. Students set a minimum wage 10%, 20% and 30% above equilibrium and record labour demanded, labour supplied and unemployment. Next they make the demand for labour more inelastic and repeat. They conclude how elasticity affects the unemployment cost of a minimum wage.

Debrief
• How did unemployment change as the minimum wage rose?
• Why does elasticity of labour demand matter for policy?
• What gains for workers does the diagram not show?

OECD Data / World Bank Open Data · https://data.oecd.org

Pairs compare gross domestic spending on R&D (% of GDP), tertiary education attainment and labour productivity growth for four countries. They build a small scatter chart in Google Sheets of R&D spending against productivity growth. Each pair writes one claim and one caveat (correlation is not causation, time lags).

Debrief
• Do high-R&D countries have faster productivity growth?
• What other factors could explain the pattern?
• How long might R&D take to affect output?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Supply-side policies are always government-funded – many are market-based and cost little, and the private sector also invests in training and R&D (p. 413).
  • Supply-side policies work quickly – most have long time lags (education, infrastructure) before LRAS shifts.
  • Industrial policy is a competition policy – it is interventionist, supporting chosen industries (p. 413).
  • Supply-side policies only affect AS – most also affect AD in the short run, e.g. infrastructure spending or tax cuts (Fig 26.11).
  • Demand-side policies never affect LRAS – low interest rates or investment incentives can raise capital stock and FDI, shifting LRAS (p. 414).
  • Market-based policies benefit everyone – they may worsen inequality, job security and the environment (pp. 415–416).
  • A supply-side policy on its own guarantees growth – without enough AD the new capacity may be unused (p. 418).

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define supply-side policies.

Long-term policies that increase productive capacity by improving the quantity and/or quality of factors of production, shifting LRAS right.

2. What does i-CLIP stand for?

Incentives, Competition, Labour market flexibility, International competitiveness, Productive capacity.

3. Distinguish market-based and interventionist supply-side policies.

Market-based policies free markets (deregulation, privatization, tax cuts); interventionist policies involve direct government action (education, R&D, infrastructure).

4. Give two policies that encourage competition.

Deregulation and trade liberalization (also privatization and anti-monopoly regulation).

5. On the minimum-wage diagram, what is Ns − Nd?

The surplus of labour, i.e. unemployment created by a minimum wage above equilibrium.

6. How might reducing unemployment benefits reduce unemployment?

It increases the incentive to search for and accept work, reducing voluntary and frictional unemployment.

7. Why can income tax cuts raise AS?

They raise the reward for working and enterprise, which may increase labour supply and investment.

8. Name three interventionist supply-side policies.

Education and training, healthcare, R&D (also infrastructure and industrial policy).

9. Explain the demand-side effect of spending on infrastructure.

Construction spending increases G and incomes, shifting AD right in the short run.

10. Give two constraints on market-based supply-side policies.

Equity issues (e.g. weaker job security) and environmental harm (also time lags and vested interests).

11. Give one strength of market-based policies.

They improve resource allocation and place no burden on the government budget.

12. Give one constraint on interventionist policies.

They are costly, with an opportunity cost for the budget, and have long time lags.

13. What does EPIC stand for?

Efficiency, Productivity, Incentives, Competitiveness.

14. Why can supply-side policies reduce inflation?

By shifting LRAS right, they allow output to grow without upward pressure on the price level.
