Market failure – asymmetric information

Syllabus 2.10 Market failure – asymmetric information (HL only)Textbook pp. 171–177Sessions 2 HL · 0 SLConcepts choice, intervention, efficiency, economic well-being

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? When sellers know more than buyers (or buyers more than sellers), can we trust markets for used cars, insurance, healthcare and finance?

Session plans

Session 1: Asymmetric information, adverse selection and moral hazard HL pp. 171–174

Objectives
  • Students will be able to explain asymmetric information and why it leads to market failure.
  • Students will be able to distinguish adverse selection (before the transaction) from moral hazard (after the transaction).
  • Students will be able to apply both ideas to used cars, insurance, banking and the labour market.

Homework

P1 Q13.1 (p. 177): explain why asymmetric information leads to market failure and inefficiency [10].

Key terms and definitions

Imperfect information HLWhen buyers or sellers lack full knowledge about prices, quality or risks, so they make choices that do not maximise their welfare.
Asymmetric information HLA type of market failure in which one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other, leading to an inefficient allocation of resources.
Perfect knowledge HLThe assumption that all buyers and sellers have full, equal and free access to information about products and prices.
Opportunistic behaviour HLWhen the better-informed party exploits the other party's lack of information for its own gain, e.g. selling a faulty used car as sound.
Adverse selection HLAsymmetric information BEFORE a transaction about hidden characteristics (quality or risk). It leads to the 'wrong' buyers or sellers dominating the market, e.g. high-risk customers buying insurance, low-quality cars driving out good ones.
Moral hazard HLWhen a party protected from risk AFTER a transaction behaves more carelessly because the costs fall on another party, e.g. insured drivers or bailed-out banks.
Legislation (information failure) HLLaws that force disclosure or ban deception, e.g. compulsory health warnings on cigarettes or the duty to disclose criminal records.
Regulation (information failure) HLMonitoring and control of firms' behaviour by agencies, e.g. advertising standards bodies that require adverts to be legal, decent, honest and truthful.
Provision of information HLThe government supplies information directly or requires firms to disclose it, e.g. nutrition labels, energy-efficiency ratings or public health campaigns.
Signalling HLAction by the better-informed party to reveal its quality to the less-informed party, e.g. warranties, money-back guarantees, qualifications or brand reputation.
Screening HLAction by the less-informed party to gather information and sort high-quality from low-quality counterparts, e.g. medical checks for insurance, job interviews, credit checks.
Information overload HLWhen there is so much information that people cannot process it, so they make poor decisions or ignore it.
No-claims bonus HLA discount on insurance premiums for customers who have not made claims. It works as screening (revealing low-risk drivers) and reduces moral hazard.
Government failure HLWhen intervention to correct a market failure is ineffective or makes the outcome worse, e.g. regulation that is poorly enforced.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Equity

Health insurance and adverse selection

A private health insurer charges a single premium of $4,000 a year, based on the average expected medical cost. Customers know their own health, smoking and drinking habits better than the insurer does. Healthy young people decide the premium is too high and leave, so the average risk of those remaining rises.

Ask the class
• What is the information asymmetry?
• Why might the premium keep rising?
• How can insurers and governments respond?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Buyers know their own health risk (a hidden characteristic); the insurer does not. This is asymmetric information before the transaction.
  2. At an average premium, low-risk people find insurance poor value and drop out, while high-risk people stay: this is adverse selection.
  3. Average costs rise, so the insurer raises premiums, which drives out more low-risk buyers. The market shrinks and may collapse, which is an inefficient outcome.
  4. Private responses: screening (medical checks, questionnaires, risk-based premiums). Government responses: compulsory insurance (so low-risk people stay in), rules requiring honest disclosure, or public provision.
  5. Evaluate. Screening improves efficiency but may price the sick out, which raises equity concerns, so many countries combine compulsory insurance with regulation.
DiagramNo IB-required diagram. Optionally, a flow chart: average premium → low-risk exit → higher average risk → higher premium.
Scenario 2 · Intervention

Too big to fail: bank bailouts

In 2008 the US government injected $45bn each into Bank of America and Citigroup (Hoang p. 122), and other governments rescued banks such as RBS in the UK. Critics argued that bank managers had taken large risks with depositors' money because they expected to be rescued. After the crisis, regulators required banks to hold more capital.

Ask the class
• Why is this moral hazard?
• Who bears the cost?
• How does regulation try to reduce the problem?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Banks know more about the riskiness of their loans than depositors, taxpayers or regulators do.
  2. Implicit insurance from the government ('too big to fail') means banks keep the profits from risky lending while taxpayers bear the losses: this is moral hazard.
  3. The result is excessive risk-taking and a misallocation of resources, which is market failure.
  4. Responses: capital and liquidity requirements (regulation), stress tests, and rules forcing disclosure of risky assets.
  5. Evaluate. Regulation reduces risk but adds compliance costs; banks may move activity to less regulated 'shadow banks' (government failure).
DiagramNo required diagram. A stakeholder map (bank managers, shareholders, depositors, taxpayers, government).
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

The Volkswagen emissions scandal

In 2015 it emerged that VW had fitted software to diesel cars that cut emissions only during official tests. About 500,000 US cars and about 10.5 million worldwide were affected, and on the road they emitted up to 40 times the legal limit of nitrogen oxides. VW paid roughly $35bn in fines, recalls and compensation (Hoang p. 175).

Ask the class
• What information asymmetry existed?
• Why did regulation fail?
• How did the market respond?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. VW knew its cars' true emissions; buyers and regulators relied on test results.
  2. Buyers paid for 'clean diesel' they did not get, and third parties suffered air pollution (asymmetric information plus a negative externality).
  3. The test regime was predictable and relied on laboratory conditions, so it was easy to game: this was government failure in regulation.
  4. Responses: large fines, new real-driving emissions tests in the EU, and loss of VW's reputation (a failed signal), which cut sales.
  5. Evaluate. The scandal was uncovered by independent researchers, which shows the value of independent testing and provision of information.
DiagramOptional: a negative production externality diagram for diesel cars where the true MSC was higher than regulators believed.
Scenario 4 · Choice

Fake online reviews

Hotel and restaurant buyers rely on TripAdvisor, Google Reviews and Yelp to screen sellers (Hoang pp. 176–177). Some firms pay for fake five-star reviews, and competition authorities in the UK and the EU have introduced rules against buying or hosting fake reviews. Platforms now use algorithms and 'verified stay' badges.

Ask the class
• How do online reviews reduce asymmetric information?
• Why do fake reviews bring the problem back?
• Which responses are private and which are government responses?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Before booking, buyers know less about quality than hotels do, which is asymmetric information.
  2. Reviews are a screening tool: the less-informed buyer gathers information from past customers.
  3. Fake reviews let low-quality sellers imitate high-quality ones, so reviews become less trusted and adverse selection returns.
  4. Private responses: platform verification and badges (signalling by honest sellers). Government responses: consumer-protection laws banning fake reviews, with fines (legislation and regulation).
  5. Evaluate. Enforcement across borders is hard, and too many reviews can cause information overload.
DiagramNo IB-required diagram. A two-column table: private responses (screening, signalling) vs government responses (legislation, regulation, provision of information).

Simulation and data activities

Market for lemons card game (or Veconlab – Lemons market) · veconlab.econ.virginia.edu (or printed cards)

Give half the class seller cards marked 'Peach' (worth $8,000 to the seller, $10,000 to buyers) or 'Lemon' (worth $1,000 to the seller, $2,000 to buyers), with two-thirds lemons. Buyers see no cards. In round 1 buyers bid for 'a car'; record the average price. Sellers of peaches refuse bids below $8,000, so they drop out. In round 2 let sellers offer a warranty (signalling) that costs lemon owners $3,000 to honour, and see whether peaches trade.

Debrief
• Why did peach sellers leave the market in round 1?
• How did the warranty change the outcome?
• Why is this an example of adverse selection rather than moral hazard?

Insurance moral hazard dice game

Each student 'owns' a $100 bike. Each round they choose 'careful' (costs $5 in effort, loss if they roll a 1) or 'careless' (free, loss if they roll 1–3). Play three rounds uninsured, then three rounds with full insurance for a $20 premium. Tally how many choose careless in each case and calculate the insurer's profit or loss.

Debrief
• Why did behaviour change once people were insured?
• How could the insurer design the contract (excess, no-claims bonus) to reduce moral hazard?
• Where do we see similar behaviour in banking or welfare systems?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Adverse selection and moral hazard are the same. Adverse selection concerns hidden characteristics before a transaction; moral hazard concerns hidden actions after it (pp. 173–174).
  • Signalling here means price signals. In this topic, signalling is an action by the better-informed party, not the signalling function of the price mechanism (p. 175).
  • Screening is done by the seller. Screening is done by the less-informed party, which is often the buyer, insurer or employer (p. 176).
  • Only sellers have more information. Buyers often know more, e.g. insurance customers about their health (p. 173).
  • More information always solves the problem. Information overload and poor enforcement can make information provision ineffective (pp. 174, 177).
  • Asymmetric information only matters in finance. It affects everyday markets such as food labels, used cars, tradespeople and online sellers (pp. 171–172).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Hoang p. 176 credits Michael Spence (1973) with first developing screening. Spence's 1973 paper ('Job Market Signaling') developed SIGNALLING; screening is usually credited to Joseph Stiglitz (e.g. Rothschild and Stiglitz, 1976). All three Nobel laureates of 2001 (Akerlof, Spence, Stiglitz) worked on asymmetric information.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define asymmetric information.

One party to a transaction has more or better information than the other, which leads to inefficient outcomes.

2. Does adverse selection happen before or after the transaction?

Before: it concerns hidden characteristics such as quality or risk.

3. Give an example of moral hazard.

A fully insured driver stops locking the car; a bank takes big risks expecting a bailout.

4. Why might good used cars disappear from a market?

Buyers pay only an average price, so owners of good cars will not sell, and lemons dominate.

5. Who uses signalling? Give one example.

The better-informed party, e.g. a dealer offering a warranty.

6. Who uses screening? Give one example.

The less-informed party, e.g. an insurer requiring a medical check.

7. Give one government response to asymmetric information.

Compulsory nutrition labels, health warnings, or advertising regulation.

8. What is information overload?

So much information that people cannot process it and still make poor choices.

9. How does a no-claims bonus reduce asymmetric information problems?

It screens for low-risk drivers and rewards careful behaviour, reducing moral hazard.

10. Why was the VW scandal also a government failure?

Predictable laboratory tests let VW game the regulation for years without detection.

11. Why does asymmetric information cause market failure?

Prices and quantities do not reflect true quality or risk, so resources are misallocated and some mutually beneficial trades do not take place.
