Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 23

Economics of inequality and poverty

Syllabus 3.4 Economics of inequality and povertyTextbook pp. 325–363Sessions 7 HL · 4 SLConcepts equity, economic well-being, intervention, choice, interdependence

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Why are income and wealth so unequally shared within and between countries – and which policies actually reduce poverty and inequality without damaging incentives?

Common SL/HL: equity and equality, income and wealth inequality, Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient, absolute and relative poverty, poverty measures (IPL, MIS, MPI), causes and impacts of inequality, progressive/regressive/proportional, direct and indirect taxes, and further policies. HL only: constructing a Lorenz curve from quintile data; average and marginal tax rates; calculating total tax and average tax rates from data; calculating indirect tax paid from a given amount of expenditure (sessions 5–7).

Key terms and definitions

Equity Economic fairness: the idea that the distribution of income and wealth should be fair, which may allow justified differences (e.g. pay for skill or effort).
Equality A situation in which everyone receives the same share of income or wealth, regardless of contribution.
Income A flow of money received over a period of time from factors of production: wages, rent, interest and profit, plus transfer payments.
Wealth A stock of assets with monetary value owned at a point in time, such as property, land, savings and shares.
Lorenz curve A graph plotting the cumulative percentage of income (or wealth) against the cumulative percentage of the population; the further it lies from the 45° line of equality, the greater the inequality.
Gini coefficient A measure of inequality equal to the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve (A) divided by the total area under the line of equality (A + B); it ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality).
Quintile One-fifth (20%) of the population ranked by income; quintile shares are used to construct Lorenz curves.
Absolute poverty A situation in which income is too low to meet the basic needs for survival, such as food, clean water, shelter and clothing.
Relative poverty A situation in which income is below the level needed for the average standard of living in a particular society, often set at a percentage (e.g. 50% or 60%) of median income.
Poverty trap A self-reinforcing cycle in which low income leads to low saving, investment and productivity, which keeps incomes low.
International poverty line The World Bank's minimum income threshold for survival, $1.90 a day at 2011 PPP (updated to $2.15 at 2017 PPP in 2022), used to count extreme poverty across countries.
Minimum income standard (MIS) The lowest income that members of the public judge necessary for a socially acceptable standard of living in their country.
Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) A composite indicator that measures deprivation in health, education and living standards using ten weighted indicators; a person deprived in at least one-third of them is counted as multidimensionally poor.
Human capital The accumulated skills, knowledge, experience and health of the workforce, which raise labour productivity.
Progressive tax A tax in which the average tax rate rises as income rises, so higher-income earners pay a larger proportion of their income.
Regressive tax A tax in which the average tax rate falls as income rises, so it takes a larger proportion of income from low earners.
Proportional tax A tax in which the average tax rate is constant at all income levels (a flat rate).
Marginal tax rate (MTR) HLThe proportion of an extra dollar of income paid in tax: ΔT ÷ ΔY.
Average tax rate (ATR) HLTotal tax paid as a proportion of total income: T ÷ Y.
Direct tax A tax levied on income, profits or wealth and paid directly to the government by the person or firm liable, e.g. personal income tax, corporation tax, wealth tax.
Indirect tax A tax on expenditure on goods and services, such as VAT/GST, excise duties and customs duties; the seller pays it to the government but part is passed on to consumers.
Transfer payment A payment from the government to individuals, such as unemployment benefits or pensions, for which no good or service is produced in return.
Conditional cash transfer (CCT) A cash payment to low-income households on condition that they meet requirements such as children's school attendance or health check-ups.
Universal basic income (UBI) A regular, unconditional cash payment made by the government to every individual regardless of income or employment.
Minimum wage The lowest legal rate of pay per time period that an employer may pay a worker.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Equity

South Africa: the world's most unequal large economy

South Africa's Gini coefficient is about 0.63 (Table 23.3, p. 331). The top 10% own 86% of wealth and the bottom 90% only 14% (p. 331). Unemployment has exceeded 30% in recent years, with youth unemployment above 50%. Apartheid-era barriers in land ownership, education and location of jobs still affect opportunities.

Ask the class
• Which causes of inequality are most relevant?
• How does inequality affect growth and social stability?
• Which two policies would you prioritise?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Causes include unequal resource ownership (land, capital), unequal human capital (poor schooling in townships), historic racial discrimination and high structural unemployment.
  2. The Lorenz curve lies far from the line of equality; the bottom 40% of the population receives a very small share of income.
  3. High inequality lowers growth by wasting human potential and reducing aggregate demand from low-income households, and raises crime and social tension (Table 23.8, p. 349).
  4. Policies: investment in education and skills (shifts LRAS right and raises low earners' productivity), expanded social grants (transfer payments). Evaluation: fiscal space is limited by high public debt; effects of education take years.
DiagramLorenz curve with the South African curve far from the 45° line; LRAS1 to LRAS2 for human-capital investment (Fig 23.9).
Scenario 2 · Economic well-being

Brazil: Bolsa Família

Brazil's Bolsa Família programme pays monthly cash to low-income families on condition that children attend school and receive vaccinations. It has reached around 14 million households at a cost of roughly 0.5% of GDP. Brazil's Gini fell from about 0.59 in 2001 to about 0.53 by 2015 (World Bank).

Ask the class
• Why is Bolsa Família a CCT rather than a UBI?
• How can it reduce both poverty and future inequality?
• What are its limitations?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. It is targeted at low-income families and conditional on health and education, so it is a conditional cash transfer (p. 360).
  2. In the short run it raises the incomes of the poorest households, reducing absolute poverty and moving the Lorenz curve towards the line of equality.
  3. In the long run school attendance and better health raise human capital, helping to break the poverty trap.
  4. Limitations: administrative costs, targeting errors, possible disincentives to formal work, and the need for good-quality schools for the conditions to work.
DiagramLorenz curve shifting inwards; poverty-trap cycle broken at the human capital stage.
Scenario 3 · Intervention

Tax reform in Country Z (HL)

Before reform, income quintile shares in Country Z were 4%, 9%, 15%, 22% and 50%. The government introduced a higher top rate of income tax and a new child benefit. After reform the shares were 6%, 11%, 16%, 22% and 45%.

Ask the class
• Construct Lorenz curves before and after.
• What happened to the share of the bottom 40%?
• Estimate the Gini before and after.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Cumulative shares before: 4, 13, 28, 50, 100. After: 6, 17, 33, 55, 100.
  2. The bottom 40% rose from 13% to 17% of income, so the new Lorenz curve lies closer to the line of equality.
  3. Area B before = 20 × [(0+4)/2 + (4+13)/2 + (13+28)/2 + (28+50)/2 + (50+100)/2] = 2,900, so Gini = (5,000 − 2,900) ÷ 5,000 = 0.42.
  4. Area B after = 20 × [3 + 11.5 + 25 + 44 + 77.5] = 3,220, so Gini = 1,780 ÷ 5,000 ≈ 0.36. Inequality has fallen because progressive taxes and transfers redistribute income.
DiagramOne diagram with the line of equality, Lorenz curve 'before' and Lorenz curve 'after' (closer to the 45° line); axes cumulative % of population and cumulative % of income.
Scenario 4 · Economic well-being

Finland's basic income experiment

In 2017–18 Finland paid 2,000 randomly selected unemployed people €560 a month with no conditions, even if they found work. Employment rose only slightly compared with a control group, but recipients reported better well-being and less stress. The government did not extend the experiment.

Ask the class
• What is the argument for UBI?
• Why might it be costly?
• What does the experiment tell us?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. UBI removes the 'benefit trap' because people keep the payment when they start work, so the MTR on extra earnings falls.
  2. It is simple to administer and removes stigma, and it provides a safety net for people in insecure work.
  3. A full UBI paid to all adults is very expensive and would require much higher taxes, which could reduce incentives.
  4. Evaluation: the small employment effect and positive well-being effect suggest UBI may be better at reducing hardship than at raising employment; results from a two-year trial may not apply to a permanent scheme.
DiagramNo formal diagram required; a table of costs and benefits, or a Lorenz curve shift.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Lorenz curve (and changes in the distribution of income) SL/HL

Fig 23.3, p. 330

  1. Draw a square; label the horizontal axis 'Cumulative % of population' and the vertical axis 'Cumulative % of income', both from 0 to 100.
  2. Draw the 45° line of equality from (0,0) to (100,100).
  3. Draw the Lorenz curve bowing below the line, starting at (0,0) and ending at (100,100).
  4. To show rising inequality, draw a second curve further from the line; for falling inequality, closer to it.
Common errorsNot starting and ending the curve at the corners; labelling axes 'population' and 'income' without 'cumulative %'; drawing a curve that crosses above the line of equality.

Gini coefficient (areas A and B) SL/HL

Fig 23.4, p. 331

  1. Draw the Lorenz curve diagram.
  2. Shade area A between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
  3. Shade area B below the Lorenz curve.
  4. Write Gini = A ÷ (A + B).
Common errorsWriting Gini = A ÷ B; claiming the Gini compares income levels between countries (p. 332).

p. 331 (Top tip)

  1. Convert quintile shares into cumulative shares.
  2. Plot points at 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100% of the population, plus (0,0).
  3. Join the points with a smooth curve (or straight segments).
  4. Add the line of equality and label the curve with the country and year.

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Construct a Lorenz curve from quintile shares of 4%, 9%, 15%, 22% and 50%, and estimate the Gini coefficient. HL

SolutionCumulative shares: 4, 13, 28, 50, 100 at 20, 40, 60, 80, 100% of population. Area B (trapezia, width 20) = 20 × (2 + 8.5 + 20.5 + 39 + 75) = 2,900. Area under the line of equality = 100 × 100 ÷ 2 = 5,000 (p. 331). A = 5,000 − 2,900 = 2,100. Gini = 2,100 ÷ 5,000 = 0.42.

Problem 2. An individual earns $20,000 and has a tax-free allowance of $12,000. Income above this is taxed at 10%. Calculate the tax paid and ATR.

SolutionTaxable income = $20,000 − $12,000 = $8,000. Tax = $8,000 × 10% = $800 (p. 352). ATR = 800 ÷ 20,000 = 4%.

Problem 3. Using the US 2019–20 bands (10% up to $9,700; 12% to $39,475; 22% to $84,200), calculate total tax, ATR and MTR on $80,000. HL

SolutionTax = $970 + $3,573 + $8,915.50 = $13,458.50 (p. 352). ATR = 13,458.50 ÷ 80,000 = 16.8%. MTR = 22%. MTR > ATR, so the system is progressive.

Problem 4. Q23.3 (p. 354): At incomes $10,000/$15,000/$20,000/$25,000, Tax A = $1,000 each; Tax B = $1,000/$1,800/$3,000/$4,500; Tax C = $1,000/$1,500/$2,000/$2,500. Identify the progressive and proportional taxes. HL

SolutionATRs: A = 10%, 6.7%, 5%, 4% (regressive); B = 10%, 12%, 15%, 18% (progressive); C = 10% at every income (proportional).

Problem 5. Q23.4 (p. 354): A person pays $5,500 on $55,000; another pays $1,500 on $15,000. What type of tax system is this? HL

SolutionATR = 5,500 ÷ 55,000 = 10% and 1,500 ÷ 15,000 = 10%. The rate is constant, so the system is proportional.

Problem 6. Q23.5 (p. 354): 0% up to $10,000; 10% on $10,001–$40,000; 15% above $40,000. Calculate total tax on $75,000 and show that the ATR is 11%. HL

SolutionBand 1: $0. Band 2: $30,000 × 10% = $3,000. Band 3: $35,000 × 15% = $5,250. Total = $8,250. ATR = 8,250 ÷ 75,000 = 0.11 = 11%.

Problem 7. Q23.6 (p. 355): First $10,000 at 0%, next $20,000 at 10%, next $20,000 at 20%, thereafter 30%. For $40,000 find MTR, total tax and ATR; then ATR at $80,000. HL

Solution$40,000: MTR = 20%. Tax = 0 + $2,000 + ($10,000 × 20%) $2,000 = $4,000. ATR = 10%. $80,000: 0 + $2,000 + $4,000 + ($30,000 × 30%) $9,000 = $15,000; ATR = 15,000 ÷ 80,000 = 18.75%.

Problem 8. Q23.7 (p. 355): Flat 20% income tax with a $15,000 allowance. Calculate the ATR on $50,000. HL

SolutionTaxable income = $35,000; tax = $7,000; ATR = 7,000 ÷ 50,000 = 14%. The allowance makes a flat tax mildly progressive.

Problem 9. Q23.8 (p. 355): MTR is 45% and income rises from $47,000 to $55,000. Calculate the change in tax paid. HL

SolutionΔY = $8,000. ΔT = MTR × ΔY = 0.45 × 8,000 = $3,600.

Problem 10. Q23.9 (p. 355): 0% up to $9,000; 10% on $9,001–$20,000; 20% on $20,001–$30,000; 30% above $30,000. Calculate tax on $35,000. HL

Solution0 + ($11,000 × 10%) $1,100 + ($10,000 × 20%) $2,000 + ($5,000 × 30%) $1,500 = $4,600. (ATR = 13.1%.)

Problem 11. A consumer spends $400 on goods before tax; VAT is 20%. How much VAT is paid? A second consumer spends $345 including 15% GST. How much GST is paid? HL

SolutionTax-exclusive: $400 × 0.20 = $80 (total paid $480). Tax-inclusive: tax = 345 × 15 ÷ 115 = $45 (pre-tax value $300). Always check whether the expenditure figure includes the tax.

Problem 12. Household X earns $15,000 and spends $12,000 on goods subject to 20% VAT (tax-exclusive). Household Y earns $90,000 and spends $40,000 on such goods. Calculate VAT paid as a percentage of income. HL

SolutionX pays $2,400 = 16% of income. Y pays $8,000 = 8.9% of income. The richer household pays more in dollars but a smaller share of income, so the VAT is regressive.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Equity and equality mean the same thing – equality is equal shares; equity is fairness, which may justify some inequality.
  • A progressive tax simply means richer people pay more tax – that is also true of a proportional tax; progressive means the tax rate rises with income (p. 352).
  • A low Gini coefficient means a country is rich – the Gini measures distribution, not the level of income (p. 332).
  • Poverty only exists in low-income countries – relative poverty exists everywhere, and even the USA had about 15% of people below its poverty line (pp. 333–334).
  • Moving into a higher tax band means all income is taxed at the higher rate – only income within that band is taxed at the higher MTR.
  • Income and wealth are the same – income is a flow over time; wealth is a stock of assets at a point in time.
  • Indirect taxes are fair because everyone pays the same rate – because low earners spend a larger share of income, indirect taxes are regressive with respect to income.
  • Raising the minimum wage always reduces poverty – it may cause unemployment for some low-skilled workers and does not help those without jobs.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 354: the text defines the marginal tax rate as the 'change in tax rate paid' from a change in income; the correct definition is the change in tax paid divided by the change in income (ΔT ÷ ΔY), as the book's own formula shows.
  • p. 327 (Table 23.1): Mauritania's GDP per capita (PPP) is shown as $372, which is far below World Bank/IMF estimates (several thousand dollars); the '30,150 per cent' comparison with Qatar depends on this figure. Check current data on IMF DataMapper before using it.
  • p. 352: Denmark's 25% tax is a VAT (not a GST); Japan's consumption tax has been 10% since October 2019 (not 5%), and India's GST has several rates (0–28%), not a single 5% rate. Treat these figures as illustrative.
  • pp. 354–355: Q23.8 on marginal tax rates is not labelled 'HL only' although MTR/ATR content is HL only in the syllabus.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Distinguish equity from equality.

Equality means equal shares for all; equity means a fair distribution, which may include justified differences.

2. What is on the axes of a Lorenz curve?

Cumulative % of population (horizontal) and cumulative % of income or wealth (vertical).

3. What is the formula for the Gini coefficient?

Gini = A ÷ (A + B), where A is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve and B the area under the curve.

4. (HL) Quintile shares are 5, 10, 15, 25, 45. Give the cumulative shares.

5, 15, 30, 55, 100.

5. Distinguish absolute and relative poverty.

Absolute poverty is inability to meet basic survival needs; relative poverty is income well below the average living standard of a society.

6. Name the three dimensions of the MPI.

Health, education and living standards.

7. Give two difficulties in measuring poverty.

Informal economy and non-cash income are hard to record; price levels and needs differ between regions and household types.

8. Give three causes of inequality.

Unequal human capital, unequal ownership of resources (land, capital), and discrimination (also globalisation, technology, tax policy).

9. Why is a sales tax regressive?

Low-income households spend a larger proportion of their income, so the tax takes a larger share of their income.

10. (HL) Define MTR and ATR.

MTR = ΔT ÷ ΔY (tax on the last dollar); ATR = T ÷ Y (total tax as a share of income).

11. (HL) In a progressive system, which is higher: ATR or MTR?

The MTR is higher than the ATR (in a regressive system ATR > MTR).

12. (HL) VAT is 10% and a shopper spends $220 including VAT. How much VAT is paid?

220 × 10 ÷ 110 = $20.

13. What is a conditional cash transfer?

A cash payment to poor families conditional on actions such as school attendance or health check-ups.

14. Give one argument against a universal basic income.

It is very costly because it is paid to everyone, requiring higher taxes that may reduce work incentives.
