Key terms and definitions

Elasticity A measure of how responsive one variable is to a change in another variable.
Price elasticity of demand (PED) The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price: PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP (the negative sign is usually ignored).
Price elastic demand PED > 1: the percentage change in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage change in price.
Price inelastic demand 0 < PED < 1: the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price.
Unit (unitary) elastic demand PED = 1: the percentage change in quantity demanded equals the percentage change in price, so total revenue is unchanged; shown by a rectangular hyperbola.
Perfectly price elastic demand PED = ∞: any rise in price causes quantity demanded to fall to zero; shown by a horizontal demand curve.
Perfectly price inelastic demand PED = 0: quantity demanded does not change when price changes; shown by a vertical demand curve.
Determinants of PED Factors that affect how responsive demand is to price: number and closeness of substitutes, degree of necessity, proportion of income spent on the good, and time (TINS).
Total revenue (TR) The total amount a firm receives from sales: price × quantity sold.
PED and total revenue If demand is price inelastic, a price rise increases TR; if price elastic, a price cut increases TR; if unit elastic, TR is unchanged.
Price discrimination Charging different prices to different groups of consumers for the same product, where the price differences are not due to cost differences, based on their different PEDs.
Surge (dynamic) pricing Raising prices when demand peaks, exploiting lower PED at those times.
Indirect tax A tax on goods and services; governments prefer to tax goods with inelastic demand because revenue is high and quantity falls little.
Tax incidence How the burden of a tax is shared between consumers and producers; the more inelastic demand is, the greater the share borne by consumers.
PED along a linear demand curve HLAlthough the slope is constant, PED falls continuously moving down a straight-line demand curve: elastic above the midpoint, unit elastic at the midpoint and inelastic below it.
Primary commodities HLRaw materials and agricultural products (e.g. crude oil, iron ore, rice) that tend to have price inelastic demand because they are necessities or inputs with few substitutes.
Manufactured products HLGoods produced from raw materials in the secondary sector; they tend to have more substitutes and so more price elastic demand than primary commodities.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Intervention

Singapore's tobacco tax

Singapore raised excise duty on cigarettes by about 15% in 2023, taking a pack of 20 to around S$16. Estimates put the PED for cigarettes at about 0.4 in high-income countries. The government wants both to raise revenue and to cut smoking.

Ask the class
• Estimate the fall in quantity if the retail price rises 10%.
• What happens to total spending on cigarettes?
• Does the tax achieve both government goals?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. With PED = 0.4, a 10% price rise reduces quantity demanded by 0.4 × 10% = 4%.
  2. Demand is price inelastic, so total spending (TR) on cigarettes rises.
  3. Tax revenue rises substantially because quantity falls only a little; most of the burden falls on consumers (Hoang Fig 7.15).
  4. Evaluation: the revenue goal is met but the health goal only partly; addiction keeps PED low, so non-price measures (plain packaging, display bans, nudges from Chapter 6) are needed too. Young smokers may have a higher PED, so the tax may be more effective among them.
DiagramCigarette market with steep (inelastic) demand; supply shifts up by the tax from S to S + tax; show a small fall in Q, large rise in P, and consumer vs producer burden.
Scenario 2 · Choice

Netflix raises its prices

Netflix raised its standard US plan from $13.99 to $15.49 (about 11%) in 2022. In the following quarter it lost around 200,000 of roughly 75 million subscribers in the region, while rivals Disney+ and HBO Max offered cheaper plans.

Ask the class
• Calculate an approximate PED.
• Did total revenue rise or fall?
• What might change PED in the long run?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. %ΔQ ≈ −200,000 ÷ 75,000,000 × 100 = −0.27%; %ΔP ≈ +11%.
  2. PED ≈ 0.27 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.02, highly price inelastic in the short run (switching costs, habit).
  3. With inelastic demand, the price rise increased total revenue.
  4. Over time, as more close substitutes appear and consumers learn about them, PED is likely to rise (time and substitutes determinants); other factors (content releases) also changed, so ceteris paribus is doubtful.
DiagramInelastic demand TR diagram (Fig 7.12): shade revenue gained (price rise × new Q) vs revenue lost (fall in Q × old P); gain rectangle is larger.
Scenario 3 · Equity

Airline fares: business vs economy travellers

An airline flying London–New York charges business travellers booking three days ahead around £2,500 and leisure travellers booking three months ahead around £450 for similar seats. Business travel is often paid for by employers and has fixed dates.

Ask the class
• Why do the two groups have different PEDs?
• How does the airline use this?
• Is price discrimination good for consumers?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Business travellers have fewer substitutes (fixed meetings), a small share of their own income is involved (employer pays) and little time to search: demand is price inelastic.
  2. Leisure travellers can choose dates, destinations and airlines, and fares are a large share of income: demand is price elastic.
  3. The airline charges a high price to the inelastic group and a low price to the elastic group, raising total revenue from both.
  4. Leisure travellers may benefit from lower fares that fill seats, but business travellers lose consumer surplus; outcomes depend on the ability to prevent resale.
DiagramTwo demand diagrams side by side: steep D for business with high P; flat D for leisure with low P; shade TR in each.
Scenario 4 · Sustainability and change

(HL) Copper and Zambia's export earnings

Copper makes up around 70% of Zambia's export earnings. When the world copper price fell by about 25% between 2014 and 2015, Zambia's export revenues and government income fell sharply and the currency lost nearly half its value.

Ask the class
• Why is demand for copper price inelastic?
• Why do small supply or demand changes cause large price swings?
• What are the consequences for Zambia?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Copper is an input into wiring, construction and electronics with few substitutes and a small share of final product cost, so its PED is low (Hoang pp. 102–103).
  2. With steep demand (and inelastic supply, Chapter 9), a shift in either curve causes a large change in price.
  3. The 2014–15 slowdown in Chinese construction reduced demand; with inelastic demand the price fell sharply.
  4. Zambia's export earnings, tax revenue and exchange rate fall, showing the risks of commodity dependence; diversification into manufacturing (with higher PED and YED) is a common policy response.
DiagramCopper market with steep D and steep S; D shifts left from D1 to D2; large fall in price P1→P2 with small fall in quantity.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – PED & total revenue calculator

Students enter an old and new price and quantity to compute PED, then check against their hand calculation. Next they select a linear demand curve and drag the price from top to bottom, watching PED and TR update. They record the price at which TR peaks and relate it to PED = 1. Finally they compare a steep and a flat curve for the same price change.

Debrief
• Where is TR maximised on a linear demand curve?
• Why does a firm with inelastic demand have an incentive to raise its price?
• What stops a firm raising price indefinitely?

Course Simulator – Tax/subsidy incidence

Students impose a $1 per-unit tax on markets with PED of 0.2, 1 and 3. For each, they record the change in price paid by consumers, the fall in quantity, tax revenue and the shares of the burden. They then write a one-paragraph recommendation to a finance minister on which good to tax to raise revenue and which to tax to reduce consumption.

Debrief
• Who bears most of the tax when demand is inelastic?
• Why is taxing inelastic goods good for revenue but weak at changing behaviour?

Class demand survey in Google Sheets

Students anonymously state how many cinema trips, coffees or streaming subscriptions they would buy per month at five price points. The teacher aggregates the class data into a demand schedule in Google Sheets. Students calculate PED between each pair of prices and compare products. They explain the differences using TINS.

Debrief
• Which product had the most elastic demand and why?
• How reliable is survey-based PED?

IMF DataMapper / World Bank commodity prices (HL) · https://www.imf.org/external/datamapper

HL pairs download ten years of price data for one primary commodity (oil, copper or cocoa) and one manufactured-goods price index. They chart both in Google Sheets and calculate the largest year-on-year change for each. They explain the difference in volatility using PED of primary vs manufactured goods.

Worked calculations

Problem 1. A supply shift raises the price of safety matches by 50% and quantity demanded falls by 10% (Hoang p. 99). Calculate PED.

SolutionPED = 10% ÷ 50% = 0.2. Demand is price inelastic.

Problem 2. Theatre tickets rise from $50 to $60 and sales fall from 5,000 to 4,500 (p. 100). Calculate PED.

Solution%ΔP = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = +20%. %ΔQ = (4,500 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = −10%. PED = −10 ÷ 20 = −0.5, i.e. 0.5: price inelastic (not 2.0, which puts %ΔP on top).

Problem 3. Sunglasses fall in price from $300 to $250 and sales rise from 85 to 95 (practice 7.2, p. 95). Calculate and comment on PED.

Solution%ΔP = −50 ÷ 300 × 100 = −16.67%. %ΔQ = 10 ÷ 85 × 100 = +11.76%. PED = 11.76 ÷ 16.67 = 0.71: price inelastic, so the price cut reduced total revenue (from $25,500 to $23,750).

Problem 4. Earrings fall from $400 to $350 and sales rise from 25 to 30 (practice 7.3a). Calculate PED.

Solution%ΔP = −50 ÷ 400 × 100 = −12.5%. %ΔQ = 5 ÷ 25 × 100 = +20%. PED = 20 ÷ 12.5 = 1.6: price elastic; TR rises from $10,000 to $10,500.

Problem 5. PED is −3.0 for a product selling 200 units at $20. The price rises to $22 (practice 7.7). What happens to quantity and total revenue?

Solution%ΔP = +10%. %ΔQ = −3 × 10% = −30%, so Q falls to 140 units. TR falls from $20 × 200 = $4,000 to $22 × 140 = $3,080, a fall of $920.

Problem 6. Juke Ltd sells 200 units at $4 with PED of −2 and raises price by 20% (practice 7.8). Calculate original and new revenue and judge the decision.

SolutionOriginal TR = $4 × 200 = $800. New price = $4.80; %ΔQ = −2 × 20% = −40%, so Q = 120. New TR = $4.80 × 120 = $576. TR falls by $224, so it was a poor decision because demand is price elastic.

Problem 7. (HL) Demand is P = 10 − Q. Calculate point PED at P = 8, P = 5 and P = 2. HL

SolutionPoint PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) and ΔQ/ΔP = 1. At P = 8, Q = 2: PED = 8/2 = 4 (elastic). At P = 5, Q = 5: PED = 1 (unit). At P = 2, Q = 8: PED = 0.25 (inelastic). PED falls down the line although the slope is constant.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • 'Price elastic' means 'price up, demand down'. That is the law of demand; elastic means the percentage change in quantity demanded exceeds the percentage change in price (p. 94).
  • Putting %ΔP on top of the formula. %ΔQd goes on top (p. 100).
  • A price cut always increases revenue. It does so only if demand is price elastic (p. 100).
  • Saying 'elastic' or 'inelastic' without 'price'. Always write 'price elastic' or 'price inelastic', especially once YED is introduced (p. 97).
  • A straight-line demand curve has constant PED. Slope is constant but PED changes along it (HL, p. 97).
  • A steep curve is always inelastic. Steepness depends on the axis scale; only percentage changes determine PED.
  • Broad and narrow categories have the same PED. 'Food' is more price inelastic than 'salmon' (Top tip, p. 97).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 100: the Common mistake worked example on theatre tickets prints the %ΔQ as '(4,500 ÷ 5,000)'; it should be (4,500 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = −10%.
  • p. 95: practice question 7.3 shows (a) the earrings PED calculation as 10 marks and (b) the significance of PED for firms and government as 4 marks; the marks appear to be swapped.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Write the PED formula.

PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP.

2. Price rises 10%, Qd falls 25%. PED and type?

2.5: price elastic.

3. Draw perfectly price inelastic demand.

A vertical line at a fixed quantity; PED = 0.

4. What does TINS stand for?

Time, Income share, Necessity, Substitutes.

5. Which is the most important determinant of PED?

The number and closeness of substitutes.

6. Why is demand more elastic in the long run?

Consumers have time to find and switch to substitutes.

7. Demand is inelastic. Should a firm raise or cut price to increase TR?

Raise price.

8. What happens to TR when PED = 1 and price changes?

TR stays the same.

9. Why do governments tax goods with inelastic demand?

Quantity falls little, so tax revenue is high and stable.

10. What is price discrimination?

Charging different groups different prices for the same product based on their different PEDs.

11. PED −2, price rises 5%. Change in Qd?

Falls by 10%.

12. (HL) Where on a linear demand curve is PED = 1?

At the midpoint, where TR is maximised.

13. (HL) Give two reasons why primary commodities have low PED.

They are necessities or essential inputs with few substitutes, and they are a small share of the final product's price.
