Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 33

Sustainable development

Syllabus 4.7 Sustainable developmentTextbook pp. 527–541Sessions 2 HL · 2 SLConcepts sustainability, economic well-being, equity, change, scarcity

Why is economic development uneven – and can the world keep growing without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs?

Common SL/HL: the meaning of sustainable development (Brundtland definition, three pillars) and the Sustainable Development Goals (pp. 527–535). HL only: the relationship between sustainability and poverty – SDG1, over-reliance on the primary sector, access to education and the gender gap (pp. 535–540). HL items are marked (HL) inside session 2; SL students use that time for an extended SDG case study.

Session plans

Session 1: What is sustainable development? The three pillars and the road to the SDGs SL/HL pp. 527–535

Objectives
  • Students will be able to state and explain the Brundtland definition of sustainable development.
  • Students will be able to explain the social, economic and environmental pillars of sustainability with examples.
  • Students will be able to outline the move from the MDGs to the 17 SDGs and explain why the goals are interdependent.

Key terms and definitions

Sustainable development Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987).
Brundtland Report The 1987 UN World Commission on Environment and Development report 'Our Common Future', which defined sustainable development around the concepts of needs and limitations.
Renewable resources Natural resources that can regenerate over time, such as forests, fish stocks and solar energy, if they are used at a rate no faster than they are replenished.
Non-renewable resources Natural resources such as fossil fuels and minerals that are finite and cannot be replaced once used up.
Three pillars of sustainability The social, economic and environmental dimensions that must all be maintained for development to be sustainable.
Social sustainability The ability of social structures and processes to support the well-being of current communities while allowing future communities to live well.
Economic sustainability Using scarce resources efficiently to support long-term economic growth without disadvantaging future generations.
Environmental sustainability Using natural resources responsibly so that ecosystems and resource stocks remain available for future use.
Tragedy of the commons The tendency for common-access resources to be over-used and depleted when individuals act in their own self-interest without regulation.
Polluter-pays principle The principle, proposed by the OECD in the 1970s, that those who cause pollution should bear the costs of managing it.
Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) Eight UN goals set in 2000 to tackle poverty, hunger, disease, illiteracy, environmental damage and discrimination against women by 2015.
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Seventeen interlinked goals with 169 targets, adopted by all UN member states in 2015, to end poverty, protect the planet and promote prosperity by 2030.
International poverty line The World Bank's threshold of income per person per day (US$1.90 at 2011 PPP in the textbook) below which a person is counted as living in extreme poverty.
Extreme poverty Living below the international poverty line, unable to meet basic needs such as food, shelter and clean water.
Primary sector HLThe part of the economy concerned with extracting and harvesting natural resources, such as agriculture, forestry, fishing and mining.
Gender gap HLDifferences between women and men in opportunities, attitudes, participation and outcomes in social, political, cultural and economic life.
Social protection HLPolicies and programmes, such as pensions, unemployment benefits and cash transfers, designed to reduce poverty and vulnerability (SDG target 1.3).
Microfinance HLThe provision of small loans and other financial services to people on low incomes who lack access to conventional banking (SDG target 1.4).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Sustainability

Amazon deforestation: growth now or later?

Brazil's forest area has fallen steadily as land is cleared for cattle ranching and soya, while Bhutan's forest area has grown and its constitution requires 60% forest cover (Hoang case study, p. 532). Clearing a hectare of rainforest may earn a rancher a few hundred dollars a year, but the forest provides carbon storage, rainfall regulation and biodiversity that are not priced in the market.

Ask the class
• Is Brazil's use of the Amazon sustainable?
• Use the three pillars to analyse the trade-off.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Economic pillar – short-run gains in income, exports and jobs in agriculture.
  2. Environmental pillar – loss of a renewable resource faster than it regenerates, higher CO2 emissions and biodiversity loss; this is a tragedy of the commons with negative externalities.
  3. Social pillar – displacement of indigenous communities and threats to future livelihoods dependent on rainfall.
  4. Judgement – growth that runs down natural capital fails the Brundtland test because future generations inherit less; policies include enforcement, payments for ecosystem services and international funding (e.g. the Amazon Fund).
DiagramNegative externality of production diagram (MSC above MPC, welfare loss triangle) from Unit 2, or a PPC for the long run shifting inward as natural capital is depleted.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Rwanda: growth, poverty and gender (EPQ 33.1)

Rwanda has about 12.5 million people. Its poverty rate fell from 59% to 39% between 2001 and 2014, and its Gini coefficient fell from 0.52 to 0.43 between 2006 and 2017 (Hoang p. 540). Women hold a majority of parliamentary seats, yet many rural women work in subsistence farming with limited access to credit.

Ask the class
• Explain how lower gender inequality aids sustainable development.
• Discuss methods the government could use to achieve sustainable development.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Gender equality increases labour force participation and productivity, raising income without depleting resources (economic pillar).
  2. Educated women tend to have fewer, healthier children and invest more in their children's education, reducing future poverty (social pillar) (HL link to education).
  3. Methods – investment in education and health, microfinance for rural women, diversification away from rain-fed agriculture, green infrastructure, land rights.
  4. Evaluate each: cost and time lags, dependence on aid, need for good governance, and the risk that growth concentrated in Kigali widens regional inequality.
DiagramPPC shifting outward as human capital rises; optionally the poverty cycle diagram (Ch35) broken at the education link.
Scenario 3 · Sustainability

Zambia and copper: primary dependence and poverty (HL)

Zambia has one of the highest poverty rates in Table 33.2 (57.5% below $1.90, p. 536), yet copper makes up around 70% of its export earnings. When world copper prices fall, government revenue and foreign exchange fall sharply. Mining creates few jobs relative to its output, and most rural households rely on small-scale farming.

Ask the class
• (HL) Explain the link between primary-sector dependence and persistent poverty.
• How does this threaten sustainability?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Price volatility – commodity prices swing, so export earnings and tax revenue are unstable, making long-term spending on health and education difficult.
  2. Low income elasticity of demand for many primary products and limited value added mean income grows slowly compared with manufactured goods.
  3. Mining is capital intensive, so the benefits are concentrated; rural poverty persists, and poor farmers may over-use land (environmental pillar).
  4. Non-renewable resources run out, so without investing resource rents in human and physical capital, future generations are left poorer – a failure of economic sustainability.
  5. Policy – diversification, sovereign wealth fund, investment in education (SDG4) and agriculture productivity.
DiagramDemand and supply for copper: a fall in world demand shifts D left, lowering price and export revenue; link to the current account (Ch32).
Scenario 4 · Economic well-being

Ping An's poverty-alleviation insurance (HL)

Chinese insurer Ping An presented rural poverty-alleviation insurance schemes at the World Economic Forum in Davos in 2020 (Hoang case study, p. 540). The schemes aim to protect farmers against crop failure and illness, which often push households back into poverty.

Ask the class
• How can insurance help achieve SDG1?
• What are the limitations of private-sector solutions?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. SDG target 1.5 aims to build the resilience of the poor to shocks; insurance smooths income after crop failure or illness.
  2. This protects savings and assets, so households do not sell livestock or pull children out of school.
  3. Limitations – premiums may be unaffordable for the poorest, moral hazard and information problems exist, and firms are profit-motivated, so coverage may be uneven.
  4. Judgement – works best alongside government social protection (target 1.3).
DiagramNo formal diagram; a poverty cycle diagram showing insurance protecting the savings and investment links.

Simulation and data activities

Fishing (common-pool) game

Groups of four share a lake of 20 fish that regrows by 50% of the remaining stock after each round. Players write down how many fish they take; the teacher collects slips, subtracts the catch and adds regrowth. After five rounds, compare total catch and remaining stock across groups. In a second game, allow groups to agree a quota or appoint a regulator and compare results.

Debrief
• Why did the stock collapse in some groups?
• Which pillar of sustainability did the quota protect, and at what short-run cost?
• How does this relate to overfishing and SDG14?

Veconlab common-pool resource experiment · veconlab.econ.virginia.edu

Run the common-pool resource game on laptops with students as fishers or water users. Start with no communication, then allow chat or a fee on extraction. Display the class results and compare efficiency across treatments.

Debrief
• How close did the class get to the sustainable level of extraction?
• Which intervention worked best and why?

Sustainable Development Report dashboards / Our World in Data · dashboards.sdgindex.org

Pairs choose two countries and record traffic-light status and trends for five SDGs. They present one goal where progress is on track and one where it is stagnating, using a chart exported from Our World in Data.

Debrief
• Which SDGs show trade-offs between economic growth and environmental sustainability?
• Why might data be missing for the poorest countries?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Sustainable development means no growth – it means growth that does not reduce the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
  • Sustainability is only about the environment – the social and economic pillars matter equally.
  • The SDGs are separate targets – they are interdependent; progress on SDG1 supports SDG2 and SDG10 (CM, p. 535).
  • Economic growth automatically reduces poverty and inequality – growth can cut extreme poverty without improving gender equality or the environment (TT, p. 540).
  • Renewable resources cannot run out – they can be depleted if used faster than they regenerate (e.g. fish stocks, forests).
  • Poverty only matters in low-income countries – relative poverty and social exclusion also threaten social sustainability in rich countries.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Data now dated: the book uses the US$1.90 a day international poverty line (2011 PPP). The World Bank revised it to $2.15 (2017 PPP) in 2022 and to $3.00 (2021 PPP) in 2025, with the higher lines also revised. Tell students which line their data source uses.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. State the Brundtland definition of sustainable development.

Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

2. Name the three pillars of sustainability.

Social, economic and environmental.

3. Give one example of a renewable resource that is being used unsustainably.

Tropical rainforest in Brazil, or fish stocks in the North Atlantic, used faster than they regenerate.

4. How many SDGs are there and when are they due?

17 goals with 169 targets, to be achieved by 2030.

5. What is the tragedy of the commons?

Over-use of a common-access resource because individuals ignore the effect of their use on others and on the stock.

6. Give one difference between the MDGs and the SDGs.

The MDGs had 8 goals focused on developing countries to 2015; the SDGs have 17 goals applying to all countries to 2030 and include environmental goals.

7. Why are the SDGs interdependent?

Progress on one goal, e.g. ending poverty (SDG1), helps others such as zero hunger (SDG2) and reduced inequality (SDG10).

8. What is the international poverty line used in the textbook?

US$1.90 per person per day at PPP (since revised by the World Bank).

9. (HL) Why does dependence on the primary sector link to poverty?

Primary products have volatile prices and low value added, so earnings are unstable and low, while resources may be over-exploited.

10. (HL) How does access to education affect poverty and sustainability?

Education raises productivity and incomes and breaks the intergenerational poverty cycle; lack of access, e.g. Covid-19 school closures, deepens poverty.

11. (HL) Give one way closing the gender gap supports sustainable development.

More women in paid work raises output and household incomes; educated mothers invest more in children's health and education.

12. Give one reason why growth in China is not fully sustainable.

It has been accompanied by heavy air pollution and rising inequality despite large falls in poverty.
