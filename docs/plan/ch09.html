Key terms and definitions

Price elasticity of supply (PES) The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's price: PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP; it is positive because of the law of supply.
Price elastic supply PES > 1: the percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price.
Price inelastic supply 0 < PES < 1: the percentage change in quantity supplied is smaller than the percentage change in price.
Perfectly price inelastic supply PES = 0: quantity supplied is fixed whatever the price; shown by a vertical supply curve (e.g. original artworks, seats in a stadium).
Perfectly price elastic supply PES = ∞: producers will supply any quantity at the given price but none below it; shown by a horizontal supply curve.
Unit elastic supply PES = 1: the percentage change in quantity supplied equals the percentage change in price; any straight-line supply curve through the origin.
Determinants of PES Time, ability to store (inventory), rate at which costs increase, unused capacity and mobility (substitution) of factors of production (TICCS).
Time period Supply is more elastic in the long run, when all factors are variable and new firms can enter, than in the short run.
Mobility of factors of production How easily and cheaply factors can be moved between uses or places; greater mobility makes supply more elastic.
Unused (spare) capacity Idle resources such as unused machinery or workers; the more spare capacity, the more elastic supply is.
Ability to store Firms that can hold stocks (inventories) can release them quickly when price rises, so supply is more elastic.
Rate at which costs increase If marginal cost rises slowly as output expands, firms can increase output without large price rises, so supply is elastic; if MC rises quickly, supply is inelastic.
Geographical and occupational immobility Barriers to moving labour between regions or between jobs and skills, which reduce PES.
Primary commodities (supply) HLAgricultural and mined products whose supply is generally price inelastic because of long production periods, perishability, specialised land and capital, and dependence on weather.
Manufactured products (supply) HLGoods produced in factories with flexible capital, storable output and shorter production times, so supply is generally more price elastic than for primary commodities.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Scarcity

The 2023–24 cocoa price spike

Cocoa prices rose from about $2,500 per tonne in early 2023 to over $10,000 in April 2024 after disease and poor weather cut harvests in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which grow around 60% of the world's cocoa. New cocoa trees take three to five years to produce beans.

Ask the class
• Why did supply not respond to the price rise?
• Show the market on a diagram.
• (HL) Why is cocoa supply less elastic than chocolate bar supply?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Time: farmers cannot increase output quickly because new trees take years to mature.
  2. Ability to store is limited for farmers, and there is little spare capacity (land and trees are already in use).
  3. With very inelastic supply, a leftward shift of supply and steady demand produce a very large price rise.
  4. (HL) Chocolate makers can use stocks, adjust recipes and run factories longer, so their supply is more elastic than that of raw cocoa (Hoang pp. 119–120).
DiagramCocoa market with steep (inelastic) supply; S1 shifts left to S2; demand relatively inelastic; price rises sharply from P1 to P2 with a small fall in quantity.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Housing in Vancouver

Average house prices in Vancouver roughly doubled between 2012 and 2022. Building a new apartment tower takes three to five years from planning to completion, and zoning rules limit land available for dense housing.

Ask the class
• Is housing supply price elastic or inelastic?
• Explain using two determinants.
• What could government do to raise PES?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Housing supply is price inelastic: large price rises lead to only small increases in completed homes in the short run.
  2. Time: construction takes years. Mobility of factors: land is fixed and zoned, and skilled construction labour is limited.
  3. With inelastic supply, rising demand (population growth, low interest rates) shows up mainly as higher prices rather than more homes.
  4. Government can raise PES by relaxing zoning, speeding up planning approvals and training construction workers.
DiagramHousing market with steep S; D1 shifts right to D2; large price rise P1 → P2, small quantity rise Q1 → Q2.
Scenario 3 · Efficiency

Angry Birds and digital goods

Rovio's Angry Birds sold for $0.99 per download and passed 2 billion downloads (Hoang p. 118). Each extra copy costs almost nothing to supply, and the firm never needed to raise the price when demand surged.

Ask the class
• What is the PES of the app?
• Why does rising demand not raise the price?
• Show on a diagram.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Once the app is written, the marginal cost of an extra download is close to zero and constant.
  2. The firm can supply any quantity at $0.99 without extra costs rising, so supply is perfectly price elastic (PES = ∞).
  3. A rightward shift of demand increases quantity but leaves price unchanged.
  4. The same applies to many digital products (e-books, streaming), explaining why their prices rarely respond to demand surges.
DiagramHorizontal supply S at P = $0.99; D1 shifts right to D2; quantity rises from Q1 to Q2 at the same price.
Scenario 4 · Change

Face masks in 2020

When COVID-19 spread in early 2020, demand for surgical masks rose by more than ten times. In China, manufacturers such as BYD and SAIC-GM-Wuling converted car and battery production lines to make masks, and China's daily mask output rose from about 20 million to over 100 million within a month.

Ask the class
• Which determinants explain the rapid supply response?
• How did PES change between January and March 2020?
• Why could mask prices fall again by mid-2020?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. In the very short run, supply was inelastic: existing capacity was fully used and stocks ran out, so prices spiked.
  2. Mobility of factors allowed firms to switch machinery and workers from other products, and spare capacity in idle car plants was used.
  3. As a result PES rose sharply within weeks: supply became much more elastic.
  4. With much more elastic supply and more producers, supply shifted right and prices fell back by mid-2020.
DiagramTwo supply curves: steep short-run S (January) and flatter S (March); demand shifts right; compare the price rise under each.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Relatively price elastic and relatively price inelastic supply SL/HL

Fig 9.2 and Fig 9.4, p. 113

  1. Label axes Price (P) and Quantity supplied (Q).
  2. Elastic: draw a relatively flat straight-line supply curve that cuts the price (vertical) axis; show a small %ΔP and large %ΔQ.
  3. Inelastic: draw a relatively steep straight-line supply curve that cuts the quantity (horizontal) axis; show a large %ΔP and small %ΔQ.
  4. Label PES values if data are given (e.g. 10 and 0.25).
Common errorsDrawing curves through the origin (this gives PES = 1); getting the axis-intercept rule backwards.

Simulation and data activities

Supply-response game (cocoa farm vs phone-case factory)

Divide the class into farms and factories. Each round the teacher announces a new market price. Factories may increase output immediately by adding a shift or releasing stock; farms must plant in one round and harvest two rounds later, and cannot store output. Groups record price and quantity each round, then calculate PES for the short run and after four rounds. Compare results on the board.

Debrief
• Which group had more elastic supply and why?
• How did PES change over time?
• Which TICCS determinants did the rules represent?

Course Simulator – Supply & demand shifter

Students set up two markets with the same demand shift but different supply slopes (steep and flat). They record the change in price and quantity for each and explain the difference. Then they set a vertical supply curve and a horizontal one and repeat the shift. Finally they write one sentence per case linking the outcome to PES.

Debrief
• Why does a demand shift raise price most when supply is inelastic?
• When does a demand increase change only quantity?

Trading Economics / FRED commodity price charts · https://tradingeconomics.com/commodities

Pairs pick one primary commodity (cocoa, coffee, wheat, copper) and one manufactured product index. They chart five years of prices and identify the largest price swings. Using TICCS they explain why the primary commodity's prices are more volatile. HL students link this to low PES for primary commodities.

Debrief
• Which determinant best explains the commodity's inelastic supply?
• How do producers try to make supply more elastic?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Price rises by 15% and quantity supplied rises by 150% (Hoang Fig 9.2). Calculate PES.

SolutionPES = 150% ÷ 15% = 10: price elastic supply.

Problem 2. Price rises by 40% and quantity supplied rises by 10% (Fig 9.4). Calculate PES.

SolutionPES = 10% ÷ 40% = 0.25: price inelastic supply.

Problem 3. PES is +3.0 and price falls from $13 to $11.70 with 5,000 units supplied initially (practice 9.1). Calculate the new quantity supplied.

Solution%ΔP = −1.30 ÷ 13 × 100 = −10%. %ΔQs = 3.0 × (−10%) = −30%. New Qs = 5,000 × 0.7 = 3,500 units.

Problem 4. Price rises from $25 to $30 and quantity supplied from 20,000 to 50,000 (Fig 9.3, practice 9.2). Calculate PES.

Solution%ΔP = 5 ÷ 25 × 100 = +20%. %ΔQs = 30,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = +150%. PES = 150 ÷ 20 = 7.5: price elastic.

Problem 5. The price of beans rises from $2.00 to $2.20 and quantity supplied from 10,000 to 10,500 (practice 9.3). Calculate PES.

Solution%ΔP = +10%; %ΔQs = +5%. PES = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5: price inelastic.

Problem 6. Murtagh watches: price falls from $300 to $250 and quantity supplied falls from 95 to 85 (practice 9.4). Calculate PES to 2 d.p. and comment.

Solution%ΔP = −50 ÷ 300 × 100 = −16.67%. %ΔQs = −10 ÷ 95 × 100 = −10.53%. PES = 10.53 ÷ 16.67 = 0.63: price inelastic, possibly because skilled labour and specialised equipment cannot easily be switched.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • 'Price elastic supply' means 'price up, supply up'. That is the law of supply; elastic means the percentage change in quantity supplied exceeds the percentage change in price (p. 112).
  • PES can be negative. Because of the law of supply, PES is positive (except in special cases beyond the syllabus).
  • Only a 45° line through the origin has PES = 1. Any straight-line supply curve through the origin is unit elastic (Fig 9.7).
  • A linear supply curve has constant PES. PES varies along most linear supply curves, although describing a curve as relatively elastic or inelastic is acceptable (Top tip, p. 115).
  • Supply is less elastic in a recession. It is usually more elastic because firms have spare capacity (Top tip, p. 117).
  • Perfectly elastic supply means quantity supplied falls to zero when price changes. The correct definition is that firms will supply any quantity at the given price (the chapter summary on p. 120 is wrong).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 120: the chapter summary wrongly defines perfectly elastic supply as quantity supplied falling to zero after a price change. Use the body definition on p. 115: producers will supply any quantity at the given price (PES = ∞).
  • pp. 113–114: the Top tips on axis intercepts (elastic supply cuts the price/vertical axis; inelastic supply cuts the quantity/horizontal axis) may appear with the axis letters missing in some versions; check against the printed page before copying.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Write the PES formula.

PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP.

2. Price rises 20%, Qs rises 5%. PES and type?

0.25: price inelastic.

3. Draw perfectly inelastic supply and give an example.

A vertical line; e.g. seats in a stadium or an original painting.

4. What shape is a unit elastic supply curve?

Any straight line through the origin.

5. Which axis does a relatively elastic linear supply curve cut?

The price (vertical) axis.

6. What does TICCS stand for?

Time, Inventory (ability to store), Costs (rate of increase), Capacity (unused), Substitution (mobility) of factors.

7. Why is supply more elastic in the long run?

All factors become variable and new firms can enter the industry.

8. Why does spare capacity raise PES?

Firms can increase output quickly using idle machines and workers without large cost increases.

9. Why is canned meat supply more elastic than fresh fish supply?

Canned meat can be stored and released when price rises; fresh fish is perishable.

10. PES = 2 and price rises 4%. Change in Qs?

Rises by 8%.

11. Why did Angry Birds' price not rise with demand?

Supply was perfectly elastic: extra downloads cost almost nothing, so any quantity could be supplied at $0.99.

12. (HL) Give two reasons why primary commodities have low PES.

Long production periods (growing seasons, mine development) and limited ability to store perishable output; also specialised land and capital.
