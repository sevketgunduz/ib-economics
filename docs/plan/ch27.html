Syllabus 4.1 Benefits of international tradeTextbook pp. 420–435Sessions 4 HL · 2 SLConcepts interdependence, efficiency, choice, change, economic well-being

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? If trade makes the world richer overall, why do some workers, firms and even countries resist it?

Key terms and definitions

International trade The exchange of goods and services across national borders, made up of exports and imports.
Exports Domestically produced goods and services sold to buyers in other countries; they bring an inflow of foreign currency.
Imports Goods and services produced abroad and bought by domestic households, firms and the government; they cause an outflow of currency.
Free trade International trade without government-imposed barriers such as tariffs, quotas or subsidies. International trade in general may still include such barriers.
Factor endowment The quantity and quality of a country's factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise), which affects which goods it can produce at relatively low cost.
World price (Pw) The price at which a good is traded internationally. A small open economy is a price-taker, so world supply is perfectly elastic at Pw.
Foreign exchange earnings Foreign currency received from selling exports, which can be used to pay for imports.
Economies of scale Falls in long-run average cost as a firm increases its scale of output; access to world markets allows firms to reach these larger outputs.
Export revenue HLThe value of a country's exports of a good: world price multiplied by the quantity exported (Pw × (Qs – Qd)).
Import expenditure HLThe amount spent on imports of a good: world price multiplied by the quantity imported (Pw × (Qd – Qs)).
Autarky HLA situation in which a country is self-sufficient and does not trade internationally, so it can consume only what it produces.
Absolute advantage HLA country has an absolute advantage in a good if it can produce more of it than another country using the same resources, or the same output using fewer resources.
Comparative advantage HLA country has a comparative advantage in a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
Opportunity cost (in trade) HLThe quantity of the other good that must be given up to produce one more unit of a good; on a linear PPC it is constant and equals the ratio of the two maximum outputs.
Specialization HLConcentrating resources on producing the goods in which a country has a comparative advantage and trading for other goods, which raises total world output.
Terms of trade (rate of exchange of goods) HLIn the comparative advantage model, the rate at which one good is swapped for another. Both countries gain only if the rate lies between their two opportunity cost ratios.
Linear PPC HLA straight-line production possibilities curve, drawn on the assumption of constant opportunity cost, used to show gains from specialization and trade.
Gains from trade HLThe extra output and consumption that becomes possible when countries specialize according to comparative advantage and trade, shown by consumption points beyond each country's PPC.
Sources of comparative advantage (REFIT) HLResearch and development, Exchange rates, Factor endowments, Inflation (price stability) and Technology: the factors that give a country relatively low opportunity costs.
Dynamic comparative advantage HLThe idea that comparative advantage changes over time as technology, skills, investment and costs change, so today's pattern of specialization may not last.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being and equity – the gains from trade are not evenly shared.

Vietnam's rice exporters at a high world price

Vietnam is one of the world's largest rice exporters (p. 420). Suppose the domestic equilibrium price is 9,000 dong/kg, but the world price rises to 12,000 dong/kg after poor harvests in other Asian countries. At the higher price, Vietnamese farmers increase output from 40 to 46 million tonnes a year, while domestic consumption falls from 40 to 37 million tonnes.

Ask the class
• How much rice does Vietnam export at the new world price?
• Who gains and who loses inside Vietnam?
• Why might the Vietnamese government be tempted to limit exports?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Vietnam is a price-taker, so the world price acts as a horizontal world supply/demand line above the domestic equilibrium.
  2. At 12,000 dong, domestic Qs = 46m t and domestic Qd = 37m t. Exports = Qs – Qd = 9m tonnes of excess supply sold abroad.
  3. Farmers gain: they sell more at a higher price, so producer surplus and foreign exchange earnings rise. Domestic consumers lose consumer surplus because they pay more and buy less, which hurts low-income households the most as rice is a staple.
  4. Because producer gains are larger than consumer losses, social surplus rises. However, the distribution matters: governments sometimes restrict rice exports to protect food security, which shows that trade has winners and losers.
  5. Diagram: free trade with exports (Fig 27.5): Dd and Sd, Pe at 9,000, Pw at 12,000, Qd = 37 and Qs = 46, exports bracketed.
DiagramFree-trade exports diagram (Hoang Fig 27.5): Price (dong/kg) vs Quantity (m tonnes); Dd, Sd; autarky Pe/Qe; horizontal Pw above Pe; Qd and Qs read off the domestic curves; bracket Qs – Qd as exports; shade the lost consumer surplus and the gained producer surplus.
Scenario 2 · Choice – consumers gain access to goods their own economy cannot produce efficiently.

Norway imports tropical fruit

Scandinavian countries cannot grow bananas and pineapples cheaply (p. 427). Suppose Norwegian greenhouse growers could supply pineapples at an autarky price of NOK 80 each, but the world price is NOK 25. At NOK 25, Norwegians buy 6 million pineapples a year and domestic greenhouses supply only 0.5 million.

Ask the class
• How many pineapples are imported, and what is Norway's import expenditure?
• Which benefits of trade does this illustrate?
• Who in Norway is worse off?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The world price is below the domestic equilibrium, so domestic buyers can buy as many pineapples as they want at NOK 25.
  2. Imports = Qd – Qs = 6m – 0.5m = 5.5 million pineapples. Import expenditure = NOK 25 × 5.5m = NOK 137.5m.
  3. Benefits: lower prices, greater choice and more efficient resource allocation, because Norwegian land and labour can be used for goods such as seafood where Norway has lower opportunity costs.
  4. Losers: Norwegian greenhouse growers lose producer surplus and some may leave the industry; their workers may face structural unemployment.
  5. Diagram: free trade with imports (Fig 27.6).
DiagramFree-trade imports diagram (Hoang Fig 27.6): Pw below Pe; Qs (0.5m) and Qd (6m) read off the domestic curves; bracket imports Qd – Qs; HL: shade the import expenditure rectangle Pw × (Qd – Qs).
Scenario 3 · Interdependence and efficiency – even the less productive country gains from trade.

Ethiopia and Germany: coffee and machine tools (HL)

With the same resources, Ethiopia can produce either 100 tonnes of coffee or 10 machine tools; Germany can produce either 120 tonnes of coffee or 60 machine tools. Both economies have linear PPCs. Before trade each country splits its resources equally between the two goods.

Ask the class
• Which country has the absolute advantage in each good?
• Calculate the opportunity cost of one machine tool in each country and identify comparative advantage.
• Show that both countries gain if they trade at 1 machine tool = 5 tonnes of coffee.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Germany has an absolute advantage in both goods (120 > 100 coffee; 60 > 10 machine tools).
  2. Opportunity cost of 1 machine tool: Ethiopia 100/10 = 10 t coffee; Germany 120/60 = 2 t coffee. Opportunity cost of 1 t coffee: Ethiopia 0.1 tools; Germany 0.5 tools. Ethiopia has the comparative advantage in coffee; Germany in machine tools.
  3. Autarky (half resources each): Ethiopia 50 coffee + 5 tools; Germany 60 coffee + 30 tools; world total 110 coffee, 35 tools.
  4. Full specialization: Ethiopia 100 coffee, Germany 60 tools. World output rises to 100 coffee and 60 tools, so there are 25 more tools for 10 less coffee. To be sure world output of both rises, Germany can partly specialize (e.g. 20 coffee + 50 tools), giving totals of 120 coffee and 50 tools.
  5. Terms of 1 tool = 5 t coffee lie between 2 and 10, so both gain. If Ethiopia swaps 45 t coffee for 9 tools: Ethiopia consumes 55 coffee + 9 tools (it had 50 + 5); Germany (at 20 coffee + 50 tools) consumes 65 coffee + 41 tools (it had 60 + 30). Both consumption points lie outside their PPCs.
  6. Diagram: two linear PPCs on the same axes (Fig 27.13 style) with autarky points and post-trade consumption points outside each PPC.
DiagramLinear PPC gains-from-trade diagram (Hoang Figs 27.12–27.13): Machine tools on the x-axis, Coffee on the y-axis; Ethiopia PPC from (0,100) to (10,0); Germany PPC from (0,120) to (60,0); mark autarky points A and B on each PPC and post-trade consumption points C and D beyond them; label the specialization points.
Scenario 4 · Change – comparative advantage is not static.

Losing a comparative advantage: British steel (HL)

The UK was once a world leader in steel and coal, but lost its comparative advantage to lower-cost producers (p. 434). In 2019 China's Jingye bought British Steel for about £50m, promising £1.2bn of investment and to keep around 4,000 jobs in Scunthorpe (p. 434). Transport costs, energy prices and exchange rates all affect whether UK steel can compete.

Ask the class
• Which assumptions of the comparative advantage model does this case challenge?
• Why might the UK government care about keeping steel production even if the theory says it should import steel?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The model assumes comparative advantage is fixed; in reality it is dynamic. Rising wages and energy costs in the UK and investment in China changed relative opportunity costs.
  2. The model assumes perfect factor mobility; in practice steelworkers in Scunthorpe cannot move easily into service jobs in London, so specialization creates structural unemployment and regional decline.
  3. The model ignores transport costs and trade barriers; steel is heavy, so transport costs reduce the gains from importing.
  4. The model also ignores non-economic concerns: steel may be seen as a strategic industry (national security, Ch29).
  5. Judgement: the model still explains why the UK imports most of its steel, but the adjustment costs fall on specific communities, which is why governments intervene.
DiagramOptional: two linear PPCs showing the change over time – the UK's PPC for steel pivots inwards relative to China's, reversing the opportunity cost ranking.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Free trade: exports when world price is above domestic price SL/HL

Fig 27.5, p. 425 (built from Fig 27.4, p. 424)

  1. Label axes: Price (P) and Quantity (Q) of the good.
  2. Draw domestic demand (Dd) and domestic supply (Sd); mark the autarky equilibrium Pe, Qe.
  3. Draw a horizontal line at the world price Pw above Pe and label it Pw (world demand is perfectly elastic at this price for a small economy).
  4. Read off domestic quantity demanded Q1 (on Dd) and domestic quantity supplied Q2 (on Sd) at Pw.
  5. Bracket Q2 – Q1 and label it 'exports'.
  6. Optional: shade the loss of consumer surplus and the gain in producer surplus; the net gain is the triangle between the curves.
Common errorsDrawing the world price at the domestic equilibrium; reading exports as Q2 – Qe instead of Q2 – Q1; drawing a sloping world supply; forgetting that domestic consumers are worse off.

Free trade: imports when world price is below domestic price SL/HL

Fig 27.6, p. 425

  1. Label axes: Price (P) and Quantity (Q).
  2. Draw Dd and Sd and mark the autarky equilibrium Pe, Qe.
  3. Draw a horizontal world supply line (Sworld) at Pw below Pe.
  4. Read domestic Qs (Q1) off Sd and domestic Qd (Q2) off Dd at Pw.

Linear PPCs showing comparative advantage and gains from trade HL

Simulation and data activities

Classroom trading game (resource envelopes)

Give five groups envelopes with unequal resources (some have lots of paper but no scissors, some have rulers and glue only). Publish a price list for paper shapes of set sizes. Round 1 (6 minutes): no trading between groups; record the value produced. Round 2 (6 minutes): trading of resources and finished goods is allowed; record value again. Round 3 (optional): add a 'tariff' of one sheet per trade to preview Ch28.

Debrief
• Why did total output rise in round 2?
• Which groups specialized and why?
• Did every group gain equally? Who gained most?
• Which of the nine benefits of trade did you experience?

Course Simulator – PPC & comparative advantage

HL students enter the Australia/Brazil comparative advantage data (30T/15C and 40T/80C). They first find each country's opportunity cost of timber, then choose which good each country specializes in. They set the terms of trade to 1:1 and record the consumption points; then they try ratios of 1T:0.4C and 1T:2.5C and note which country refuses to trade. Finally, they enter their own data from homework.

Debrief
• What range of terms of trade makes both countries better off?
• Why does the country with the absolute advantage in both goods still gain?
• What happens to the gains if we add a transport cost?

Our World in Data / World Bank Open Data · https://ourworldindata.org/trade-and-globalization

Students plot trade openness (trade as % of GDP) for Singapore, the USA, Brazil and one sub-Saharan country from 1970 to the latest year. They then use World Bank Open Data to identify each country's main export category and link it to a REFIT source of comparative advantage. Each pair posts a one-sentence claim and one chart on a shared slide.

Debrief
• Why are small economies usually more open to trade?
• Which REFIT source best explains each country's export pattern?
• Does more openness always mean higher living standards?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Country Z plum oil (in the style of EPQ 27.2, p. 426). Domestic demand Qd = 500 – 50P and domestic supply Qs = 50P – 100 (Q in '000 litres, P in $). The world price is $7. Calculate (a) the autarky price and quantity, (b) the quantity of exports, (c) export revenue, (d) the value of domestic consumption, (e) social surplus under free trade. HL

Solution(a) 500 – 50P = 50P – 100 → P = $6, Q = 200,000 litres. (b) At $7: Qd = 150, Qs = 250, so exports = 250 – 150 = 100,000 litres. (c) Export revenue = $7 × 100,000 = $700,000. (d) Domestic consumption value = $7 × 150,000 = $1,050,000. (e) Demand price intercept = $10, supply price intercept = $2. CS = ½ × 150,000 × (10 – 7) = $225,000; PS = ½ × 250,000 × (7 – 2) = $625,000; social surplus = $850,000 (vs $800,000 in autarky, a gain of $50,000).

Problem 2. Using the same curves, suppose the world price falls to $3. Calculate the quantity of imports, import expenditure and domestic producer revenue. HL

SolutionAt $3: Qd = 500 – 150 = 350; Qs = 150 – 100 = 50 ('000 litres). Imports = 350 – 50 = 300,000 litres. Import expenditure = $3 × 300,000 = $900,000. Domestic producer revenue = $3 × 50,000 = $150,000.

Problem 3. Absolute advantage (Tables 27.1–27.4, pp. 427–428). Australia can produce 6 timber or 12 cattle; Brazil 10 timber or 5 cattle. In autarky Australia produces 3T + 6C and Brazil 4T + 3C. Show the gains from specialization and a 6-for-6 swap. HL

SolutionAustralia has the absolute advantage in cattle (12 > 5), Brazil in timber (10 > 6). Autarky world output = 7T + 9C. Specialization: Australia 12C, Brazil 10T, so world output = 10T + 12C (+3T, +3C). Swap 6T for 6C: Australia consumes 6T + 6C (was 3T + 6C); Brazil consumes 4T + 6C (was 4T + 3C). Both are better off.

Problem 4. Comparative advantage (Tables 27.5–27.7, pp. 429–430). Australia: 30 timber or 15 cattle; Brazil: 40 timber or 80 cattle. Calculate opportunity costs, identify comparative advantage and show the outcome of trading 25T for 25C. HL

SolutionOpportunity cost of 1T: Australia 15/30 = 0.5C; Brazil 80/40 = 2C. Opportunity cost of 1C: Australia 2T; Brazil 0.5T. Australia has the comparative advantage in timber, Brazil in cattle, even though Brazil has the absolute advantage in both. Specialize: Australia 30T, Brazil 80C. Trade 25T for 25C (terms 1:1, between 0.5 and 2): Australia consumes 5T + 25C; Brazil consumes 25T + 55C. Both points lie outside their PPCs.

Problem 5. EPQ 27.3 (p. 430): Country K can produce 8,000 fruits or 10,000 vegetables; Country P 4,000 fruits or 8,000 vegetables. (a) Who has the absolute advantage in vegetables? (b) Who should specialize in fruit? (c) Calculate the opportunity cost of 8 units of fruit for Country P. HL

Solution(a) Country K (10,000 > 8,000 vegetables). (b) Opportunity cost of 1 fruit: K = 10,000/8,000 = 1.25 vegetables; P = 8,000/4,000 = 2 vegetables. K has the lower opportunity cost, so K should specialize in fruit (and P in vegetables, where its opportunity cost is 0.5 fruit vs K's 0.8). (c) 8 × 2 = 16 vegetables.

Problem 6. Worked example (p. 433): Lucland can produce 1,500 Aris or 600 Walkers; Mayland 2,000 Aris or 500 Walkers. Which country should specialize in which good? HL

SolutionOpportunity cost of 1 Walker: Lucland 1,500/600 = 2.5 Aris; Mayland 2,000/500 = 4 Aris. Lucland has the lower opportunity cost in Walkers, so it specializes in Walkers. Opportunity cost of 1 Aris: Lucland 0.4 Walkers; Mayland 0.25 Walkers, so Mayland specializes in Aris. Any terms between 2.5 and 4 Aris per Walker benefit both.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • The benefits of international trade are the same as the benefits of free trade – trade can still happen with tariffs and quotas; free trade adds further efficiency gains (p. 422).
  • Trade makes everyone better off – social surplus rises, but import-competing producers and their workers lose, and consumers of exported goods pay more (p. 425).
  • A country with an absolute advantage in both goods cannot gain from trade – gains depend on differences in opportunity cost, not absolute productivity.
  • Comparative advantage is permanent – it changes with technology, wages, exchange rates and investment (p. 430).
  • Exports equal the whole of domestic output at the world price – exports are only the excess of domestic Qs over domestic Qd.
  • Any terms of trade will benefit both countries – the rate must lie between the two countries' opportunity cost ratios.
  • The opportunity cost of good X is found by dividing X's output by Y's – it is the other good's output divided by this good's output (the amount of Y given up per unit of X).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 420: the date of the Assyrian merchant colonies appears as '18BCE'; it should read the 18th/19th century BCE (about 1900–1800 BCE). Correct this for students.
  • p. 432 (Delta–Gamma worked example): the figures for each country are the outputs when resources are split equally between the two goods, not maximum outputs. Point this out before students compare them with the maximum-output tables on pp. 429–430, so they do not mix the two approaches.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Name four of the nine benefits of international trade.

Any four of: increased competition, lower prices, greater choice, acquisition of resources, foreign exchange earnings, access to larger markets, economies of scale, more efficient resource allocation, more efficient production.

2. Why does a small economy face a horizontal world supply curve?

It is a price-taker: its purchases are too small to affect the world price, so it can buy any quantity at Pw.

3. On the exports diagram, how is the quantity of exports found?

Domestic quantity supplied minus domestic quantity demanded at the world price (Qs – Qd).

4. Who loses when a country starts exporting a good at a world price above the domestic price?

Domestic consumers, who pay a higher price and buy less; consumer surplus falls.

5. (HL) Pw = $5, Qd = 40,000, Qs = 10,000. Calculate import expenditure.

Imports = 30,000; import expenditure = $5 × 30,000 = $150,000.

6. (HL) Define comparative advantage.

The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

7. (HL) Country A makes 20 cars or 40 computers; B makes 10 cars or 30 computers. Who has the comparative advantage in cars?

A: 1 car costs A 2 computers but costs B 3 computers.

8. (HL) What condition must the terms of trade meet for both countries to gain?

It must lie between the two countries' opportunity cost ratios.

9. (HL) What does REFIT stand for?

R&D, Exchange rates, Factor endowments, Inflation (price stability), Technology.

10. (HL) On a gains-from-trade PPC diagram, where are the post-trade consumption points?

Outside each country's PPC, showing consumption that was impossible in autarky.

11. (HL) Give two limitations of comparative advantage theory.

Any two of: transport costs, imperfect factor mobility, increasing (not constant) costs, trade barriers, changing (dynamic) comparative advantage, overspecialization risks, it ignores who gains.

12. Give one example of acquiring resources through trade from Hoang.

Lenovo importing cobalt from the DR Congo, or Singapore importing over 99% of its primary resources (p. 422).

Theory of knowledge

Free trade is advocated on the grounds that it leads to greater efficiency. Yet it results in both winners and losers. Do economists have a moral responsibility toward the losers when they advocate free trade?
