What is economics?

If resources are scarce and wants are unlimited, who should decide what gets produced, how, and for whom, and how can a simple model help us see the trade-offs?

Key terms and definitions

Economics The social science that studies how scarce resources are allocated to satisfy the unlimited needs and wants of individuals and societies.
Social science The study of human behaviour and of the relationships between individuals and societies; its subject is people, whose behaviour is less predictable than natural phenomena.
Microeconomics The study of the behaviour of individual consumers, firms and particular markets.
Macroeconomics The study of the economy as a whole, including national output, unemployment, inflation and economic growth.
Factors of production The resources used to produce goods and services: land (natural resources), labour (human effort), capital (human-made resources) and entrepreneurship (organising the other factors and taking risks).
Factor rewards The income earned by each factor: rent for land, wages for labour, interest for capital and profit for entrepreneurship.
Scarcity The situation in which resources are limited relative to unlimited human needs and wants, so choices must be made.
Needs and wants Needs are goods and services essential for survival (food, water, shelter); wants are desires for goods and services beyond survival.
Opportunity cost The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
Free good A good that is not scarce, needs no factors of production to obtain and so has zero opportunity cost (e.g. sunlight, seawater).
Economic good A good that is scarce and so has an opportunity cost; includes goods provided free at the point of use by government.
Basic economic questions What and how much to produce, how to produce it, and for whom to produce it.
Free market economy An economic system in which resources are mainly privately owned and allocated by the price mechanism.
Planned (command) economy An economic system in which the government owns most resources and decides what, how and for whom to produce.
Mixed economy An economic system combining a market-based private sector with a public sector in which government provides some goods and intervenes in markets.
Production possibilities curve (PPC) A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce in a given period when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, with given technology.
Increasing opportunity cost As more of one good is produced, ever larger amounts of the other must be given up because resources are not equally suited to both; shown by a concave PPC.
Constant opportunity cost The same amount of one good is given up for each extra unit of the other because resources are equally suited to both; shown by a straight-line PPC.
Actual growth An increase in real output from using existing resources more fully, shown as a movement from a point inside the PPC towards the curve.
Growth in production possibilities An increase in an economy's productive capacity due to more or better resources or technology, shown as an outward shift of the PPC.
Pareto efficiency A situation where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off; every point on the PPC is productively efficient in this sense.
Circular flow of income model A model showing the flows of money (incomes and spending) and of real resources (factors and goods) between households, firms and other sectors of the economy.
Leakages (withdrawals) Income that leaves the circular flow: saving (S), taxes (T) and import spending (M).
Injections Spending added to the circular flow that does not originate from households' consumption: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Scarcity and sustainability

Water in the Atacama vs the Amazon

A mining town in Chile's Atacama Desert pays about US$3 per cubic metre for desalinated water, while a village in the Brazilian Amazon draws river water at no cost. The local copper mine uses around 500 litres of water per second, and the town council is considering rationing household use.

Ask the class
• Is water a free good or an economic good in each place?
• What is the opportunity cost of the mine's water use?
• Which key concept best frames the council's decision?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Scarcity is relative (Hoang p. 12): water is limited relative to wants in the Atacama but plentiful in the Amazon.
  2. In the Atacama, water needs factors of production (desalination plants, energy, labour), so it has an opportunity cost and is an economic good. In the Amazon, untreated river water is close to a free good, although treating it for safe drinking would make it economic.
  3. The mine's water could otherwise go to households or farms; that next best use forgone is the opportunity cost.
  4. Sustainability (future access) and equity (who gets water) frame the rationing decision; rationing would be intervention replacing the price mechanism.
DiagramPPC with 'Copper output' on one axis and 'Household and farm water use' on the other; a movement along the concave curve shows the trade-off and rising opportunity cost.
Scenario 2 · Change

Bangladesh floods and the PPC

Monsoon floods in 2020 submerged roughly a quarter of Bangladesh, destroying rice paddies, roads and garment-factory stock. Many workers could not reach their jobs for weeks, but most factories reopened within a year.

Ask the class
• Show the short-run effect on the PPC.
• Distinguish unemployment of resources from a loss of productive capacity.
• What happens as factories reopen?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Draw a concave PPC for agricultural vs manufactured goods, with the economy initially on the curve at A.
  2. Workers unable to reach jobs are idle resources, so output moves inside the curve (unemployment of resources).
  3. Destroyed land and capital reduce productive capacity, so the PPC shifts inward (Hoang Fig 1.25).
  4. As factories reopen, output moves back towards the new curve: actual growth. Rebuilding with better flood defences and new machinery could later shift the PPC out: growth in production possibilities.
DiagramConcave PPC, axes 'Agricultural goods' and 'Manufactured goods'; PPC1 shifts in to PPC2; point A on PPC1 moves to C inside PPC2; arrow C→B back to PPC2.
Scenario 3 · Intervention and efficiency

Choosing an economic system: Estonia after 1991

When Estonia left the Soviet Union in 1991, almost all firms were state-owned and prices were set by planners. Within a decade it privatised most industry, freed prices and introduced a flat income tax. By 2020 it ranked 10th in the Heritage Foundation economic freedom index (Hoang Table 1.1, p. 18).

Ask the class
• How did the answers to 'what, how and for whom' change?
• What role does government still play?
• Is Estonia now a free market economy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Under planning, government decided what to produce (output quotas), how (state factories) and for whom (rationing and fixed wages).
  2. After reform, consumer demand and prices signal what to produce, firms choose methods to cut costs, and incomes determine who can buy.
  3. Government still provides education, healthcare and defence and regulates markets, so Estonia is a mixed economy near the market end of the spectrum (Fig 1.16).
  4. Evaluate: efficiency and growth rose, but inequality also rose during transition, illustrating a possible trade-off between efficiency and equity.
DiagramSpectrum of economic systems (Fig 1.16) with Estonia 1990 near 'planned' and Estonia 2020 near 'free market'; optionally a PPC shifting outward over the period.
Scenario 4 · Interdependence

Saving, spending and the circular flow in Japan

Japanese households save a large share of their income, and in the late 1990s firms cut investment sharply. The government responded with large public works programmes, while exports of cars and electronics stayed strong.

Ask the class
• Identify the leakages and injections involved.
• What happens to national income if saving rises but investment falls?
• How can government spending offset this?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Saving (S) is a leakage; investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X) are injections.
  2. If S rises while I falls, leakages exceed injections (W > J), so the flow of income shrinks and economic activity falls (Hoang p. 26).
  3. Extra G adds an injection, helping to restore S + T + M = G + I + X at a higher level of income.
  4. Strong exports also support the flow, showing interdependence with the foreign sector.
DiagramFive-sector circular flow (Fig 1.29): S to banks, T to government, M abroad as leakages; I from banks, G from government and X from abroad into firms as injections.

Diagrams: how to draw them

PPC showing choice and opportunity cost SL/HL

Fig 1.17 and Fig 1.22, pp. 19–21

  1. Label axes with two named goods (e.g. Consumer goods and Capital goods) and the origin 0.
  2. Draw a concave curve from the vertical to the horizontal axis and label it PPC.
  3. Mark two points on the curve, C and D, with dotted lines to the quantities on both axes.
  4. Show the movement C→D with an arrow; label the gain of one good and the loss (opportunity cost) of the other.
Common errorsDrawing the curve convex to the origin; not labelling quantities on the axes; forgetting that all points on the curve are efficient.
Graphing toolCourse Simulator – PPC & comparative advantage / Desmos

PPC showing constant vs increasing opportunity cost SL/HL

Fig 1.19, Figs 1.20–1.21, pp. 20–21

  1. Draw two diagrams side by side with similar axes.
  2. Left: a straight-line PPC (e.g. work vs leisure hours in a 168-hour week); mark equal steps to show constant opportunity cost (MRT = 1).
  3. Right: a concave PPC; mark equal gains of the horizontal good and show the growing sacrifice of the vertical good.
  4. Optionally add tangents that get steeper along the curve.
Common errorsMaking the concave curve so flat the difference is invisible; confusing the slope with the quantity of either good.

PPC showing unemployment of resources, actual growth and growth in production possibilities SL/HL

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – PPC & comparative advantage

Students enter a production table for two goods and generate the PPC. They drag a point inside the curve and notice that more of both goods is possible. They then apply a 'new technology' shock and an 'earthquake' shock and sketch the before and after. Finally they toggle between linear and concave PPCs and calculate opportunity cost for three successive steps.

Debrief
• Why does the concave PPC show rising opportunity cost?
• Which shock moved the curve, and which only moved the point?
• Which assumption of the model did the technology shock relax?

Classroom island allocation game (counters)

Give each group 20 counters representing land, labour and capital. Groups choose between producing food, shelter and tools according to recipe cards. Run three rounds under free market, planned and mixed rules, recording total output and a satisfaction score. Display results on the board to compare efficiency and equity across systems.

Debrief
• Which system produced the most output, and which the fairest distribution?
• What information did the planner lack?
• How did prices emerge in the free market round?

Heritage Foundation Index of Economic Freedom with Our World in Data · heritage.org/index ; ourworldindata.org

Pairs choose two countries from Hoang Table 1.1 or from the planned and mixed lists on p. 18. They look up economic freedom scores and GDP per capita and add them to a shared Google Sheets scatter chart. The class discusses whether more market freedom is associated with higher income and what else might explain the pattern.

Debrief
• Does correlation imply causation here?
• Which indicators of well-being might tell a different story?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. A country can produce 5,000 consumer goods and 4,000 producer goods, or 6,000 consumer goods and 3,000 producer goods (Hoang p. 14). What is the opportunity cost of the extra 1,000 consumer goods?

SolutionMoving to the second combination gains 1,000 consumer goods and loses 4,000 − 3,000 = 1,000 producer goods. Opportunity cost = 1,000 producer goods, i.e. 1 producer good per consumer good.

Problem 2. An economy can produce 100 consumer goods and 50 producer goods, or 80 consumer goods and 60 producer goods (practice 1.1, p. 14). What is the opportunity cost of one unit of producer goods?

SolutionGaining 10 producer goods costs 20 consumer goods. Opportunity cost of one producer good = 20 ÷ 10 = 2 consumer goods.

Problem 3. Concave PPC (p. 21): A→B gains 15 units of food (40 → 55) for 10 units of clothes; C→D gains 5 units of food (90 → 95) for 10 units of clothes. Show that opportunity cost is increasing.

SolutionA→B: 1 unit of food costs 10 ÷ 15 = 0.67 clothes. C→D: 1 unit of food costs 10 ÷ 5 = 2 clothes. The cost of food in terms of clothes rises as more food is produced, so opportunity cost is increasing.

Problem 4. In a year, S = $40bn, T = $60bn, M = $50bn, I = $45bn, G = $55bn, X = $40bn. Will national income rise, fall or stay constant?

SolutionW = 40 + 60 + 50 = $150bn; J = 45 + 55 + 40 = $140bn. W exceeds J by $10bn, so the level of economic activity will fall.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Capital means money. In economics capital is human-made resources such as machinery and buildings; money only buys them (p. 10).
  • Land means only physical space. It covers all natural resources: minerals, forests, fish stocks, water (p. 10).
  • Government-provided healthcare or roads are free goods. They use scarce resources and have an opportunity cost, so they are economic goods (p. 15).
  • Income only comes from work. Income is the total of rent, wages, interest and profit (p. 10).
  • An outward shift of the PPC is 'actual growth'. In IB usage an outward shift is growth in production possibilities; a movement towards the curve is actual growth (Hoang swaps these on p. 23).
  • Opportunity cost is the money price paid. It is the next best alternative given up, which may be time or another good.
  • The USA is a pure free market economy. It is a mixed economy with a large private sector (p. 18).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 23: Hoang describes moving from inside to on the PPC as 'growth in production possibilities' and an outward shift as 'actual growth'. The IB guide uses the opposite: moving towards the PPC is actual growth; an outward shift is growth in production possibilities. Correct this explicitly when teaching Figs 1.24–1.25.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Why is economics a social science?

It studies human behaviour and how people and societies interact to allocate scarce resources, so its subject is people rather than natural phenomena.

2. Give one micro and one macro question.

Micro: why has the price of coffee risen? Macro: why has national unemployment risen?

3. List the four factors of production and their rewards.

Land–rent, labour–wages, capital–interest, entrepreneurship–profit.

4. Define opportunity cost.

The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

5. Why is public healthcare not a free good?

It uses scarce resources (doctors, buildings, drugs) paid for by taxes, so it has an opportunity cost.

6. What are the three basic economic questions?

What/how much to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce.

7. How does a planned economy answer 'for whom'?

Government decides distribution, e.g. by rationing or setting wages, rather than leaving it to ability to pay.

8. State two assumptions of the PPC model.

Fixed quantity and quality of resources and fixed technology; resources are fully and efficiently employed producing only two goods.

9. What does a concave PPC show?

Increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods.

10. What does a point inside the PPC show?

Unemployed or inefficiently used resources.

11. Distinguish actual growth from growth in production possibilities on a PPC.

Actual growth is a movement from inside towards the curve; growth in production possibilities is an outward shift of the curve.

12. Calculate: 100C + 50P or 80C + 60P. What is the opportunity cost of 1P?

2 consumer goods (20C ÷ 10P).

13. Name the three leakages and three injections.

Leakages: saving, taxes, imports. Injections: investment, government spending, exports.

14. What happens to national income if injections exceed leakages?

It rises, because more spending is entering the circular flow than leaving it.
