Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 32

Balance of payments

Syllabus 4.6 Balance of paymentsTextbook pp. 506–526Sessions 6 HL · 3 SLConcepts interdependence, change, economic well-being, intervention, sustainability

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? When a country buys more from the world than it sells, how does it pay – and does it matter?

Common SL/HL: credit and debit items, surplus/deficit on an account, the structure of the current, capital and financial accounts, interdependence between the accounts and BOP calculations from data (pp. 506–515). HL only: current account and financial account links to the exchange rate (with diagram), implications of a persistent current account deficit, methods to correct it and their effectiveness, the Marshall–Lerner condition and J-curve (with diagram), and implications of a persistent current account surplus (pp. 515–526).

Key terms and definitions

Balance of payments (BOP) A record of all financial transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world over a given period, usually one year.
Credit items Transactions that bring money into the economy from abroad, such as export revenues, inflows of investment income or inward FDI; recorded as positive values.
Debit items Transactions that send money out of the economy to foreigners, such as import spending or investment income paid abroad; recorded as negative values.
Current account The part of the BOP that records the balance of trade in goods and services, net income flows (wages, interest, rent and profit) and net current transfers.
Balance of trade The value of exports of goods and services minus the value of imports of goods and services (X − M).
Net income Factor income received from abroad (wages, interest, rent, profits and dividends) minus factor income paid to non-residents.
Current transfers One-way payments for which nothing is received in return, such as remittances from workers abroad, foreign aid grants and pensions paid abroad.
Capital account The part of the BOP that records capital transfers (such as debt forgiveness and migrants' assets) and transactions in non-produced, non-financial assets such as patents and resource rights.
Financial account The part of the BOP that records changes in the ownership of assets across borders: foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, reserve assets and official borrowing.
Portfolio investment The purchase of financial assets such as shares and bonds in another country without gaining a controlling interest in the enterprise.
Reserve assets Foreign currencies and other liquid assets such as gold held by the central bank, used to intervene in the foreign exchange market or to finance a deficit.
Errors and omissions A balancing item that corrects for statistical discrepancies so that the sum of the current, capital and financial accounts equals zero.
Current account deficit A situation in which debits on the current account exceed credits, so that the country spends more abroad on goods, services, income and transfers than it earns.
Credit rating HLAn assessment by agencies such as Moody's or S&P of a borrower's ability to repay its debts; a lower rating raises the interest rate a government must pay.
Expenditure switching policies HLPolicies that shift domestic and foreign spending away from imports and towards domestically produced goods, such as protectionism, export promotion and devaluation.
Expenditure reducing policies HLContractionary fiscal or monetary policies that reduce aggregate demand and disposable income, and hence spending on imports.
Marshall–Lerner condition HLThe condition that a depreciation or devaluation improves the trade balance only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than one.
J-curve effect HLThe tendency for the trade balance to worsen in the short run after a depreciation, because demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, before improving as elasticities rise over time.
Imported inflation HLInflation caused by a rise in the domestic price of imports, often following a depreciation of the currency.
Hot money HLShort-term speculative capital that moves between countries in search of higher interest rates or expected currency appreciation; recorded in the financial account.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence

Remittances and the Philippine current account

In a given year, the Philippines records exports of goods of $75bn and imports of goods of $125bn. Services exports (largely business process outsourcing) are $45bn and services imports $25bn. Net income is +$3bn and net current transfers, mostly remittances from overseas workers, are +$32bn.

Ask the class
• Calculate the balance of trade and the current account balance.
• Is the large goods deficit a sign of economic weakness?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Goods balance = 75 − 125 = −$50bn; services balance = 45 − 25 = +$20bn; balance of trade = −$30bn.
  2. Current account = −30 + 3 + 32 = +$5bn, a small surplus.
  3. Remittances are current transfers, so they offset the trade gap; the goods deficit partly reflects imports of inputs for export industries and consumer spending financed by overseas earnings.
  4. Evaluate: dependence on remittances is a risk (a global downturn reduces them), and it reflects the loss of skilled workers abroad (brain drain), but it supports household consumption and development.
DiagramNo diagram required; lay out the current account in the IB format (Table 32.2): goods, services, income, transfers, with sub-totals.
Scenario 2 · Interdependence

Hong Kong: a deficit financed by investment

Hong Kong is a small, open economy with few natural resources that imports most of its food and energy (Hoang p. 514). Assume in one year its current account is −$8bn while net FDI inflows are +$10bn, portfolio flows −$1bn and the capital account is zero.

Ask the class
• How is the current account deficit financed?
• What is the value of errors and omissions?
• What risk does this pattern create?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The financial account = +10 − 1 = +$9bn, a surplus that provides the foreign exchange needed to pay for the excess of imports.
  2. CA + KA + FA + E&O = 0, so −8 + 0 + 9 + E&O = 0 and E&O = −$1bn.
  3. The pattern shows interdependence: deficits in one account are matched by surpluses in another.
  4. If investor confidence falls, FDI inflows could reverse, putting pressure on the currency (or on the peg and on reserves) and forcing a correction in spending.
DiagramHL: foreign exchange market diagram; a fall in FDI inflows shifts D for the currency left, putting downward pressure on the exchange rate (with a peg, the central bank must sell reserves to hold ER).
Scenario 3 · Sustainability

Persistent deficit in Country P (HL)

Country P has run a current account deficit of 5–7% of GDP for eight years, financed by short-term portfolio inflows attracted by interest rates of 9%. Foreign ownership of its property and banks has risen, external debt has reached 60% of GDP and a rating agency has downgraded its bonds.

Ask the class
• Explain three implications of the persistent deficit.
• Why is financing through portfolio inflows riskier than through FDI?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Exchange rate – the continuous supply of the currency to pay for imports creates depreciation pressure, held off only while hot money flows in (p. 517).
  2. Interest rates – the central bank must keep rates high to attract inflows, which raises borrowing costs, reduces domestic investment and slows growth.
  3. Foreign ownership and debt – selling assets means future profits flow abroad (a debit on the income account), and rising debt raises interest payments; the downgrade raises the cost of new borrowing (p. 518).
  4. Portfolio money is mobile; a sudden stop would cause sharp depreciation, imported inflation and possibly the need for IMF borrowing. FDI is longer term and harder to withdraw.
  5. Demand management is constrained: expansionary policy would widen the deficit further.
DiagramForeign exchange market: sudden stop shifts D for the currency left, ER falls from ER1 to ER2; optionally AD–AS showing a leftward AD shift if interest rates rise.
Scenario 4 · Change

Depreciation in Country Z: does the deficit shrink? (HL)

Country Z's currency falls by 15% after an election. In the first year, contracts are fixed and consumers keep buying imported fuel and medicines; estimated PEDx = 0.3 and PEDm = 0.2. After two years firms find new markets and consumers switch to domestic brands; PEDx = 0.9 and PEDm = 0.6.

Ask the class
• What happens to the trade balance in the short run and the long run?
• Draw the J-curve and link it to the Marshall–Lerner condition.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Short run: PEDx + PEDm = 0.5 < 1, so import spending in domestic currency rises more than export revenue; the trade balance worsens (A to B).
  2. Long run: PEDx + PEDm = 1.5 > 1, so export volumes rise and import volumes fall enough to improve the balance (B to C).
  3. Side effects: imported inflation from dearer fuel and medicines reduces real incomes; the central bank may need to tighten policy.
  4. Evaluate: the improvement depends on non-price competitiveness and trading partners' incomes, and may be eroded if domestic inflation cancels the price advantage.
DiagramJ-curve: vertical axis trade balance (deficit below zero, surplus above), horizontal axis time; depreciation at point A, dip to B, recovery through the zero line to C.
Scenario 5 · Economic well-being

Germany's persistent surplus (HL)

Germany has run current account surpluses of around 6–8% of GDP for much of the past decade, based on strong exports of cars and machinery. Critics, including the IMF and the US government, argue that German households and government save too much and invest too little at home.

Ask the class
• Evaluate the implications of the surplus for Germany and its trading partners.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Benefits – export-led jobs, AD boosted by net exports (Fig 32.12), and accumulation of foreign assets that yield future income.
  2. Costs – consumption and domestic investment are lower than they could be; the surplus is lent abroad rather than spent on German infrastructure.
  3. Exchange rate and competitiveness – outside the euro the currency would appreciate, reducing export competitiveness (inverse J-curve); inside the euro, the surplus contributes to imbalances with deficit members.
  4. Inflation and employment – high net exports can push AD beyond full employment, raising inflation.
  5. Judgement: desirability depends on the cause – competitiveness is healthier than suppressed domestic demand or protectionism (Top tip, p. 524).
DiagramAD–AS: an increase in (X − M) shifts AD right, raising real GDP and the price level (Fig 32.12, p. 523).

Diagrams: how to draw them

Current account and the exchange rate (floating exchange-rate diagram) HL

Fig 32.6, p. 515

  1. Label the vertical axis 'Price of A$ in £' (exchange rate) and the horizontal axis 'Quantity of A$'.
  2. Draw D (A$) – representing credits (foreigners buying Australian exports and assets) – sloping down.
  3. Draw S (A$) – representing debits (Australians buying imports and foreign assets) – sloping up.
  4. Mark the equilibrium ER1, Q1 where credits = debits and the BOP balances.
  5. To show a current account deterioration, shift S right (more imports) giving a lower ER2; for a financial-account inflow, shift D right giving a higher ER.
Common errorsLabelling the axes with price and quantity of goods; forgetting that the exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another; shifting D when imports rise instead of S.
Graphing toolCourse Simulator – Exchange-rate market / EconGraphs

The J-curve (with reference to the Marshall–Lerner condition) HL

Fig 32.10, p. 522

  1. Horizontal axis: Time; vertical axis: Current account (trade) balance, with a zero line; deficit below and surplus above.
  2. Start at point A, in deficit, at the moment of depreciation.
  3. Draw the curve dipping further into deficit to B (short run, PEDx + PEDm < 1).
  4. Draw the curve rising through the zero line to C (long run, PEDx + PEDm > 1).
  5. Annotate: 'Marshall–Lerner not satisfied' on the A–B segment and 'Marshall–Lerner satisfied' on B–C.
Common errorsStarting the curve at zero or in surplus; not labelling the axes; failing to explain why short-run elasticities are low; confusing the J-curve with the inverse J-curve used for surplus correction.

Inverse J-curve (surplus correction through appreciation) HL

Fig 32.11, p. 523

  1. Same axes as the J-curve.
  2. Start from a surplus; after appreciation the surplus initially widens, then falls towards balance as elasticities rise.
Common errorsDrawing it as the same shape as the J-curve; not stating that it follows appreciation or revaluation.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Exchange-rate market

Project the currency-market tool. Students predict the direction of each shock before it is applied: an import boom, a rise in the domestic interest rate, a large inward FDI project and a sudden capital flight. After each shift, they record the new exchange rate and identify the BOP account affected. Finish with a peg: fix the exchange rate and ask what the central bank must do with reserves when the supply of currency rises.

Debrief
• Which shocks came through the current account and which through the financial account?
• Why can a financial account surplus keep a currency strong even when the current account is in deficit?
• What happens to reserve assets under a peg when there is a persistent deficit?

Google Sheets J-curve model · sheets.google.com

Give students a template with quarterly export and import volumes, prices in foreign currency and an exchange rate. They apply a 20% depreciation in quarter 1 and enter elasticities that rise each quarter. The sheet converts values to domestic currency and plots the trade balance. Students adjust elasticities to find the point where the Marshall–Lerner condition is just met.

Debrief
• At what combined elasticity does the trade balance start to improve?
• Why are elasticities low immediately after a depreciation?
• What real-world factors might keep elasticities low for an ELDC exporting primary commodities?

IMF DataMapper / Trading Economics · imf.org/external/datamapper

Students map the current account balance (% of GDP) for the world and identify five persistent surplus countries and five persistent deficit countries. Each pair picks one of each, downloads a 10-year series, and plots it alongside the exchange rate from Trading Economics. They write a short commentary on whether the exchange-rate movements are consistent with theory.

Debrief
• Are persistent surplus countries mainly oil exporters, manufacturers, or both?
• Did the deficit countries' currencies depreciate as theory predicts? If not, why not?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Country K (EPQ 32.1, p. 510): exports total 85 (goods 57, services 28); imports of goods 88 and services 15. Calculate total imports, the balance of trade in goods, the balance of trade in services and the balance of trade.

SolutionTotal imports = 88 + 15 = 103. Goods balance = 57 − 88 = −31. Services balance = 28 − 15 = +13. Balance of trade = 85 − 103 = −18 (a deficit).

Problem 2. Country L (EPQ 32.2, p. 514): trade in goods −$18.3bn, trade in services +$21.8bn, net income +$6.7bn, net current transfers −$5.6bn. Calculate the balance of trade and the current account.

SolutionBalance of trade = −18.3 + 21.8 = +$3.5bn. Current account = 3.5 + 6.7 − 5.6 = +$4.6bn (a surplus).

Problem 3. Country J (EPQ 32.3, p. 515): exports of goods 235, exports of services 320, imports of goods −440, imports of services −235, net income 20, net current transfers −30, net direct investment 65, net portfolio investment 38, capital transfers 26, non-produced non-financial assets 20 ($bn). Calculate the current, financial and capital accounts and errors and omissions.

SolutionCurrent account = 235 + 320 − 440 − 235 + 20 − 30 = −$130bn. Financial account = 65 + 38 = +$103bn. Capital account = 26 + 20 = +$46bn. Sum = −130 + 103 + 46 = +$19bn, so errors and omissions = −$19bn for the BOP to balance.

Problem 4. Country Z: current account −$40bn, capital account +$5bn, financial account +$32bn. Calculate errors and omissions.

SolutionCA + KA + FA + E&O = 0, so −40 + 5 + 32 + E&O = 0; E&O = +$3bn.

Problem 5. Marshall–Lerner: a country's PED for exports is 0.45 and PED for imports is 0.35 in the short run, rising to 0.85 and 0.7 in the long run. Will a depreciation improve the trade balance in each period? HL

SolutionShort run: 0.45 + 0.35 = 0.8 < 1, so the trade balance worsens. Long run: 0.85 + 0.7 = 1.55 > 1, so the trade balance improves. This produces the J-curve shape.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A current account deficit always signals a weak economy – rich countries such as the USA and UK run persistent deficits, while some low-income countries run surpluses (CM, p. 511).
  • The balance of payments can be 'in deficit' overall – by construction the overall BOP balances; only individual accounts are in surplus or deficit.
  • The balance of trade and the current account are the same thing – the current account also includes net income and current transfers.
  • FDI and portfolio investment are interchangeable – FDI involves a lasting controlling interest; portfolio investment does not.
  • Depreciation immediately improves the trade balance – it may worsen it first if the Marshall–Lerner condition is not met (J-curve).
  • A current account surplus is always good – it may reflect suppressed consumption, low domestic investment or protectionism, and can provoke retaliation (p. 524).
  • BOP disequilibrium only refers to deficits – persistent surpluses are also a form of disequilibrium (CM, p. 524).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Identity on p. 514: the book writes 'Current account = Capital account + Financial account (+ Errors and omissions)'. With the sign conventions used in the book's own exercises (debits negative), the correct identity is CA + KA + FA + E&O = 0, i.e. a current account deficit is matched by a combined capital and financial account surplus. Teach the sign form to avoid calculation errors in EPQ 32.3.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define the balance of payments.

A record of all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period, usually one year.

2. What does GIST stand for?

Goods, Income, Services, Transfers – the four components of the current account.

3. A Kenyan worker in Dubai sends money home. Which account and which component for Kenya?

Current account, current transfers – a credit for Kenya.

4. Give one example of a capital account item.

Debt forgiveness, migrants' transfer of assets, or the sale of patent or resource rights.

5. What are reserve assets?

Foreign currencies and gold held by the central bank, used to intervene in the currency market or finance a deficit.

6. Goods −20, services +8, income −3, transfers +5. Calculate the current account.

−20 + 8 − 3 + 5 = −10, a current account deficit of 10.

7. Why must the BOP balance overall?

Every deficit in one account must be financed by a surplus in another (borrowing, selling assets or using reserves), with errors and omissions correcting statistical gaps.

8. (HL) On a currency diagram, what represents debits?

The supply of the domestic currency, because residents sell it to buy foreign goods and assets.

9. (HL) How can a financial account inflow hide a current account problem?

Hot money or FDI inflows raise demand for the currency, keeping the exchange rate high even though imports exceed exports.

10. (HL) Name two implications of a persistent current account deficit.

Any two of: depreciation pressure, higher interest rates, more foreign ownership of domestic assets, rising debt, lower credit rating, constraints on demand management, slower growth.

11. (HL) Distinguish expenditure switching and expenditure reducing.

Switching redirects spending from imports to domestic goods (e.g. devaluation, tariffs); reducing cuts total spending, including on imports, through contractionary policy.

12. (HL) State the Marshall–Lerner condition.

A depreciation improves the trade balance only if PEDx + PEDm > 1.

13. (HL) Why does the J-curve dip first?

In the short run demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, so import spending in domestic currency rises more than export revenue.

14. (HL) Give one disadvantage of a persistent current account surplus.

Lower domestic consumption and investment, appreciation pressure that harms competitiveness, inflation, or retaliation from trading partners.
