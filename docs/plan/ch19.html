Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 19

Macroeconomic objectives: low unemployment

Syllabus 3.3 Macroeconomic objectives – low unemploymentTextbook pp. 275–288Sessions 3 HL · 2 SLConcepts economic well-being, equity, change, intervention

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Why do some people fail to find work even when the economy is growing, and what does unemployment cost them and society?

All syllabus content is common SL/HL (sessions 1–2). Session 3 is an HL-marked extension: real-wage unemployment and the minimum wage in more depth (Hoang Paper 3 Q19.2, HL), the natural rate and policies to reduce it, and data-based policy evaluation for Paper 3.

Key terms and definitions

Unemployment The situation where people of working age who are able, willing and actively seeking work at the going wage rate cannot find a job.
Labour force All people of working age who are employed (including the self-employed) plus those who are unemployed and actively seeking work.
Unemployment rate The number of unemployed people as a percentage of the labour force: (unemployed ÷ labour force) × 100.
ILO measure of unemployment Counts people without work who have actively looked for a job in the last four weeks and can start within the next two weeks (or are waiting to start a job within two weeks).
Claimant count The number of people out of work and claiming unemployment-related benefits; it usually understates true unemployment.
Hidden unemployment Unemployment that is not shown in official figures, e.g. discouraged workers, people on training schemes or those not eligible for benefits.
Discouraged workers People who want a job but have stopped actively searching after repeated failure, so they are not counted as unemployed.
Underemployment When workers are not fully used: part-time workers who want full-time work, or people in jobs below their skill level.
Cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment Unemployment caused by a fall in AD in a downturn, which reduces the derived demand for labour; shown by a deflationary gap.
Structural unemployment Long-term unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, e.g. because of the decline of an industry, technological change or globalisation.
Seasonal unemployment Unemployment caused by regular changes in the demand for labour at certain times of the year, e.g. in agriculture, tourism and construction.
Frictional unemployment Short-term unemployment of people moving between jobs or entering the labour market while they search for work.
Real-wage unemployment Unemployment caused when real wages are held above the market-clearing level, for example by a minimum wage or union bargaining, creating an excess supply of labour.
Natural rate of unemployment (NRU) The rate of unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium at full employment output: the sum of structural, seasonal and frictional unemployment.
Seasonally adjusted data Statistics that have been modified to remove the usual seasonal ups and downs so that the underlying trend can be seen.
Formal sector employment Officially registered jobs that pay taxes and are recorded in national statistics.
Brain drain The emigration of skilled and educated workers to other countries in search of better jobs and pay.
Female participation rate The percentage of women of working age who are in the labour force (employed or actively seeking work).
Costs of unemployment Personal costs (loss of income, stress, deskilling), social costs (crime, inequality, family breakdown) and economic costs (lost output, lower tax revenue, higher benefit spending).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being

Spain after the global financial crisis

Spain's unemployment rate rose from about 8% before 2008 to 26.9% in 2013, with youth unemployment above 55% (Hoang pp. 280–281). The construction sector collapsed and household spending fell.

Ask the class
• Which type of unemployment was mainly created?
• Show it on a diagram.
• Why did part of it become structural?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Falling house building and weaker confidence cut I and C, so AD shifted left (AD1 → AD2).
  2. Real GDP fell below full employment output (Ye < Yf), opening a deflationary gap; firms need fewer workers, so cyclical unemployment rose.
  3. Construction never returned to its pre-crisis size, so former builders lacked the skills for growing sectors; long spells out of work also caused deskilling. Part of the cyclical unemployment became structural.
  4. Policy implication: demand-side stimulus for the cyclical part, retraining and labour-market reform for the structural part.
DiagramAD/AS diagram with AD1 shifting left to AD2, LRAS at Yf and Ye < Yf labelled as the deflationary gap (cyclical unemployment).
Scenario 2 · Change

Sheffield steel and the Full Monty

In the 1980s UK steel production fell sharply because of cheaper imports and new technology. In Sheffield thousands of steelworkers lost their jobs, as shown in the film The Full Monty (Hoang p. 283). The national economy later recovered but many older steelworkers never worked again.

Ask the class
• Which type of unemployment is this?
• Show it on the labour market diagram.
• What policies would help?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Demand for UK steel fell permanently, so the derived demand for steelworkers fell.
  2. In the steel labour market, demand for labour shifts left (DL1 → DL2): the wage falls from W1 to W2 and employment falls from N1 to N2.
  3. Laid-off workers had industry-specific skills and lived in one region, so they could not easily move into new service jobs: occupational and geographical immobility.
  4. A rise in AD would not bring the steel jobs back. Retraining, regional investment, relocation grants and support for new industries are more suitable.
DiagramLabour market for steelworkers: wage rate (vertical), number of workers (horizontal); demand for labour shifts left from DL1 to DL2 with supply SL unchanged; mark W1→W2 and N1→N2.
Scenario 3 · Equity

A minimum wage rise for young workers

Suppose a country's market-clearing wage for young, low-skilled workers is $8 per hour with 1.8m employed. The government sets a minimum wage of $10: firms now want to hire 1.5m workers and 2.2m young people want to work.

Ask the class
• How much real-wage unemployment results?
• Who gains and who loses?
• What determines the size of the effect?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. At $10 the quantity of labour supplied (2.2m) exceeds the quantity demanded (1.5m): excess supply of 0.7m workers.
  2. Employment falls by 0.3m compared with equilibrium; 0.4m extra people are attracted into the labour market but cannot find work.
  3. Winners: the 1.5m who keep jobs at a higher wage. Losers: the 0.3m who lose jobs, firms facing higher costs, and consumers if prices rise.
  4. Evaluate: the effect is smaller if labour demand is wage-inelastic or if firms have monopsony power; real-world studies often find small job losses for moderate rises.
DiagramMinimum wage diagram: wage rate on the vertical axis, number of workers on the horizontal; draw Wm above We, mark quantity demanded (1.5m) and supplied (2.2m) and label the excess supply as unemployment.
Scenario 4 · Equity

Saudi Arabia: women entering the labour force

Saudi Arabia lifted its ban on women driving in 2018–19, and the female labour force participation rate roughly doubled in the following years (Hoang p. 280). Many new entrants are searching for their first job.

Ask the class
• What happens to the labour force?
• Why might the measured unemployment rate rise at first?
• Is this a bad sign for the economy?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. More women become willing and able to work, so the labour force grows.
  2. New entrants take time to find suitable jobs, so frictional unemployment rises and the unemployment rate may increase even while employment grows.
  3. This shows a limit of the unemployment rate as a single indicator: the employment rate and participation rate also matter.
  4. In the long run a larger, more educated labour force raises potential output (LRAS shifts right).
DiagramAD/AS diagram with LRAS shifting right as the labour force grows; alternatively the natural rate diagram with the labour force curve shifting right.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Minimum wage causing unemployment (real-wage unemployment) SL/HL

Fig 19.1, p. 276; Fig 19.2, p. 278

  1. Label axes: Real wage rate (vertical) and Number of workers / employment (horizontal).
  2. Draw aggregate demand for labour (ADL) sloping down and aggregate supply of labour (ASL) sloping up; mark We and Ne.
  3. Draw a horizontal line at the minimum wage Wm above We.
  4. Mark quantity demanded (Nd) and quantity supplied (Ns) at Wm.
  5. Label Ns – Nd as excess supply of labour = unemployment.
Common errorsDrawing the minimum wage below equilibrium; labelling curves D and S without saying labour; measuring unemployment from Ne instead of Nd.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Price ceiling/floor (as a labour market)

Tell students to read the vertical axis as the wage and the horizontal as the number of workers. Set a floor above equilibrium and ask them to read off quantity demanded and supplied. Change the floor three times and record excess supply in a table. Then make demand steeper (less elastic) and repeat. Students write a sentence on how elasticity changes the unemployment effect.

Debrief
• Why does a higher minimum wage attract more people into the labour market?
• When would a minimum wage cause very little unemployment?
• Who are the winners and losers of a minimum wage?

Trading Economics / ILOSTAT data comparison · https://ilostat.ilo.org

Each pair chooses two countries with different income levels. They collect the unemployment rate, youth unemployment rate, labour force participation rate and share of informal employment. They present the data in a small table and answer: which country's headline rate gives a truer picture of the labour market? One pair per region reports back.

Debrief
• Why might a low-income country have a low official unemployment rate?
• What does a low participation rate suggest about hidden unemployment?
• Which extra indicator would you publish next to the unemployment rate?

Job search 'frictional unemployment' classroom game

Give half the class 'job' cards (skill required, town, wage) and half 'worker' cards (skill, town, reservation wage). In round 1 workers can only ask one employer per minute; in round 2 you post all vacancies on the board (a job centre). Record how many are matched after three minutes in each round. In round 3 announce that one industry closes and its workers keep their old skill cards.

Debrief
• Why did the job centre reduce unemployment in round 2?
• What type of unemployment appeared in round 3?
• Which policy fits each type?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Basic rate (p. 277): labour force 50m, of whom 5m are unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate.

Solution5m ÷ 50m × 100 = 10%.

Problem 2. Worked example (p. 278): labour force 30m, working-age population 35m, unemployment rate 7.9%. Calculate the number unemployed.

SolutionUse the labour force, not the working-age population: 30m × 0.079 = 2.37m unemployed.

Problem 3. Q19.1 (p. 278): total population 100m; 74.8% of the population employed; 15m unemployed; 20% retired. Calculate the unemployment rate.

SolutionEmployed = 74.8m. Labour force = employed + unemployed = 74.8m + 15m = 89.8m. Retired people are not in the labour force, so ignore them. Rate = 15 ÷ 89.8 × 100 ≈ 16.7%.

Problem 4. Minimum wage (Fig 19.1, p. 276): at the equilibrium wage We 18m are employed; at the minimum wage Wm firms demand 10m workers and 26m offer to work. Calculate the unemployment caused and the jobs lost.

SolutionUnemployment (excess supply) = 26m – 10m = 16m. Jobs lost compared with equilibrium = 18m – 10m = 8m; the other 8m are extra people attracted into the labour market by the higher wage.

Problem 5. Change in the rate: labour force rises from 32.0m to 32.5m while unemployment falls from 1.6m to 1.56m. Calculate the change in the unemployment rate.

SolutionBefore: 1.6 ÷ 32.0 × 100 = 5.0%. After: 1.56 ÷ 32.5 × 100 = 4.8%. The rate fell by 0.2 percentage points (a 4% fall in the rate itself).

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Everyone without a job is unemployed – students, retirees and others not seeking work are economically inactive, not unemployed.
  • The unemployment rate uses the whole population or working-age population as the denominator – it uses the labour force.
  • Full employment means zero unemployment – there is always frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment (the natural rate).
  • All unemployment can be cured by raising AD – demand-side policy only reduces cyclical unemployment; structural unemployment needs supply-side measures.
  • Structural unemployment is shown on an AD/AS diagram – it is shown by a fall in demand for labour in one market or region.
  • A rising unemployment rate always means fewer jobs – it can rise because more people join the labour force.
  • A minimum wage always causes large unemployment – the effect depends on how far above equilibrium it is set and on labour demand elasticity.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 278, Q19.1: the data are not consistent. 74.8% employed (74.8m) + 15m unemployed + 20% retired (20m) = 109.8m, which is more than the total population of 100m. The intended answer ignores the retired figure: 15 ÷ (74.8 + 15) × 100 ≈ 16.7%. Tell students before setting the question, or use it as a 'spot the problem' exercise.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define unemployment.

People of working age who are able, willing and actively seeking work but cannot find a job.

2. Write the formula for the unemployment rate.

(Number unemployed ÷ labour force) × 100.

3. Labour force 25m, unemployed 1.5m. What is the unemployment rate?

6%.

4. Why does the claimant count usually understate unemployment?

Many unemployed people are not eligible for, or do not claim, benefits.

5. What are discouraged workers?

People who have stopped looking for work after repeated failure, so they are not counted as unemployed.

6. Give an example of underemployment.

A graduate working part-time in a café who wants a full-time job in their field.

7. Which diagram shows cyclical unemployment?

An AD/AS diagram with a deflationary gap (Ye < Yf).

8. What causes structural unemployment?

A permanent fall in demand for labour in an industry or region, with workers unable to move because of skill or location mismatch.

9. Which three types make up the natural rate?

Structural, seasonal and frictional unemployment.

10. Why does a minimum wage above equilibrium cause unemployment?

It raises the quantity of labour supplied and reduces the quantity demanded, creating excess supply.

11. Give one personal, one social and one economic cost of unemployment.

Loss of income and self-esteem; higher crime or family breakdown; lost output and lower tax revenue.

12. Suggest a policy for seasonal unemployment.

Encourage diversification of the local economy and retraining so workers have off-season jobs.

13. Why can the unemployment rate rise while employment rises?

If the labour force grows faster than the number of jobs, e.g. as more people start looking for work.
