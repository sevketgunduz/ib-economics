Key terms and definitions

Demand The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price during a given time period, ceteris paribus.
Quantity demanded The amount consumers are willing and able to buy at one particular price; a single point on the demand curve.
Law of demand There is a negative (inverse) relationship between the price of a good and its quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.
Demand curve A graph showing the relationship between price (vertical axis) and quantity demanded (horizontal axis); it slopes downward from left to right.
Income effect HLWhen the price of a good falls, consumers' real income (purchasing power) rises, so they can buy more of it.
Substitution effect HLWhen the price of a good falls, it becomes cheaper relative to substitutes, so consumers switch towards it.
Law of diminishing marginal utility HLAs more units of a good are consumed, the additional satisfaction from each extra unit falls, so consumers buy more only if the price falls.
Market demand The sum of all individual consumers' demand for a good at each price (horizontal summation of individual demand curves).
Non-price determinants of demand Factors other than the good's own price that change demand and shift the demand curve: prices of related goods, income, tastes and preferences, expectations, number of consumers.
Normal good A good for which demand increases when consumers' income rises, ceteris paribus.
Inferior good A good for which demand falls when consumers' income rises, as they switch to preferred alternatives.
Substitutes Goods in competitive demand that can replace each other; a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other.
Complements Goods in joint demand that are used together; a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other.
Future price expectations If consumers expect a price to rise in future, current demand increases; if they expect it to fall, current demand decreases.
Movement along the demand curve A change in quantity demanded caused only by a change in the good's own price: an expansion (price falls) or a contraction (price rises).
Shift of the demand curve A change in demand at every price caused by a non-price determinant: an increase (rightward shift) or a decrease (leftward shift).
Ceteris paribus The assumption that all other factors affecting demand remain constant while the price changes.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Change

Oat milk in Sweden

Sales of oat milk in Sweden have grown rapidly as more consumers adopt plant-based diets, helped by Oatly's marketing. At the same time, dairy milk prices rose by about 15% in 2022 because of higher feed and energy costs.

Ask the class
• What happens to the demand for oat milk?
• Which determinants are involved?
• Show the effect on a diagram.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A change in tastes and preferences towards plant-based products increases demand for oat milk at every price.
  2. Dairy milk is a substitute; its price rise also increases demand for oat milk.
  3. Both factors shift the oat-milk demand curve right from D1 to D2; at the original price more is demanded.
  4. Note that for dairy milk the price rise causes a contraction along its own demand curve, not a shift.
DiagramDemand diagram for oat milk: axes Price and Quantity of oat milk per week; D1 shifts right to D2; at P1 quantity rises from Q1 to Q2.
Scenario 2 · Interdependence

PlayStation price cut and the games market

Sony cuts the price of its console by $100. Game publishers report that demand for new titles rises by 12% in the following quarter, while demand for a rival console falls.

Ask the class
• What happens in the console market?
• What happens to demand for games?
• What happens to demand for the rival console?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. In the console market, the price cut causes an expansion along the demand curve (movement, not shift).
  2. Games are complements; more console owners increase demand for games, shifting the games demand curve right.
  3. The rival console is a substitute; its demand curve shifts left because the Sony console is now relatively cheaper.
  4. This shows interdependence between markets (Hoang Fig 3.6 on smartphones and apps).
DiagramThree panels: consoles (movement along D from A to B), games (D1 → D2 right), rival console (D1 → D3 left).
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

Recession and supermarket own-brands in the UK

In 2023 UK real wages fell as inflation outpaced pay rises. Supermarkets reported rising sales of own-label products and falling sales of premium brands.

Ask the class
• Classify own-label and premium goods.
• Explain the shifts in each market.
• Why must we use real rather than nominal income?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A fall in real income reduces purchasing power.
  2. Premium brands are normal goods: demand falls, so D shifts left.
  3. Own-label goods behave as inferior goods for many households: demand rises as income falls, so D shifts right.
  4. Real income matters because a nominal pay rise below inflation still reduces what people can buy.
DiagramTwo demand diagrams: premium brand D1 → D2 (left); own-label D1 → D2 (right).
Scenario 4 · Scarcity

Population growth and housing demand in Hong Kong

Hong Kong has one of the world's least affordable housing markets (Hoang p. 52). Inward migration from mainland China and smaller household sizes raise the number of households each year, while buyers also expect prices to keep rising.

Ask the class
• Which two determinants increase housing demand?
• Show the combined effect.
• Why is the effect on price not shown by the demand curve alone?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. More consumers (households) increase market demand, since market demand is the sum of individual demands.
  2. Expectations of future price rises lead buyers to purchase now, increasing current demand.
  3. Both shift D right from D1 to D2, and by more than either alone.
  4. The actual price change depends on supply too, which is covered in Chapter 5 on equilibrium.
DiagramDemand for housing: D1 shifts right to D2 (and possibly D3 for the expectations effect); axes Price of flats and Quantity of flats per year.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Downward-sloping demand curve SL/HL

Fig 3.2, p. 48

  1. Label the vertical axis Price (P) and the horizontal axis Quantity demanded (Q) per time period.
  2. Draw a straight downward-sloping line and label it D.
  3. Mark P1 and Q1 at one point, then a lower price P2 with larger Q2.
  4. Add dotted lines to both axes and an arrow along the curve.
Common errorsLabelling the horizontal axis 'Demand'; omitting the time period; drawing the curve upward.
Graphing toolDesmos / Course Simulator – Supply & demand shifter

Movements along the demand curve (expansion and contraction) SL/HL

Fig 3.11, p. 53

  1. Draw one demand curve D.
  2. Mark an initial point A at P1, Q1.
  3. For a price rise, move up the curve to B at P2, Q2 and label 'contraction'.
  4. For a price fall, move down to C at P3, Q3 and label 'expansion'.
Common errorsShifting the curve when only the own price changes; writing 'demand increases' instead of 'quantity demanded increases'.

Shifts of the demand curve (increase and decrease) SL/HL

Fig 3.13, p. 54

  2. Draw D2 to the right of D1 (increase) and D3 to the left (decrease), parallel for clarity.
  3. At a constant price P1, show quantities Q3 < Q1 < Q2.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Supply & demand shifter

Hide the supply curve so only demand is visible. Read out an event card (e.g. 'The price of petrol rises'), give students 20 seconds to sketch the predicted effect on demand for large cars, then reveal the correct shift on the simulator. Repeat for eight events mixing own-price changes and non-price determinants. Students keep a tally of their correct answers.

Debrief
• Which events caused a movement and which a shift?
• Which determinants did you find hardest to classify?
• Why must we say 'ceteris paribus' in each case?

Classroom auction to build a demand schedule

Give each student a private card showing their maximum willingness to pay for a snack. Call out prices from high to low and count how many would buy at each price. Record the results as a market demand schedule and plot it in Google Sheets. Then give half the class a 'pay rise' card (add $1 to their value) and repeat, showing the curve shift.

Debrief
• Why did the curve slope downward?
• What caused the curve to shift?
• How is market demand related to individual demand?

EconGraphs demand explorer · https://www.econgraphs.org

Students open the demand graph and move the income and related-price sliders. They record how the curve moves and write a one-line rule for normal goods, inferior goods, substitutes and complements. As an extension they compare a linear and a curved demand function.

Debrief
• What does each slider represent in RIPEN?
• What happens to quantity demanded at the old price after a shift?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. At a price of $15, 500 male and 400 female customers wish to buy tickets (Hoang Fig 3.3, p. 48). What is market demand at $15?

SolutionMarket demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands: 500 + 400 = 900 tickets at $15.

Problem 2. Three consumers demand 4, 6 and 10 units at $2 and 2, 3 and 5 units at $4. Construct the market demand schedule.

SolutionAt $2: 4 + 6 + 10 = 20 units. At $4: 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 units. Market demand falls from 20 to 10 as price rises from $2 to $4, consistent with the law of demand.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Demand is what people want. Demand is what people are willing and able to buy (p. 47).
  • A fall in price increases demand. It increases quantity demanded, which is a movement along the curve (p. 54).
  • 'Demand' and 'quantity demanded' are interchangeable. Demand is the whole curve; quantity demanded is a point on it (p. 48).
  • Market demand is simply 'demand in a market'. It is the sum of individual demands at each price (p. 48).
  • Inferior goods are poor-quality goods. The term describes how demand responds to income, not quality.
  • A rise in the price of a complement shifts demand for the good right. It shifts it left, because the joint cost of using the pair rises.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define demand.

The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given period, ceteris paribus.

2. State the law of demand.

As price rises, quantity demanded falls (and vice versa), ceteris paribus.

3. (HL) Explain the substitution effect of a price fall.

The good becomes cheaper relative to alternatives, so consumers switch towards it.

4. (HL) How does diminishing marginal utility explain the demand curve's slope?

Each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so consumers will buy more only at a lower price.

5. What is market demand?

The horizontal sum of individual demands at each price.

6. What does RIPEN stand for?

Related goods, Income, Preferences, Expectations, Number of consumers.

7. Petrol prices rise. What happens to demand for large cars?

It decreases (shifts left) because petrol and cars are complements.

8. Coffee prices rise. What happens to demand for tea?

It increases (shifts right) because tea is a substitute.

9. Income rises. What happens to demand for bus travel if it is inferior?

It decreases (shifts left).

10. What causes a movement along the demand curve?

Only a change in the good's own price.

11. Name the movement when price falls.

An expansion (extension) of quantity demanded.

12. Draw: a health report says blueberries prevent illness. Which shift?

Demand for blueberries shifts right (increase) due to tastes and preferences.
