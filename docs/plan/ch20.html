Macroeconomic objectives: low and stable rate of inflation

Syllabus 3.3 Macroeconomic objectives – low and stable rate of inflationTextbook pp. 289–309Sessions 4 HL · 3 SLConcepts economic well-being, equity, change, intervention

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Why do central banks aim for about 2% inflation rather than zero – and why is falling prices' 'good news' often bad news?

Key terms and definitions

Inflation A sustained rise in the general (average) price level in an economy over a period of time, which reduces the purchasing power of money.
Inflation rate The percentage change in the price index (usually the CPI) over a period, normally one year.
Price stability A low and stable rate of inflation (many central banks target about 2%), not zero inflation.
Hyperinflation Extremely high and accelerating inflation (often defined as over 50% a month) that destroys confidence in money.
Consumer price index (CPI) A weighted index of the prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by a typical household, used to measure changes in the cost of living.
Base year The reference period given an index value of 100, against which later prices are compared.
Statistical weights Values attached to each item in the CPI to reflect its relative importance in household spending, based on quantities purchased or on the share of income spent.
Weighted price index HLAn index calculated by multiplying each item's price index by its weight, adding these, and dividing by the sum of the weights.
Demand-pull inflation Inflation caused by AD rising faster than AS, especially as the economy approaches full capacity; shown by a rightward AD shift.
Cost-push inflation Inflation caused by rising costs of production, such as wages, raw materials, imported inputs or indirect taxes; shown by a leftward SRAS shift.
Wage-price spiral A process in which rising prices lead workers to demand higher wages, which raise firms' costs and prices further.
Real interest rate The nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate; it shows the true return on saving or cost of borrowing.
Menu costs The costs to firms of frequently changing prices, e.g. reprinting price lists and updating systems.
Shoe leather costs The time and effort people spend managing money and searching for the best prices when inflation is high.
Deflation A persistent fall in the general price level, i.e. a negative inflation rate.
Disinflation A fall in the rate of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly.
Benign (good) deflation Falling prices caused by increases in SRAS or LRAS (e.g. new technology), with rising real output.
Malign (bad) deflation Falling prices caused by a fall in AD, associated with falling real output, unemployment and bankruptcies.
Deferred consumption Consumers postponing purchases because they expect prices to fall further, which reduces AD.
Deflationary spiral A self-reinforcing cycle of falling prices, falling spending, lower output, lower wages and further price falls.
Negative equity When the value of a property falls below the value of the mortgage owed on it.
Misery index The unemployment rate plus the inflation rate; a rough measure of the combined economic costs faced by households (Arthur Okun).

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being

Venezuela and Zimbabwe: hyperinflation

Zimbabwe's inflation reached about 231 million per cent in July 2008 and it printed a Z$100 trillion note; it later used the US dollar and rand. Venezuela's inflation passed one million per cent in 2019 (Hoang pp. 290, 308). In both countries governments financed spending by printing money while output collapsed.

Ask the class
• What caused the hyperinflation?
• Show it on a diagram.
• Explain three costs, using REUSER.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Rapid growth in the money supply raised spending (AD shifts right repeatedly) while output fell (SRAS/LRAS shifted left as farms and factories closed).
  2. On an AD/AS diagram, AD shifts right while SRAS shifts left, so the price level rises very sharply.
  3. Costs: uncertainty (firms cannot plan or price), savings wiped out (real interest rates deeply negative), redistribution from savers and fixed-income earners to debtors, export competitiveness lost, and resources wasted on shoe-leather and menu costs.
  4. People stop using the currency (dollarisation), which is why Zimbabwe abandoned its own money.
DiagramAD/AS diagram: AD1 → AD2 → AD3 shifting right and SRAS1 → SRAS2 shifting left; the average price level rises from PL1 to PL3 with real GDP falling.
Scenario 2 · Equity

Europe's 2022 energy shock: cost-push inflation

In 2022 wholesale gas prices in Europe rose several times over after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Euro-area HICP inflation peaked at 10.6% in October 2022. Unions in several countries demanded large pay rises to protect real wages.

Ask the class
• Which cause of inflation is this?
• What risk do the pay claims create?
• Who is hurt most?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Energy is an input for almost all firms, so costs of production rise and SRAS shifts left: cost-push inflation, with lower real GDP.
  2. If wages rise to match prices, costs rise again and SRAS shifts left again: a wage-price spiral, especially if inflation expectations rise.
  3. Low-income households spend a larger share on energy and food, so their personal inflation rate is higher than the CPI – a limitation of the CPI and a redistributive cost.
  4. Evaluate: the ECB raised interest rates to stop expectations rising, but this reduces AD and output further.
DiagramAD/AS diagram with SRAS1 shifting left to SRAS2 (and then SRAS3 for the wage-price spiral); PL rises and real GDP falls.
Scenario 3 · Change

Japan's lost decades: malign deflation

After asset prices collapsed in 1990–91, Japan had long periods of zero or negative inflation (Fig 20.11, Hoang p. 303). Households delayed spending, firms cut investment and interest rates fell to zero.

Ask the class
• Is this benign or malign deflation?
• Why is it hard to escape?
• Explain two costs using R U CUPID.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Falling asset prices and confidence reduced C and I, so AD shifted left: the price level fell along with real GDP – malign deflation.
  2. Consumers expecting lower prices deferred purchases, lowering AD further (deflationary spiral, Fig 20.16).
  3. Deflation raised the real value of debt, so indebted firms and households cut spending and some firms went bankrupt.
  4. With interest rates at zero, monetary policy became ineffective (policy ineffectiveness), which is why Japan relied on fiscal stimulus and quantitative easing.
DiagramAD/AS diagram: AD1 shifts left to AD2, PL1 falls to PL2 and real GDP falls from Y1 to Y2 (Ye < Yf).
Scenario 4 · Efficiency

Cheaper televisions: benign deflation in one sector

The price of a 55-inch flat-screen television has fallen by more than half over a decade while quality has improved, thanks to new technology and economies of scale. Imagine a whole economy where technology lowers costs in this way.

Ask the class
• Show benign deflation on a diagram.
• Why is it not harmful?
• Why might the CPI understate real improvements here?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Better technology and productivity reduce costs, so SRAS (and LRAS) shift right.
  2. The price level falls while real GDP rises; employment and incomes can grow.
  3. Because falling prices come with rising output and real incomes, consumers do not defer spending for long, so there is no deflationary spiral.
  4. The CPI does not fully capture quality improvements, so the true fall in quality-adjusted prices is even larger.
DiagramAD/AS diagram with SRAS1 → SRAS2 and LRAS1 → LRAS2 shifting right; PL falls and real GDP rises.

Simulation and data activities

ECB 'Inflation Island' game · https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb/educational/html/index.en.html

Students play in pairs for 15 minutes. They manage an island economy and see how money supply and inflation affect prices, savings and trade. They record what happens to savers and borrowers in a table. Stop the game after two inflation episodes and ask each pair to report one cost of inflation they observed.

Debrief
• Which groups gained and which lost when inflation rose?
• Why did the island's exports suffer?
• How does this link to REUSER?

Class CPI builder in Google Sheets · https://sheets.google.com

Students enter items, quantities and two years' prices into a shared template. Formulas calculate each item's spending, the basket total, the index and the inflation rate. Then split the class into 'students' and 'parents' baskets and compare their inflation rates. HL students switch to expenditure-share weights and use SUMPRODUCT.

Debrief
• Why did the two groups get different inflation rates?
• What does this show about the CPI as an 'average'?
• How would you update the basket for new products?

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

Give students four headlines (house price boom, oil price spike, new technology cuts costs, consumer confidence collapses). For each they predict on mini-whiteboards which curve shifts and what happens to the price level, then check on the simulator. Finally they classify each outcome as demand-pull inflation, cost-push inflation, benign deflation or malign deflation.

Debrief
• Why does the same fall in the price level have very different welfare effects?
• Which case creates a policy dilemma for the central bank?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Unweighted basket (p. 291), base year 2017 basket = $20. 2019 prices: pizza $9, cinema $10, petrol $3; 2020: $10, $11, $3.50. Calculate the index for each year and the 2019–20 inflation rate.

SolutionBasket 2019 = $22.0, index = 22 ÷ 20 × 100 = 110.0. Basket 2020 = $24.5, index = 122.5. Inflation = (122.5 – 110.0) ÷ 110.0 × 100 = 11.36%.

Problem 2. Quantities as weights (p. 292): pizza $8 → $10 (6 bought), cinema $9 → $10 (3), public transport $4 → $5 (15). Calculate the inflation rate.

Solution2019 basket = (8 × 6) + (9 × 3) + (4 × 15) = 48 + 27 + 60 = $135. 2020 basket = (10 × 6) + (10 × 3) + (5 × 15) = 60 + 30 + 75 = $165. Inflation = (165 – 135) ÷ 135 × 100 = 22.22%.

Problem 3. Q20.4–20.6 (p. 293): (a) CPI rises from 123.0 to 129.15 – inflation rate? (b) CPI was 130 and inflation is 3% – new CPI? (c) A basket costs $1,200 when the CPI is 125; what does it cost when the CPI is 135?

Solution(a) (129.15 – 123.0) ÷ 123.0 × 100 = 5.0%. (b) 130 × 1.03 = 133.9. (c) 1,200 × 135 ÷ 125 = $1,296.

Problem 4. Q20.1 (p. 293): inflation 2.5%, 3.1%, 2.9% and wage increases 3.0%, 3.5%, 3.1% in Years 1–3. In which year did real wages rise most?

SolutionReal wage growth ≈ wage growth – inflation: Year 1 = 0.5%, Year 2 = 0.4%, Year 3 = 0.2%. Largest in Year 1. Wages were still higher in Year 3 than Year 2 because money wages rose by 3.1% (and faster than prices).

Problem 5. Real interest rates (pp. 298, 306): (a) savings rate 1%, inflation 2.5%; (b) mortgage rate 5%, inflation 3.5%; (c) interest rate 1%, inflation –1.5%.

Solution(a) 1 – 2.5 = –1.5% (savers lose). (b) 5 – 3.5 = 1.5%. (c) 1 – (–1.5) = 2.5% (deflation raises the real cost of borrowing).

Problem 6. Q20.3 (HL, p. 293): food and drink index 120 (weight 10), transportation 130 (20), leisure 140 (30), housing 150 (40). Calculate the weighted price index. HL

SolutionΣ(index × weight) = 1,200 + 2,600 + 4,200 + 6,000 = 14,000. Σ weights = 100. Weighted index = 14,000 ÷ 100 = 140.

Problem 7. Q20.7 (HL, p. 294), Jukeland: clothing 110 (weight 10), food 120 (20), housing 130 (30), others 140 (40). Calculate the weighted CPI. HL

Solution(110 × 10) + (120 × 20) + (130 × 30) + (140 × 40) = 1,100 + 2,400 + 3,900 + 5,600 = 13,000; ÷ 100 = 130.

Problem 8. Expenditure shares (p. 292): food 0.40 (index 110), entertainment 0.20 (115), transport 0.25 (116.4), others 0.15 (123.3). Calculate the weighted and unweighted index. HL

SolutionWeighted = 44.0 + 23.0 + 29.1 + 18.5 = 114.6. Unweighted = (110 + 115 + 116.4 + 123.3) ÷ 4 = 116.18. The weighted index is lower because the item with the smallest price rise (food) has the largest weight.

Problem 9. HL two-year problem: weights food 30, housing 45, transport 25. Year 1 indices: 104, 110, 106. Year 2 indices: 110, 113, 101. Calculate both weighted indices and the inflation rate. HL

SolutionYear 1 = (104 × 30 + 110 × 45 + 106 × 25) ÷ 100 = (3,120 + 4,950 + 2,650) ÷ 100 = 107.2. Year 2 = (110 × 30 + 113 × 45 + 101 × 25) ÷ 100 = (3,300 + 5,085 + 2,525) ÷ 100 = 109.1. Inflation = (109.1 – 107.2) ÷ 107.2 × 100 ≈ 1.77%.

Problem 10. Session 4 exit ticket: weights food 30, housing 50, transport 20; indices 108, 104, 115. Calculate the weighted index. HL

Solution(108 × 30 + 104 × 50 + 115 × 20) ÷ 100 = (3,240 + 5,200 + 2,300) ÷ 100 = 107.4.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Inflation means every price is rising – it is a rise in the average price level; some prices may fall.
  • Price stability means zero inflation – central banks usually target low, positive inflation (about 2%).
  • Demand-pull and cost-push are 'types' of inflation – they are causes.
  • Deflation is the same as disinflation – disinflation is a slower rate of increase; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Deflation always makes people better off – only benign deflation with rising output does; malign deflation brings unemployment and bankruptcies.
  • The CPI measures the price of goods only – it includes services, which are a large share of spending.
  • Inflation is always bad – low, stable inflation gives firms room to adjust real wages and avoids the risks of deflation.
  • Weights in a price index must add up to 100 – any weights work if you divide by their sum.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 292 (quantity-weighted worked example): the second working line shows the 2020 basket as '($10 × 6) + ($10 × 3) + ($5 × 15) = $135'. The correct total is $165 (60 + 30 + 75), as the table and the final calculation [(165 – 135) ÷ 135 × 100 = 22.22%] show. Point this out before students copy the working.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define inflation.

A sustained rise in the general price level over a period of time.

2. What is the CPI?

A weighted index of the prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by a typical household.

3. The CPI rises from 140 to 147. What is the inflation rate?

(147 – 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = 5%.

4. A basket costs $250 in the base year and $270 now. What is the index?

270 ÷ 250 × 100 = 108.

5. Give two limitations of the CPI.

It reflects an average household only, and it does not fully capture quality changes (also regional differences, time lags, changing consumption patterns).

6. Which curve shifts in cost-push inflation, and in which direction?

SRAS shifts left.

7. State three costs of high inflation.

Uncertainty, redistribution from savers and fixed-income earners, and lower export competitiveness (also savings, growth, resource allocation).

8. Interest on savings is 2% and inflation is 4%. What is the real interest rate?

–2%.

9. Distinguish disinflation from deflation.

Disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate (prices still rising); deflation is a fall in the price level.

10. What causes malign deflation?

A fall in AD, which lowers prices and real output.

11. Why does deflation increase the burden of debt?

The money value of debt is fixed while prices and incomes fall, so the real value of debt rises.

12. Why might consumers defer spending during deflation?

They expect prices to be lower in the future.

13. What is the misery index?

The unemployment rate plus the inflation rate.

14. (HL) Indices 120, 140; weights 70, 30. What is the weighted index?

(120 × 70 + 140 × 30) ÷ 100 = 126.
