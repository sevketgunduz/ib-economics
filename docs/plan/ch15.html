Unit 2 · Microeconomics · Hoang Chapter 15

The market's inability to achieve equity

Syllabus 2.12 The market's inability to achieve equity (HL only)Textbook pp. 217–224Sessions 2 HL · 0 SLConcepts equity, economic well-being, interdependence, intervention

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? Why does a free market that rewards productivity also produce large and lasting gaps in income and wealth, within and between countries?

HL only (the entire chapter). IB-required diagram: the circular flow model showing why the free market leads to inequality. Measuring inequality (Lorenz curve, Gini) is covered in Unit 3.4.

Key terms and definitions

Income HLA flow of money received over a period, as payment for factors of production: wages (labour), rent (land), interest (capital) and profit (enterprise), plus transfer payments.
Wealth HLA stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as property, land, shares and savings, minus debts.
Income inequality HLAn uneven distribution of income among individuals or households in an economy, or between countries.
Wealth inequality HLAn uneven distribution of assets among the population. It is usually much greater than income inequality, because wealth builds up and is passed on.
Equity HLFairness in the distribution of income and wealth. It is a normative idea and is not the same as equality.
Circular flow of income model HLA model showing how money flows between households and firms (factor incomes and consumer spending), with leakages (S, T, M) and injections (I, G, X).
Leakages (withdrawals) HLIncome that leaves the circular flow and is not spent on domestic output: saving (S), taxation (T) and imports (M).
Injections HLSpending that enters the circular flow from outside the household–firm flow: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X).
Human capital HLThe skills, knowledge, qualifications and experience of workers. More human capital usually raises productivity and earnings.
Inheritance HLWealth passed from one generation to the next. It allows inequality to persist across generations regardless of effort.
Disposable income HLHousehold income after direct taxes are paid and transfer payments received. It is a better indicator of living standards than gross income.
Progressive tax HLA tax in which the proportion of income paid in tax rises as income rises, e.g. income tax with higher marginal rates on higher bands. It reduces income inequality.
Narrow tax structure HLA tax system with a small gap between the highest and lowest tax rates and a limited range of taxes (e.g. Hong Kong). It does little to redistribute income.
Marginal tax rate HLThe percentage of an additional dollar of income paid in tax.
Transfer payments HLPayments from the government to individuals, such as unemployment benefit, pensions and child benefit, funded by taxes, with no output produced in return.
Value added HLThe increase in the value of a product at each stage of production. Primary commodities have low value added compared with manufactured goods and services.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence

Coffee farmers in Honduras

Honduras, with GDP per capita of about $2,200, relies heavily on coffee exports (Hoang pp. 222–223). World coffee prices fell sharply between 2016 and 2019, reaching their lowest level in over a decade (Fig 15.4). Farmers sell raw beans for a small share of the retail price, while roasting, branding and café sales – most of the value added – happen in high-income countries. Honduras imports high value-added goods such as smartphones and tablets.

Ask the class
• How does the circular flow explain lower incomes in Honduras?
• Why does primary-sector dependence widen the gap between countries?
• What could reduce this inequality?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Exports (X) are an injection; lower coffee prices reduce export revenue and so reduce injections into the Honduran circular flow.
  2. Imports (M) of high-value goods are a leakage. If M > X, there is a net leakage, so national income and employment fall.
  3. Low value added in primary production means low factor incomes for farmers, while high value added and profit go to firms in richer countries.
  4. In a free market, incomes follow the value of the factors and products people sell, so the gap between countries grows.
  5. Responses: diversification, Fairtrade premiums, processing and roasting locally, investment in human capital. These are examined further in Unit 4 (development).
DiagramCircular flow model (Fig 15.2) for Honduras. Draw a smaller X arrow and a larger M arrow; label the net leakage and its effect on household incomes. Optionally sketch Fig 15.5 (coffee vs iPad value chain).
Scenario 2 · Equity

Hong Kong's narrow tax structure

Hong Kong has a top salaries tax rate of 17%, no sales tax and a corporate tax of 16.5% (Table 15.2). It has one of the highest Gini coefficients among high-income economies (about 0.47 even after taxes and transfers, 2016), while Sweden, with a top rate of 57.2%, has a Gini of about 0.28. Hong Kong property prices are among the highest in the world, so homeowners' wealth has risen far faster than renters' incomes.

Ask the class
• How does the tax system affect income distribution?
• Why is wealth inequality even larger than income inequality?
• What are the trade-offs of a more progressive system?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. In the circular flow, taxation (T) is a leakage and government spending (G) is an injection. How they are designed decides who pays and who benefits.
  2. A narrow tax structure with low top rates takes a small share from high earners, so post-tax incomes stay very unequal.
  3. Rising property prices increase the wealth of existing owners (a stock), which generates further income (rent) that compounds over time.
  4. A more progressive system (higher marginal rates, more transfers) would reduce inequality.
  5. Evaluate. Very high marginal rates may reduce work incentives and attract less investment, and a low-tax regime is part of Hong Kong's competitiveness.
DiagramCircular flow with an emphasis on T and G: a small T arrow from high-income households and small transfer flows back to low-income households.
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

Two graduates, two inheritances

Ana and Ben graduate from the same university with the same grades. Ana's parents give her $150,000 towards a flat; Ben starts with $40,000 of student debt and rents. After ten years their salaries are similar, but Ana owns an asset worth $400,000 while Ben has savings of $20,000.

Ask the class
• Why do their wealth positions differ so much?
• How does the circular flow (S and I) explain this?
• Should the government intervene?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Their incomes (flows) are similar, but their wealth (stocks) is very different because of inheritance.
  2. Ana's wealth earns returns (capital gains, no rent to pay), which lets her save more (S) and invest (I) further, so the gap compounds.
  3. Ben's rent is a transfer of income to property owners, and his debt repayments reduce his ability to save.
  4. The free market rewards ownership of assets, so inherited advantage persists across generations.
  5. Possible interventions: inheritance and property taxes, first-time buyer support, free higher education. Each has trade-offs for incentives and fairness.
DiagramCircular flow focusing on the financial sector: savings flow in from wealthy households, and investment returns flow back to asset owners.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Circular flow of income model with injections and leakages HL

Fig 15.2, p. 219; Fig 15.6, p. 223

  1. Draw two boxes, Households and Firms. The top loop shows factor services from households to firms and factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) back to households.
  2. The bottom loop shows goods and services from firms to households and consumer spending (C) back to firms.
  3. Add the financial sector (S out of households, I into firms), the government (T out, G in) and the foreign sector (M out, X in).
  4. Label S, T, M as leakages and I, G, X as injections.
  5. Annotate for inequality: unequal factor ownership leads to unequal factor incomes; only high earners can save; the design of T and G; the effect of M > X on national income.
Common errorsDrawing money and real flows in the same direction; mixing up leakages and injections; drawing the diagram without explaining how it shows inequality.

Simulation and data activities

Unequal-start board game (Monopoly variant)

Split the class into tables of four. At each table one player starts with double cash and two properties, one with standard cash, and two with half cash. Play 20 minutes of fast rounds, then record each player's total wealth and income from rents. Change one rule (e.g. a 'wealth tax' of 10% each time you pass Go, paid to the poorest player) and play 10 more minutes. Compare the outcomes.

Debrief
• How did starting wealth affect final outcomes?
• Which part of the circular flow does rent represent, and who receives it?
• How did the redistribution rule change incentives and fairness?

World Bank Open Data / Our World in Data inequality explorer · data.worldbank.org / ourworldindata.org

Pairs download Gini index data for the ten countries in Table 15.2 (p. 221) and enter them next to the top income-tax rates in Google Sheets. They build a scatter chart and add a trend line, then pick one outlier and research why it does not fit. Pairs present their chart and one finding in one minute.

• Is there a clear link between top tax rates and inequality?
• What other factors (transfers, education, labour markets) could explain the pattern?
• Why is data on wealth inequality harder to find than data on income inequality?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Illustrative (not an IB-required calculation): a household earns $4,000 a month in wages, $500 rent from a flat and $300 interest, and receives no transfers. It pays 20% direct tax on all income. Calculate gross and disposable income. HL

SolutionGross income = 4,000 + 500 + 300 = $4,800. Tax = 0.2 × 4,800 = $960. Disposable income = $3,840.

Problem 2. Illustrative: in an economy S = $200bn, T = $300bn, M = $250bn, I = $180bn, G = $280bn and X = $220bn. Compare leakages and injections and state the likely effect on national income. HL

SolutionLeakages = 200 + 300 + 250 = $750bn; injections = 180 + 280 + 220 = $680bn. Leakages exceed injections by $70bn, so national income, output and employment tend to fall, which Hoang links to growing inequality (p. 223).

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Income and wealth are the same thing. Income is a flow over time; wealth is a stock of assets at a point in time (pp. 217–218).
  • Equity means equality. Equity is about fairness, which is a value judgement; an equitable distribution need not be equal.
  • Inequality is only about individuals. It also exists between regions and countries, so answers should be written in context (p. 218).
  • A higher top tax rate always reduces inequality. The effect depends on the whole system: tax bands, avoidance, transfers and spending.
  • The circular flow diagram is only a macro tool. It appears in 1.1, 2.12 and 3.1 and can show how the pattern of flows causes inequality (p. 223).
  • Free markets pay people what they 'deserve'. Market incomes reflect the productivity and scarcity of the factors people own, which depend on inheritance, education and discrimination as well as effort.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Is income a stock or a flow?

A flow: money received over a period of time.

2. Name the four factor incomes.

Wages (labour), rent (land), interest (capital) and profit (enterprise).

3. What are the three leakages from the circular flow?

Saving, taxation and imports (S, T, M).

4. What are the three injections?

Investment, government spending and exports (I, G, X).

5. Give two reasons a free market produces unequal incomes.

Unequal ownership of factors (wealth and inheritance), and differences in human capital, bargaining power and discrimination.

6. Why is wealth inequality usually greater than income inequality?

Wealth accumulates, earns further income and is inherited, so gaps compound across generations.

7. What is a progressive tax?

A tax where the proportion of income paid rises as income rises.

8. How do transfer payments reduce inequality?

They raise the disposable incomes of low-income groups (e.g. the unemployed and pensioners), funded by taxes.

9. Why does dependence on primary exports widen inequality between countries?

Primary goods have low value added and volatile prices, while high value-added goods are imported, so there is a net leakage of income.

10. What happens to national income if S + T + M > I + G + X?

It falls, reducing output and employment.

11. How can gender discrimination lead to income inequality?

Women may be paid less, have less access to education and credit, or be kept out of high-paid jobs, which lowers their factor incomes.
