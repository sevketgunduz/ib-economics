Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 17

Variations in economic activity: aggregate demand and aggregate supply

Syllabus 3.2 Variations in economic activity – aggregate demand and aggregate supplyTextbook pp. 243–263Sessions 7 HL · 5 SLConcepts change, intervention, economic well-being, efficiency

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Why do some recessions correct themselves while others last for years – and should governments step in?

All 3.2 syllabus content is common SL/HL. Sessions 1–5 are taught to both levels. Sessions 6–7 are HL-only: deeper analysis of Keynesian vs new classical equilibrium, their assumptions and policy implications (preparation for the Paper 3 policy question), the Q17.4 AD/AS data table (Paper 3, HL, p. 257) and extended exam practice.

Key terms and definitions

Aggregate demand (AD) The total planned spending on a country's goods and services at each average price level in a given period: C + I + G + (X – M).
Wealth effect A fall in the price level raises the real value of households' money and assets, so they spend more; one reason AD slopes downwards.
Interest rate effect A lower price level reduces the demand for money, so interest rates fall and consumption and investment rise; one reason AD slopes downwards.
Exchange rate (international trade) effect A lower domestic price level makes exports cheaper and imports dearer (and lower interest rates may weaken the currency), so net exports rise.
Consumption (C) Spending by households on goods and services; the largest component of AD in most countries.
Investment (I) Spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery, buildings and technology, which adds to productive capacity.
Disposable income Household income after direct taxes and national insurance contributions have been paid, plus transfer payments.
Indebtedness The amount households or firms owe; high debt means more income goes on repayments, reducing consumption or investment.
Aggregate supply (AS) The total output of goods and services that firms in an economy are willing and able to produce at each average price level in a given period.
Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) The planned output at each price level when factor prices (especially money wages) and productivity are held constant; it slopes upwards.
Adverse supply-side shock An unexpected rise in production costs or disruption to production (e.g. an oil price spike, war, pandemic) that shifts SRAS to the left.
Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) In the monetarist/new classical view, a vertical curve at the full employment (potential) level of output, set by the quantity and quality of resources and independent of the price level.
Keynesian AS curve An AS curve with three sections: horizontal where there is large spare capacity, upward-sloping as bottlenecks appear, and vertical at full employment output (Yf).
Inflationary (positive output) gap The amount by which actual real GDP exceeds potential (full employment) output, putting upward pressure on the price level.
Deflationary (recessionary / negative output) gap The amount by which actual real GDP falls short of potential (full employment) output because AD is too low, leaving unemployed resources.
Macroeconomic equilibrium The price level and real output at which AD equals AS.
Full employment The level of output at which all those willing and able to work at the going wage have a job; unemployment equals the natural rate, not zero.
Natural rate of unemployment (NRU) The unemployment that exists when the economy is at full employment output: the sum of frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment.
Automatic adjustment In the new classical model, flexible wages and prices shift SRAS until output returns to potential output without government intervention.
Sticky wages (downward wage rigidity) Money wages that do not fall easily because of contracts, trade unions, minimum wages and worker resistance, so labour markets fail to clear.
Monetarist/new classical school A school of thought holding that markets clear through flexible prices, the economy tends to full employment in the long run, and demand management mainly causes inflation.
Keynesian school A school of thought holding that the economy can remain below full employment for long periods because of sticky wages and weak AD, so governments should manage demand.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence

Germany when China slows down

China is one of Germany's largest export markets, especially for cars and machinery. Suppose Chinese real GDP growth slows sharply and German exports to China fall by €30bn. At the same time German business confidence falls and firms postpone investment.

Ask the class
• What happens to German AD?
• Show the short-run effect on output and the price level.
• Which of the two models predicts a faster recovery, and why?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Lower income in a trading partner cuts German exports, so X – M falls.
  2. Weaker confidence reduces I. Both shift AD from AD1 to AD2 (leftward).
  3. With an upward-sloping SRAS, real GDP falls from Y1 to Y2 and the price level falls from PL1 to PL2 (or rises less). A deflationary gap opens (Y2 < Yf).
  4. New classical: lower wages and costs shift SRAS right until output returns to Yf. Keynesian: wages are sticky, so the gap may persist and fiscal stimulus is needed.
  5. Judgement depends on German labour-market flexibility (collective wage agreements suggest stickiness).
DiagramAD/AS diagram: average price level (vertical), real GDP (horizontal); AD1 shifts left to AD2 on an upward-sloping SRAS; mark LRAS at Yf and show Y2 < Yf as a deflationary gap.
Scenario 2 · Change

Energy price shock in the euro area, 2022

After Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, gas and electricity prices in Europe rose several times over. Euro-area inflation peaked above 10% in late 2022 while growth slowed close to zero.

Ask the class
• Which curve shifts and why?
• What happens to real GDP and the price level?
• Why is this a dilemma for policymakers?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Energy is a key input, so firms' costs of production rise: an adverse supply-side shock.
  2. SRAS shifts left from SRAS1 to SRAS2.
  3. On the new equilibrium the price level rises (PL1 to PL2) and real GDP falls (Y1 to Y2): higher inflation with lower output (stagflation).
  4. Raising AD to protect output adds to inflation; cutting AD to reduce inflation deepens the fall in output. This is why the ECB and governments used targeted subsidies (which shift SRAS back right) as well as interest rate rises.
DiagramAD/AS diagram: SRAS1 shifts left to SRAS2 with AD unchanged; label PL1→PL2 (up) and Y1→Y2 (down); optional LRAS at Yf.
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

Spain after 2008: a gap that would not close

Spain's unemployment rose from about 8% before the global financial crisis to nearly 27% in 2013, and youth unemployment passed 55% (Hoang pp. 235, 281). Real GDP stayed below its pre-crisis level for about a decade.

Ask the class
• Show Spain's position on a Keynesian AS diagram.
• Why did the gap persist?
• What does each school recommend?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The collapse of the construction boom and credit crunch cut I and C, shifting AD left.
  2. On the Keynesian AS curve the economy moved into the horizontal section: output far below Yf with little change in the price level.
  3. Money wages did not fall quickly (contracts, minimum wage, worker resistance) and weak confidence meant low interest rates did not revive spending, so no automatic recovery.
  4. Keynesians recommend expansionary fiscal policy; new classical economists recommend labour-market reform to make wages flexible and cut the NRU. Spain's euro membership meant it could not devalue, and debt limits restricted fiscal policy (link to Ch 21).
DiagramKeynesian AS diagram: horizontal, rising and vertical sections; AD2 cuts AS in the horizontal section at Ye well below Yf; label the deflationary gap.
Scenario 4 · Efficiency

5G and digital platforms raising potential output

Investment in 5G networks and digital platforms (Alibaba, Airbnb, Uber) lets firms use existing resources more efficiently and creates new products (Hoang pp. 247, 254–255). Suppose a country's potential output rises by 3% over five years as a result.

Ask the class
• Which AS curve shifts?
• What happens to the price level and real GDP in the long run?
• What is the short-run effect of the investment itself?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. In the short run the investment spending raises I, shifting AD right.
  2. In the long run, better technology and efficiency raise productive capacity: LRAS shifts right (Yf1 to Yf2), or the vertical part of the Keynesian AS moves right.
  3. If AD grows in line, real GDP rises with little inflation; if AS grows faster than AD, the price level can fall (benign deflation, Ch 20).
  4. Evaluate: gains may be uneven – workers in displaced jobs (taxi drivers, retail staff) may become structurally unemployed (Ch 19).
DiagramAD/AS diagram with LRAS1 shifting right to LRAS2 (Yf1 → Yf2), AD1 shifting right to AD2; show higher real GDP and a stable price level.

  1. Start at long-run equilibrium: AD1, SRAS1 and LRAS meet at Yf.
  2. Shift AD1 left to AD2: short-run equilibrium at Y2 < Yf, PL falls.
  3. Show falling wages shifting SRAS1 right to SRAS2 until a new equilibrium at Yf and a lower PL3.
  4. For an inflationary gap reverse: AD right, then SRAS left back to Yf at a higher PL.
Common errorsSkipping the short-run step; shifting AD back instead of SRAS in the self-correction story; not explaining why wages change.

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

Project the tool and put it in LRAS mode. Give students a shock (e.g. a fall in business confidence) and ask them to predict on mini-whiteboards the short-run change in PL and Y. Shift AD and reveal. Switch to Keynesian mode, repeat the same AD fall starting from the horizontal section, and ask what – if anything – moves the economy back.

Debrief
• What assumption makes SRAS shift back in the new classical model?
• In which section of the Keynesian AS does fiscal stimulus cause the least inflation?
• Which model better fits the COVID-19 recession of 2020?

Classroom 'sticky wages' labour market game

Give half the students 'firm' cards and half 'worker' cards with reservation wages. Run a pit-market round to find the market wage. Announce that demand for output has fallen: firms now value workers less. In round two allow free bargaining; in round three impose a rule that money wages cannot fall (contracts). Record employment in each round on the board.

Debrief
• Why did employment fall more in round three?
• Which real-world factors make wages sticky?
• What does this imply for the Keynesian argument for government intervention?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. AD from components: C = $540bn, I = $120bn, G = $180bn, X = $210bn, M = $230bn. Calculate AD at the current price level.

SolutionAD = C + I + G + (X – M) = 540 + 120 + 180 + (210 – 230) = $820bn.

Problem 2. Q17.4 (HL, p. 257). Price index 128: C 20, I 4, G 5, X 6, M 10, AS 53. Index 124: C 22, I 6, G 7, X 7, M 9, AS 47. Index 120: C 24, I 8, G 9, X 8, M 8, AS 41. Index 116: C 26, I 10, G 11, X 9, M 7, AS 35 (all $bn). (a) Net exports at 128; (b) equilibrium real output; (c) price level when AD = $49bn; (d) investment when AD = $33bn. HL

SolutionAD at each index: 128 → 20 + 4 + 5 + (6 – 10) = 25; 124 → 22 + 6 + 7 – 2 = 33; 120 → 24 + 8 + 9 + 0 = 41; 116 → 26 + 10 + 11 + 2 = 49. (a) X – M = 6 – 10 = –$4bn. (b) AD = AS = $41bn at a price index of 120. (c) 116. (d) $6bn (at price index 124).

Problem 3. HL extension of Q17.4: G rises by $8bn at every price level. Find the new equilibrium from the table. HL

SolutionNew AD: 128 → 33; 124 → 41; 120 → 49; 116 → 57. AS: 53, 47, 41, 35. At 120, AD (49) > AS (41), so there is excess demand; at 124, AD (41) < AS (47), so there is excess supply. The new equilibrium therefore lies between price index 120 and 124, with output between $41bn and $47bn (by linear interpolation about index 122.3 and output of about $44.4bn). The price level rises and output rises, but by less than the $8bn increase in G, because the AS curve slopes upwards.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • AD slopes down for the same reasons as a demand curve for one good – the reasons are the wealth, interest rate and exchange rate effects, not substitution between goods.
  • Any tax change shifts SRAS – income tax changes shift AD; indirect taxes and business costs shift SRAS.
  • The Keynesian AS curve is a long-run curve – it should be labelled AS/Keynesian AS, not LRAS.
  • Full employment means zero unemployment – it means unemployment is at the natural rate.
  • A change in the price level shifts AD – it causes a movement along AD.
  • An increase in AD always causes inflation – with large spare capacity (horizontal Keynesian section) output can rise with little effect on prices.
  • In the new classical model the government shifts AD back to close a gap – it is the SRAS curve that shifts, through changes in wages and costs.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. State the four components of AD.

Consumption, investment, government spending and net exports (X – M).

2. Give the three reasons why AD slopes downward.

The wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the exchange rate (international trade) effect.

3. A central bank raises interest rates. Which components of AD are affected and how?

C and I fall (borrowing is dearer, saving more attractive), and a stronger currency may reduce net exports, so AD shifts left.

4. Name the two IB determinants of SRAS.

Costs of factors of production and indirect taxes.

5. Does a rise in income tax shift AD or SRAS?

AD – it reduces disposable income and consumption.

6. What does a vertical LRAS imply about the effect of AD in the long run?

AD changes only the price level; real output stays at potential output.

7. Name the three sections of the Keynesian AS curve.

Horizontal (spare capacity), upward-sloping (bottlenecks) and vertical (full employment, Yf).

8. Define a deflationary gap.

When equilibrium real GDP is below full employment output because AD is too low.

9. Give two causes of a rightward shift of LRAS.

An increase in the quantity or quality of factors (e.g. education) and better technology (also efficiency, institutions).

10. In the new classical model, how does a deflationary gap close?

Unemployment pushes down wages and costs, SRAS shifts right and output returns to Yf at a lower price level.

11. Why may a deflationary gap persist in the Keynesian model?

Money wages and prices are sticky downwards and confidence is weak, so there is no automatic rise in output.

12. What is unemployment at full employment output called?

The natural rate of unemployment.

13. Sketch the effect of an oil price rise on PL and Y.

SRAS shifts left: the price level rises and real GDP falls.

14. (HL) In Q17.4, what are the equilibrium price index and output?

Price index 120, real output $41bn, where AD = AS.
