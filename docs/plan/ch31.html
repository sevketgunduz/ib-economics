Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 31

Exchange rates

Syllabus 4.5 Exchange ratesTextbook pp. 485–505Sessions 5 HL · 4 SLConcepts interdependence, intervention, change, economic well-being, equity

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? When a currency rises or falls, who gains, who loses, and should governments try to control it?

Common SL/HL: floating exchange rate determination, appreciation and depreciation; the factors that change demand for and supply of a currency; consequences for inflation, growth, unemployment, the current account and living standards (AD/AS diagrams); fixed exchange rates (devaluation, revaluation, how they are maintained); managed exchange rates, overvalued and undervalued currencies; the calculations of the price of a good in different currencies and of changes in the value of a currency. The IB guide lists these two calculations without an HL-only label, so they are taught to all students. Hoang labels his conversion questions 'Paper 2 and 3', and the unit brief treats them as HL; check the current guide and teach them as common content. HL only: fixed versus floating exchange rate systems (pp. 502–504) – HL session 5.

Key terms and definitions

Exchange rate The price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency.
Floating exchange rate An exchange rate system in which the value of a currency is determined by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no deliberate government intervention.
Appreciation An increase in the value of a currency against another currency in a floating exchange rate system.
Depreciation A decrease in the value of a currency against another currency in a floating exchange rate system.
Demand for a currency The quantity of a currency that foreigners want to buy at each exchange rate, e.g. to buy the country's exports, invest in it or send remittances; it represents an inflow of money.
Supply of a currency The quantity of a currency that residents offer in exchange for foreign currency at each exchange rate, e.g. to buy imports or invest abroad; it represents an outflow.
Foreign direct investment (FDI) Long-term investment by a multinational company in productive assets in another country. Inward FDI increases demand for the currency; outward FDI increases its supply.
Portfolio investment The purchase of foreign financial assets, such as shares and bonds, which does not involve control of the firm.
Remittances Money sent home by people working abroad; inflows of remittances increase the demand for the home currency.
Speculation Buying or selling a currency in the hope of making a profit from future changes in its value; expectations can shift demand and supply sharply.
Hot money Short-term capital that moves between countries to take advantage of interest-rate differences or expected exchange-rate changes.
Fixed exchange rate An exchange rate system in which the central bank keeps the currency at a set value against another currency (or basket) by buying and selling currencies and adjusting interest rates.
Foreign currency reserves Stocks of foreign currencies (and other reserve assets such as gold) held by a central bank, which it can use to buy its own currency and support its value.
Devaluation A deliberate decision by the authorities to lower the value of a currency in a fixed exchange rate system.
Revaluation A deliberate decision by the authorities to raise the value of a currency in a fixed exchange rate system.
Managed exchange rate A system in which the exchange rate is mainly set by market forces, but the central bank intervenes from time to time to keep it within a target range or to prevent sharp changes.
Crawling peg A pegged rate that is adjusted gradually in small, frequent steps, often combined with a band within which the market rate may move.
Overvalued currency A currency whose value is above its long-run equilibrium (market) value, making exports uncompetitive and imports cheap.
Undervalued currency A currency whose value is below its long-run equilibrium (market) value, making exports cheap and imports expensive.
Current account The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services, primary income (e.g. investment income) and secondary income (current transfers).
Currency liquidity HLHow much of a currency is available and easily traded in the foreign exchange market.
Impossible trinity (policy trilemma) HLThe idea that a country cannot at the same time have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital and an independent monetary policy; it must give up one.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence – one country's monetary policy moves another's currency.

The Japanese yen and interest-rate differences

Between 2021 and 2024 the US Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply, while the Bank of Japan kept its rates close to zero. The yen fell from about ¥110 to over ¥150 per US dollar. Japanese car exporters reported record profits, while Japanese households faced higher prices for imported food and energy.

Ask the class
• Explain why the yen depreciated against the dollar.
• Calculate the percentage change in the value of the yen.
• Explain the consequences for Japan's inflation, growth and living standards.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Higher US interest rates gave a higher return on dollar assets, so investors (including Japanese ones) sold yen to buy dollars: the supply of yen shifted right and demand for yen shifted left.
  2. The value of ¥1 fell from $1/110 = $0.00909 to $1/150 = $0.00667, a depreciation of about 27%. (Equivalently, the dollar appreciated by (150 – 110)/110 = 36% against the yen.)
  3. Exports became cheaper for foreign buyers and imports dearer for Japanese buyers: net exports rose, shifting AD right (higher growth, lower unemployment).
  4. Dearer imported energy and food raised firms' costs: SRAS shifted left (cost-push inflation). Real incomes of households fell, reducing living standards for many, even though exporters gained.
  5. Diagrams: the market for yen priced in dollars (S¥ right / D¥ left, ER falls); AD/AS with AD right and SRAS left.
DiagramCurrency market: y-axis 'Price of yen in US$ ($ per ¥)', x-axis 'Quantity of yen'; S¥ shifts right (and D¥ left), ER falls from ER1 to ER2. Then AD/AS: AD1 → AD2 (net exports), SRAS1 → SRAS2 (imported costs); price level rises, real GDP effect depends on relative shifts.
Scenario 2 · Choice – exchange rates change the relative prices consumers face.

Converting prices: an Australian student buying a textbook

A textbook costs £40 in the UK and US$55 in the USA. The exchange rates are £1 = A$1.95 and US$1 = A$1.52. Shipping is the same from both countries.

Ask the class
• Calculate the price in A$ from each source.
• Which is cheaper?
• If the A$ appreciates by 10% against the US$ only, does the answer change?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. UK price in A$ = £40 × 1.95 = A$78.
  2. US price in A$ = US$55 × 1.52 = A$83.60. The UK book is cheaper.
  3. A 10% appreciation of the A$ against the US$ means A$1 buys 10% more US$. Before: A$1 = US$0.658; after: A$1 = US$0.724, so US$1 = A$1.382.
  4. New US price in A$ = 55 × 1.382 ≈ A$76.00, which is now cheaper than the UK price (A$78).
  5. This shows how an appreciation makes imports cheaper and can switch spending between suppliers.
DiagramMarket for A$ priced in US$: D$ shifts right (e.g. stronger demand for Australian exports), ER rises by 10%.
Scenario 3 · Intervention – the trade-off between stability and policy independence.

Holding a peg: the Hong Kong dollar

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) keeps the HK$ between 7.75 and 7.85 per US$ (around the HK$7.80 peg since 1983, p. 497). When US interest rates rose above Hong Kong rates, investors sold HK$ for US$ and the HK$ reached the weak end of the band (7.85).

Ask the class
• Explain, with a diagram, what the HKMA must do at 7.85.
• What happens to Hong Kong interest rates and why?
• What is the main cost of maintaining the peg?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Selling HK$ increases the supply of HK$ (S shifts right), pushing its value down to the weak edge of the band.
  2. The HKMA must buy HK$ with its US$ reserves, shifting demand for HK$ right so the rate stays at 7.85. This reduces the amount of HK$ in the banking system.
  3. With fewer HK$ available, Hong Kong interbank interest rates rise towards US rates, which stops the outflow. Hong Kong effectively imports US monetary policy.
  4. Cost: Hong Kong cannot set interest rates to suit its own economy (e.g. during a property slump); it also needs very large reserves.
  5. Benefit: certainty for trade and finance in one of the world's most open economies – the reason the peg has lasted for decades.
DiagramFixed/managed exchange rate diagram (Hoang Figs 31.20 and 31.22): y-axis 'Price of HK$ in US$', band limits drawn as horizontal lines; S shifts right, then the HKMA's purchases shift D right to keep the rate at the lower limit.
Scenario 4 · Economic well-being and equity – the distribution of the gains and losses from floating.

Nigeria's naira: from multiple rates to a more flexible float (HL)

For years Nigeria's central bank kept the official naira rate far above the parallel-market rate, rationing US dollars. In 2023 it unified its exchange rates and let the naira float more freely; the official rate fell from about ₦460 to over ₦1,400 per US$ within a year. Inflation rose above 30%, but the dollar shortages that had hit importers began to ease.

Ask the class
• Why was the naira overvalued before 2023?
• Evaluate the decision to move to a more flexible exchange rate.
• Which stakeholders gained and which lost?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The official rate was above the market-clearing rate, so the quantity of US$ demanded at that rate exceeded the quantity supplied: a shortage of foreign exchange, rationing and a parallel market.
  2. Floating removes the shortage: the rate falls to equilibrium and foreign investors gain confidence that they can take profits home, which may raise FDI and portfolio inflows.
  3. Costs: a large depreciation raises import prices (fuel, food, medicines) → cost-push inflation and lower real incomes; firms with dollar debts face higher repayment costs.
  4. Winners: exporters, remittance recipients (each dollar buys more naira), foreign investors. Losers: importers, consumers of imported goods, those on fixed incomes.
  5. Judgement: in the long run a market-based rate improves allocation and removes corruption linked to rationing, but only if it is supported by credible monetary policy to control inflation – linking to the fixed vs floating debate.
DiagramMarket for naira priced in US$: an official rate drawn above equilibrium shows excess supply of naira (shortage of dollars); after the float, the rate falls to the equilibrium. Then AD/AS showing SRAS shifting left.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Floating exchange rate determination SL/HL

Fig 31.3, p. 487

  1. Label the y-axis 'Price of [currency A] in [currency B]' (e.g. £ per A$) and the x-axis 'Quantity of [currency A]'; use 'ER' if no number is given (p. 487).
  2. Draw a downward-sloping demand curve DA$ (foreign buyers of A$) and an upward-sloping supply curve SA$ (Australians selling A$).
  3. Mark the equilibrium exchange rate ERe and quantity Qe.
  4. Name the currency on every curve label.
Common errorsLeaving the axis as just 'Price'; putting the wrong currency on the x-axis; labelling curves simply D and S without naming the currency.

Appreciation and depreciation in a floating system SL/HL

Figs 31.4–31.5, pp. 487–488

  1. Start from the equilibrium diagram.
  2. Appreciation: shift DA$ right (e.g. more exports or inward FDI) or SA$ left; the ER rises from ER1 to ER2.
  3. Depreciation: shift SA$ right (e.g. more imports or outward investment) or DA$ left; the ER falls.
  4. Write the cause next to the shift arrow and state the effect on the other currency (it moves the opposite way).
Common errorsShifting both curves without a reason; forgetting that one currency's appreciation is the other's depreciation; using 'devaluation' for a floating currency.

AD/AS consequences of a change in the exchange rate SL/HL

Figs 31.12–31.16, pp. 494–495

  1. Label axes: Average price level and Real GDP; draw AD, SRAS and LRAS (new classical) or a Keynesian AS curve.
  2. Depreciation: shift AD right (net exports rise); show the higher price level (demand-pull) and higher real GDP.
  3. Add a leftward SRAS shift for dearer imported inputs (cost-push) where relevant (Fig 31.16).
  4. Appreciation: shift AD left (net exports fall); show lower inflationary pressure and lower real GDP; optionally SRAS right as imported inputs become cheaper.
Common errorsShowing AS shifting for the net export effect; ignoring the cost-push channel; claiming the current account always improves after a depreciation without mentioning elasticities.

Maintaining a fixed exchange rate (devaluation and revaluation pressure) SL/HL

Fig 31.18, p. 498; Fig 31.20, p. 499

  1. Label axes: Price of RMB in US$ and Quantity of RMB; draw a horizontal dashed line at the fixed rate (FR).
  2. Upward pressure: D shifts right; to hold FR, the central bank sells RMB and buys US$, shifting S right until equilibrium returns to FR; reserves of US$ rise.
  3. Downward pressure: S shifts right or D shifts left; the central bank sells US$ reserves to buy RMB (D shifts right) or raises interest rates; reserves fall.
  4. Show a devaluation (or revaluation) as a deliberate move of the FR line to a new level.
Common errorsDescribing the central bank 'printing' foreign currency; forgetting reserves are limited; showing a devaluation as a market-driven fall.

  3. Show the intervention (buying or selling the currency with reserves, or an interest-rate change) that returns the rate to the band limit.
  4. Label the areas where the currency would be overvalued or undervalued relative to the market rate.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – Exchange-rate market

In floating mode, students apply a list of events (higher domestic interest rates, a tourism boom, outward FDI, speculation about a fall) and record which curve shifts and the new rate. In fixed mode they choose a target rate and, after each shock, enter the central bank's purchase or sale of reserves; the reserve balance is tracked. In managed mode they set a ±3% band and intervene only when the rate leaves it. The class compares how many reserves each regime used over the same list of shocks.

Debrief
• Which regime needed the most reserves and why?
• What could the central bank use instead of reserves?
• When did the managed band avoid intervention altogether?

Foreign-exchange pit market (classroom card game)

Students receive role cards (exporter, importer, tourist, MNC, migrant worker, pension fund, speculator) and tokens of two currencies. In each 4-minute round they trade to meet their card's target, and the teacher records the rate of each trade on the board. Between rounds, the teacher reads news cards that change targets (interest-rate rise, recession abroad, rumour of devaluation). In the final round, one student acts as a central bank defending a fixed rate with limited reserves.

Debrief
• Which news changed the rate most and why?
• Which roles supplied the currency and which demanded it?
• Could the central bank hold the fixed rate? What would have helped?

FRED / Trading Economics exchange-rate data · https://fred.stlouisfed.org

Students download monthly exchange rates (e.g. JPY/USD, GBP/USD, and their own currency) and the relevant policy interest rates for five years. They chart them together in Google Sheets and mark periods when the interest-rate gap widened. Each pair writes a paragraph linking one change in the exchange rate to a determinant, with the percentage change calculated.

Debrief
• Do interest-rate differences explain all movements?
• Which other determinant best explains the unexplained moves?
• Why are exchange rates so volatile compared with goods prices?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. EPQ 31.1 (p. 488): £1 = $1.35. A car costs $35,500 in the USA. What is the price in £?

Solution£ price = $35,500 ÷ 1.35 = £26,296.30 (about £26,296).

Problem 2. EPQ 31.2 (p. 488): $1 = £0.65. A concert ticket costs $65. What is the price in £? If the US$ depreciates to $1 = £0.60, what is the new £ price?

Solution$65 × 0.65 = £42.25. After the depreciation: $65 × 0.60 = £39.00. The ticket is £3.25 cheaper for British buyers.

Problem 3. EPQ 31.3 (p. 488): $1 = ¥6.5; a textbook costs $65. Find the price in ¥. Also, C$1 = £0.65 = €0.75. Find the value of £1 in €.

Solution$65 × 6.5 = ¥422.50. Cross rate: £0.65 = €0.75, so £1 = 0.75 ÷ 0.65 = €1.154 (to 3 d.p.).

Problem 4. EPQ 31.4 (p. 489): the market shows £1 = $1.587. Find the value of $1 in £.

Solution$1 = 1 ÷ 1.587 = £0.630.

Problem 5. EPQ 31.5 data (p. 494): the price of KWD in NGN rises from 900 to 1,150 naira per dinar. Calculate the percentage appreciation of the KWD and the percentage depreciation of the NGN.

SolutionKWD: (1,150 – 900) ÷ 900 × 100 = +27.8%. NGN: value of ₦1 was 1/900 = 0.001111 KWD; now 1/1,150 = 0.000870 KWD; change = (0.000870 – 0.001111) ÷ 0.001111 × 100 = –21.7%. The two percentages differ because they use different base values.

Problem 6. A Thai hotel room costs ฿3,600 a night. The exchange rate changes from €1 = ฿36 to €1 = ฿40. Calculate the room price in € before and after, and the percentage change in the value of the baht against the euro.

SolutionBefore: 3,600 ÷ 36 = €100. After: 3,600 ÷ 40 = €90. Value of ฿1: €1/36 = €0.02778 → €1/40 = €0.025, a change of –10%: the baht has depreciated by 10% (the euro appreciated by 11.1%), so Thai tourism becomes cheaper for Europeans.

Problem 7. EPQ 31.7 (p. 504, HL): foreign currency reserves in June 2020 were China $3,101,700m and USA $128,802m. (a) Calculate US reserves as a percentage of China's. (b) Explain one reason for the difference. HL

Solution(a) 128,802 ÷ 3,101,700 × 100 = 4.15%. (b) China has managed the RMB against the dollar and run large current account surpluses, buying US$ to limit appreciation; the USA issues the world's main reserve currency and allows the dollar to float, so it needs few foreign reserves.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Appreciation and revaluation mean the same thing – appreciation happens through market forces in a floating system; revaluation is a deliberate decision in a fixed system (the same applies to depreciation and devaluation).
  • A strong currency is always good – an appreciation lowers inflation and raises purchasing power abroad, but it can hurt exporters, growth and employment.
  • A depreciation always improves the current account – only if demand for exports and imports is sufficiently price elastic; in the short run the balance may worsen (J-curve).
  • Supply of a currency comes from the central bank printing money – in the forex market, supply comes from residents selling their currency to buy foreign currency (imports, outward investment).
  • Fixed and managed exchange rates are the same – a fixed rate requires continuous matching intervention; a managed rate allows movement within a band (p. 502).
  • Higher inflation makes a currency appreciate because prices are higher – higher relative inflation makes exports less competitive, lowering demand for the currency and raising its supply, so it depreciates.
  • When calculating percentage changes, the change in one currency equals the opposite change in the other – the percentages differ because the base values are different.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 496: the book says an appreciation worsens the current account if the PED for exports and the PED for imports are each greater than one. The correct condition (the Marshall–Lerner condition) is that the sum of the two elasticities must be greater than one. Also mention the J-curve: in the short run, demand is inelastic.
  • p. 494 (Fig 31.10): the y-axis is labelled 'Price of KWD per NGN'. The values (900 → 1,150) are naira per dinar, so the label should read 'Price of KWD in NGN' (NGN per KWD).
  • p. 500: a 'crawling peg' is described simply as a currency kept within two bands (the Vietnamese dong ±3%). A crawling peg is a peg that is adjusted gradually over time; the ±3% limit is a band around it. Vietnam's central rate is adjusted daily with a band (widened to ±5% in 2022), so this is a crawling band. Update the example.
  • p. 503: the claim that central banks buying currencies 'crowds out' currency for private investors is not a standard argument. Present currency liquidity mainly as the cost and limit of holding large reserves.
  • p. 504: fixed exchange rates are described as 'very rare' today, with Hong Kong as the closest example in developed economies. Many countries still peg (e.g. Denmark to the euro, Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states to the US$, the CFA franc zone). Free floats and pegs both remain common, and many regimes are in between.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Define an exchange rate.

The price of one currency expressed in terms of another.

2. How should the axes of a currency market diagram be labelled?

y-axis: price of currency A in currency B (e.g. £ per A$); x-axis: quantity of currency A.

3. Which curve shifts, and which way, when a country's exports increase?

Demand for its currency shifts right, so it appreciates.

4. How do higher domestic interest rates affect the currency?

They attract hot money/portfolio inflows, so demand for the currency rises and it appreciates.

5. £1 = $1.25. What does a $100 jacket cost in £?

$100 ÷ 1.25 = £80.

6. The rate changes from €1 = $1.10 to €1 = $1.21. By what percentage has the euro appreciated?

(1.21 – 1.10) ÷ 1.10 × 100 = 10%.

7. Give two effects of a depreciation on the economy.

Any two of: net exports and AD rise (growth, jobs), demand-pull and cost-push inflation, current account may improve, living standards may fall as imports cost more.

8. When does a depreciation improve the current account?

When demand for exports and imports is sufficiently price elastic (Marshall–Lerner condition).

9. Distinguish devaluation from depreciation.

Devaluation is a deliberate lowering of a fixed rate by the authorities; depreciation is a market-driven fall in a floating rate.

10. How does a central bank stop its fixed currency from falling?

It sells foreign currency reserves to buy its own currency, and/or raises interest rates.

11. What is an overvalued currency?

A currency whose value is above its long-run market equilibrium, which makes exports uncompetitive.

12. How does a managed exchange rate differ from a fixed rate?

The central bank intervenes only occasionally to keep the rate within a band, rather than holding it at one rate all the time.

13. (HL) Give one advantage of a fixed exchange rate and one of a floating exchange rate.

Fixed: certainty for trade and investment. Floating: independent monetary policy and automatic adjustment without reserves.

14. (HL) Why can a peg become a target for speculators?

Speculators know the rate must be defended; if they believe reserves are insufficient, they sell heavily, hoping to profit from a forced devaluation (e.g. Thailand 1997).
