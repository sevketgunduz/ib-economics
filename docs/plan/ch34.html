Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 34

Measuring development

Syllabus 4.8 Measuring developmentTextbook pp. 542–563Sessions 4 HL · 3 SLConcepts economic well-being, equity, sustainability, change

Why is economic development uneven – and how can we tell whether a country is really developing rather than just growing?

All IB content in 4.8 is common SL/HL. Sessions 1–3 cover every syllabus point for both levels, including a compact treatment of the growth–development relationship and the strengths and limitations of measures in session 3. Session 4 is marked HL as an HL-only extension (deeper evaluation of measurement approaches and a data investigation); SL classes may run it as an optional consolidation session.

Key terms and definitions

Economic development A multidimensional process of improving living standards that includes reductions in poverty, inequality and unemployment and improvements in health, education, freedom and choice.
Economic growth An increase in real GDP (or real GDP per capita) over time; a quantitative change in output that may or may not bring development.
Goulet's core values Denis Goulet's (1971) three components of development: life sustenance (basic needs), self-esteem (dignity) and freedom (from servitude and to make choices).
Single indicator A measure of development that uses one statistic, such as GNI per capita, life expectancy or the literacy rate.
GDP per capita The value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year divided by the population.
GNI per capita Gross national income (GDP plus net factor income from abroad) divided by the population; a measure of average income of a country's residents.
Purchasing power parity (PPP) An exchange rate that equalizes the purchasing power of different currencies, so that the same basket of goods costs the same in each country; used to compare real living standards.
Life expectancy at birth The average number of years a newborn is expected to live if current mortality patterns stay the same.
Under-five mortality rate The number of children who die before their fifth birthday per 1,000 live births.
Mean years of schooling The average number of years of education received by people aged 25 and over.
Expected years of schooling The number of years of schooling a child of school-entrance age can expect to receive if current enrolment rates persist.
Gini coefficient A measure of income inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality), derived from the Lorenz curve.
Gross fixed capital formation (GFCF) Investment in fixed assets such as machinery, buildings and infrastructure, often expressed as a percentage of GDP.
Energy indicators Measures of access to affordable, reliable and modern energy, such as the percentage of the population with electricity or the share of renewables in energy use.
Environmental indicators Measures of environmental quality and sustainability, such as CO2 emissions per capita, forest cover, municipal solid waste and biodiversity.
Composite indicator An index that combines several single indicators into one number, such as the HDI.
Human Development Index (HDI) A UNDP composite index from 0 to 1 combining a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (GNI per capita at PPP).
Gender Inequality Index (GII) A UNDP index of gender-based disadvantage in reproductive health (maternal mortality, adolescent births), empowerment (parliamentary seats, secondary education) and the labour market; 0 means equality.
Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI) The HDI adjusted for inequality in the distribution of each dimension across the population; the gap between HDI and IHDI measures the loss due to inequality.
Happy Planet Index (HPI) An index of sustainable well-being calculated as well-being × life expectancy × inequality of outcomes, divided by the ecological footprint.
Maternal mortality ratio The number of women who die from pregnancy-related causes per 100,000 live births.
Adolescent fertility rate The number of births per 1,000 women aged 15–19.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being

Two Gulf economies: rich but developed?

Qatar ranks first in the world for GDP per capita at PPP (about $139,000 in Table 34.1, p. 545) but its HDI rank is much lower than its income rank. A large share of the population are migrant workers on low wages, and CO2 emissions per capita are among the highest in the world. Kuwait also has a higher GNI per capita than the USA yet a lower HDI (p. 556).

Ask the class
• Why might high income per capita overstate development?
• Which other indicators would you use?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. GDP per capita is an average; it hides how income is distributed between citizens and migrant workers (inequality).
  2. Income from oil may be high while education outcomes lag, lowering the education dimension of the HDI.
  3. Income measures ignore environmental costs – high emissions per capita would lower the Happy Planet Index.
  4. Use IHDI, GII, CO2 per capita and HPI to give a fuller picture; conclude that income is necessary but not sufficient for development.
DiagramPPC (Fig 34.15): P → R, growth without development, if extra output goes to goods that do not raise welfare for most people.
Scenario 2 · Economic well-being

Sierra Leone's health spending paradox

Sierra Leone spends over 13% of GDP on health (Table 34.3, p. 547) – close to many rich countries – yet has one of the world's highest under-five mortality rates (105 per 1,000, Table 34.2) and a life expectancy of about 54. Its GDP per capita is under $600.

Ask the class
• Explain why high health spending as a share of GDP does not guarantee good health outcomes.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A percentage of a small GDP is a small absolute amount per person – spending per capita is very low.
  2. Much spending may be out-of-pocket or donor-funded and poorly targeted; weak infrastructure, few doctors and the legacy of the Ebola epidemic limit effectiveness.
  3. Health also depends on clean water, sanitation, nutrition and education, not just spending.
  4. So an input indicator (spending) is a weak proxy for an outcome indicator (mortality); use both.
DiagramNo formal diagram; a scatter chart of health spending per capita against life expectancy (Gapminder).
Scenario 3 · Equity

Botswana vs Costa Rica: which measure tells the truth?

Botswana and Costa Rica have broadly similar GNI per capita at PPP. Botswana's growth has been driven by diamonds, but it has a very high Gini coefficient (around 0.53) and a large IHDI loss. Costa Rica has lower inequality, near-universal electricity from renewables and one of the highest Happy Planet Index scores in the world.

Ask the class
• Compare development in the two countries using at least three indicators.
• Evaluate the usefulness of the HPI.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Similar income per capita suggests similar living standards, but single income measures ignore distribution.
  2. Botswana's high Gini and IHDI loss show that growth benefits are concentrated; Costa Rica's lower loss suggests more inclusive development.
  3. Costa Rica's HPI reflects high well-being and life expectancy with a low ecological footprint.
  4. Evaluate the HPI – captures sustainability, but relies on subjective survey data on well-being and ignores human rights or freedom.
  5. Conclude that a dashboard of indicators gives a more reliable picture than any single index.
DiagramLorenz curves for both countries (Course Simulator – Lorenz curve & Gini builder), with Botswana's curve further from the line of equality.
Scenario 4 · Equity

Growing apart: the income gap (EPQ 34.4)

Country A has income per capita of $2,000 and Country B $35,000. Both grow at 2.5% a year (Hoang p. 561). Country A's leaders argue that equal growth rates mean convergence.

Ask the class
• Calculate the change in the absolute income gap after one year.
• Does equal percentage growth reduce global inequality?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A's income rises by $50 to $2,050; B's by $875 to $35,875.
  2. The gap rises from $33,000 to $33,825 – an increase of $825.
  3. The ratio of incomes stays at 17.5, but the absolute gap widens; convergence requires the poorer country to grow faster.
  4. Link to development: faster growth is needed in ELDCs, but only if its benefits are widely shared.
DiagramNo diagram; a simple line chart of both incomes over 20 years in Google Sheets.

Diagrams: how to draw them

PPC: relationship between economic growth and economic development SL/HL

Fig 34.15, p. 562

  1. Label axes, e.g. 'Staple foods (merit goods)' on the vertical axis and 'Defence goods' on the horizontal axis.
  2. Draw the original PPC1 and mark point P on it.
  3. Show P → Q: a movement along PPC1 towards more staple foods – development without growth.
  4. Draw PPC2 outward and mark R with more defence goods but no more staple foods – growth without development.
  5. Mark S on PPC2 with more of both – growth with development.
Common errorsShowing development as an outward shift only; forgetting that a movement along the PPC can represent development; unlabelled axes.

Simulation and data activities

Gapminder Tools · gapminder.org/tools

Project a bubble chart of income per person (PPP) against life expectancy and press play from 1950 to today. Students track three countries (e.g. China, Nigeria, Rwanda) and note when income rose without a matching rise in life expectancy or vice versa. Pairs then change the vertical axis to an education or CO2 indicator and repeat. Each pair presents one surprising finding.

Debrief
• Is the relationship between income and health linear?
• Which country developed with relatively little growth?
• What does the CO2 chart suggest about sustainability?

UNDP Human Development Reports data centre · hdr.undp.org/data-center

Students download HDI, IHDI and GII values for five countries of their choice. In Google Sheets they calculate the IHDI loss (%) and rank the countries by HDI and by IHDI. They then compute a simplified HDI for one country using the published goalposts to see how the index is built.

Debrief
• Which country loses most from inequality, and why?
• Why does the HDI use a geometric rather than an arithmetic mean?
• What value judgements are built into the choice of goalposts?

Course Simulator – Lorenz curve & Gini builder

Students enter quintile income shares for two countries of similar GNI per capita (from World Bank data) and compare the resulting Gini coefficients. They then adjust shares to model a redistribution policy and observe the change.

Debrief
• How can two countries with the same average income have very different living standards?
• What does the Gini coefficient not tell us?

OECD Better Life Index · oecdbetterlifeindex.org

Students set their own weights across 11 well-being dimensions and see how country rankings change. Compare rankings produced by different students.

Debrief
• Why did rankings change when weights changed?
• Is there an objective way to weight dimensions of development?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. EPQ 34.1 (p. 546): 1 dem = 3.5 dans at PPP. A Big Mac costs 3.5 dems. What is its PPP price in dans?

Solution3.5 × 3.5 = 12.25 dans.

Problem 2. EPQ 34.2 (p. 546): real GNI rises from $50bn to $54bn while population rises from 68m to 71m. Calculate the change in real GNI per capita.

SolutionYear 1: 50,000m ÷ 68m ≈ $735. Year 2: 54,000m ÷ 71m ≈ $761. Increase ≈ $25 (about 3.4%).

Problem 3. EPQ 34.4 (p. 561): Country A has income per capita $2,000 and Country B $35,000; both grow by 2.5%. Calculate the change in the income gap.

SolutionA: 2,000 × 1.025 = $2,050. B: 35,000 × 1.025 = $35,875. Gap: 35,875 − 2,050 = $33,825 versus $33,000 before – the gap widens by $825.

Problem 4. A country has an HDI of 0.80 and an IHDI of 0.68. Calculate the percentage loss due to inequality.

SolutionLoss = (0.80 − 0.68) ÷ 0.80 × 100 = 15%.

Problem 5. Simplified HDI dimension index: life expectancy is 70 years; the UNDP goalposts are 20 (minimum) and 85 (maximum). Calculate the health index.

Solution(70 − 20) ÷ (85 − 20) = 50 ÷ 65 ≈ 0.769.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Economic growth and economic development are the same – growth is an increase in real output; development is a broader improvement in well-being (CM, p. 561).
  • High GNI per capita guarantees a high HDI – Kuwait, Qatar and Liechtenstein have higher GNI per capita than the USA but lower HDI (CM, p. 556).
  • PPP figures are always higher than nominal figures – they are higher for most low-income countries but can be lower for high-cost countries such as Switzerland or Norway.
  • A high share of GDP spent on health means good health outcomes – Sierra Leone spends over 13% of GDP but has very high child mortality (p. 547).
  • Female labour force participation alone measures gender equality – the top countries are often low-income countries where women work out of necessity (p. 557).
  • The HDI captures inequality – it uses national averages; the IHDI adjusts for inequality.
  • GDP per capita measures all economic activity – it misses the informal (parallel) economy, which is large in many ELDCs (TT, p. 545).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Rwanda inconsistency (Ch34 vs Ch36): the Ch34 case study on gender equality in Rwanda (p. 557, Tables 34.7–34.8) presents Rwanda as a world leader for women in parliament (61%) and female labour force participation (84.16%), while Ch36 (p. 623) cites Ghana and Rwanda as examples of countries that fail to empower women and promote gender equality. Tell students that Rwanda scores highly on political representation and participation but less well on other gender measures (e.g. GII components, access to credit and land); avoid using p. 623 as evidence without qualification.
  • HDI figures for Norway differ within the chapter: 0.953 on p. 555 and 0.954 in Table 34.9 (p. 558), reflecting different HDR editions. Always state the year and source of HDI data in answers.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Distinguish economic growth from economic development.

Growth is an increase in real GDP; development is a multidimensional improvement in living standards, including health, education, freedom and reduced poverty and inequality.

2. Name Goulet's three core values.

Life sustenance, self-esteem and freedom.

3. Why use PPP when comparing incomes?

It adjusts for differences in the cost of living, so comparisons reflect what incomes can actually buy.

4. Why might GNI per capita differ from GDP per capita?

GNI includes net factor income from abroad, e.g. profits paid to foreign firms or income earned by residents abroad.

5. Give two health indicators.

Life expectancy at birth and under-five mortality rate (also maternal mortality, health spending % of GDP).

6. What does a Gini coefficient of 0.25 indicate?

Relatively low income inequality.

7. Name one energy and one environmental indicator.

Share of population with access to electricity; CO2 emissions per capita (or forest cover, municipal solid waste).

8. What are the three dimensions of the HDI?

A long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living.

9. What does the gap between the HDI and IHDI show?

The loss in human development due to inequality in health, education and income.

10. Name the three dimensions of the GII.

Reproductive health, empowerment and labour market participation.

11. What does the HPI divide by, and why?

The ecological footprint, to measure how efficiently well-being is achieved relative to environmental resources used.

12. On a PPC, how do you show development without growth?

A movement along the same PPC towards more merit goods or basic necessities (P to Q).

13. HDI = 0.90, IHDI = 0.81. Calculate the loss due to inequality.

(0.90 − 0.81) ÷ 0.90 = 10%.

14. Give one limitation of composite indicators.

Weights are arbitrary and averages hide distribution; data may be unreliable in low-income countries.
