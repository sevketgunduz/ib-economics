Unit 4 · The global economy · Hoang Chapter 29

Arguments for and against trade control/protection

Syllabus 4.3 Arguments for and against trade control/protectionTextbook pp. 455–468Sessions 2 HL · 2 SLConcepts intervention, interdependence, economic well-being, sustainability, equity

Who are the winners and losers of the integration of the world's economies? If most economists favour free trade, why do almost all governments protect some industries?

Whole chapter is common SL/HL: ten arguments for protection (AO2), seven arguments against (AO2) and the evaluation of free trade versus trade protection (AO3). There are no HL-only items and no required diagrams or calculations; students reuse the Ch28 tariff, quota and subsidy diagrams to support their arguments.

Session plans

Session 1: The case for protection: ten arguments SL/HL pp. 455–462

Objectives
  • Students will be able to explain the ten IB arguments for trade protection with a real-world example for each.
  • Students will be able to distinguish economic arguments (infant industry, anti-dumping, ELDC diversification) from political or non-economic arguments (national security, some job-protection claims).
  • Students will be able to identify a weakness of each argument as a first step towards evaluation.
TimePhaseActivity

Ask students to rank the two arguments from 'most convincing' to 'least convincing' and justify in one sentence.
20 minKey terms & direct instructionTeach the ten arguments in four clusters: (1) development: infant industries (Hong Kong film quotas, p. 456) and ELDC diversification (p. 461); (2) non-economic: national security (arms embargoes, p. 457), health and safety (Chinese electronics, p. 457), environmental standards (Volkswagen case study, p. 457); (3) fairness: anti-dumping (China's 2018 probe into chlorobutyl rubber, p. 458) and unfair competition (p. 459); (4) macro/fiscal: balance of payments correction (p. 459), government revenue (Table 29.1, p. 460) and jobs (p. 460). For each, give one-line counter-arguments so students see that evaluation starts here.

The panel scores each pitch for economic reasoning (not just emotion) and asks one tough question.

Session 2: The case against protection and free trade versus protection SL/HL pp. 462–468

Objectives
  • Students will be able to explain the seven arguments against protection using the PC CRIME framework.
  • Students will be able to explain how retaliation can escalate into a trade war.
  • Students will be able to evaluate free trade against protection by weighing stakeholders, time frames, the type of good and the size of the protected industry.
TimePhaseActivity

Key terms and definitions

Trade protection The use of tariff and non-tariff barriers to shield domestic producers from foreign competition.
Infant (sunrise) industry A new domestic industry that may develop a comparative advantage in the future but cannot yet compete with established foreign firms, because it lacks economies of scale and experience.
Dynamic comparative advantage Comparative advantage that develops over time through investment, learning and scale; the infant-industry argument claims that free trade can lock a country into its current (static) advantage.
National security argument The claim that strategic industries (defence, energy, food, key technology) should be protected so the country is not dependent on potentially hostile suppliers in war or crisis.
Embargo A ban on trade with a particular country or in a particular good, used for security, political or health reasons.
Dumping Selling goods in a foreign market below their cost of production (or below the price in the home market), often supported by subsidies.
Anti-dumping measures Tariffs or other barriers imposed on dumped imports, permitted under WTO rules if dumping and injury to domestic producers can be shown.
Unfair competition The argument that foreign firms gain an advantage from lower standards or government support (e.g. subsidies, weak labour or environmental rules, low corporate taxes), so protection 'levels the playing field'.
Balance of payments correction Using protection to reduce import spending and so reduce a current account deficit; it treats a symptom rather than the causes of low competitiveness.
Current account deficit When the value of a country's exports of goods and services plus income and transfer credits is less than the value of its imports plus income and transfer debits in a period.
Economically least developed countries (ELDCs) Low-income countries with severe structural barriers to development, low human capital and high vulnerability to economic shocks.
Diversification Widening the range of goods a country produces and exports (e.g. from primary commodities into manufacturing) to reduce dependence on a few volatile, price-inelastic exports.
Misallocation of resources Protection moves resources into industries where the country lacks a comparative advantage, causing a net loss of social surplus (the welfare-loss triangles).
Retaliation A country responding to trade barriers imposed on its exports by introducing barriers of its own.
Trade war An escalating series of reciprocal trade barriers between countries, which reduces trade and harms both sides.
Regressive effect of tariffs Tariffs take a larger proportion of the income of low-income households, who spend a higher share of their income on traded goods.
X-inefficiency Higher-than-necessary costs in firms that face little competition and so have little incentive to control costs or innovate.
Export competitiveness Domestic firms' ability to sell successfully abroad; protection of inputs raises costs and can reduce it.
Offshoring Firms moving production overseas, which can happen when protection raises the cost of domestic inputs.
Non-tariff barriers All forms of protection other than tariffs, such as quotas, subsidies and administrative barriers.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Economic well-being and change – protection as a development strategy.

Ethiopia's infant garment and leather industries

Ethiopia has tried to move from coffee and other primary exports into manufacturing through industrial parks. Suppose the government proposes a 30% tariff on imported shoes for eight years to give domestic factories time to reach scale, while coffee still earns about a third of export revenue.

Ask the class
• Which arguments for protection support this policy?
• What are the risks?
• How could the government design the tariff to reduce those risks?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Infant-industry argument: Ethiopian firms have higher average costs because they have not yet reached economies of scale. A temporary tariff raises the domestic price so they can survive while they learn.
  2. ELDC diversification argument: dependence on coffee exposes the economy to volatile, price-inelastic commodity prices; manufacturing offers more stable earnings and higher value added.
  3. Diagram: tariff diagram – domestic output rises and imports fall; consumers pay more for shoes.
  4. Risks: the industry may never 'grow up' (lack of incentive, X-inefficiency), consumers on low incomes pay more (regressive), trading partners may retaliate and resources are misallocated in the short run.
  5. Design: a time-limited, falling tariff with performance targets (e.g. export goals), combined with supply-side policies (training, infrastructure), makes the infant-industry case stronger. Judgement depends on whether Ethiopia can realistically develop a dynamic comparative advantage in footwear.
DiagramTariff diagram (Ch28, Fig 28.2) for imported shoes: Sworld and Sworld+tariff; show the rise in domestic output and fall in imports; label consumer surplus loss and welfare loss.
Scenario 2 · Interdependence – protection in one industry raises costs in others.

Anti-dumping duties on Chinese steel

The EU imposes anti-dumping duties on some Chinese steel products, arguing that state support lets Chinese firms sell below cost. EU car and construction firms buy large amounts of steel. China argues the duties are disguised protectionism.

Ask the class
• Explain the anti-dumping argument.
• Which stakeholders in the EU lose from the duties?
• Why is it difficult to set the 'correct' duty?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. If imports are priced below cost because of subsidies, domestic producers may be driven out even if they are efficient; once they have gone, foreign firms could raise prices. Anti-dumping duties aim to restore fair competition, and the WTO allows them if dumping and injury are shown.
  2. Losers include EU firms that use steel (car makers, construction), whose costs rise, and their customers. This is the 'increased costs' and 'reduced export competitiveness' part of PC CRIME.
  3. Measuring the true cost of production, the size of the foreign subsidy and the relevant elasticities is very hard, so any duty may over- or under-correct (Hoang's $2 subsidy/$2 tariff illustration, pp. 458–459).
  4. Judgement: anti-dumping has a stronger economic case than most protection, but it is easily captured by domestic lobbies.
DiagramTariff diagram with the world supply line drawn at the dumped price and a duty that raises it towards the 'fair' price; show the cost increase to downstream firms in words.
Scenario 3 · Interdependence – actions in one economy trigger responses in others.

The US–China trade war

From 2018 the USA imposed tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods, and China retaliated with tariffs on US soybeans, pork and cars (p. 463). US farmers lost sales to China, and the US government paid them billions of dollars in support. Studies suggest US importers and consumers paid most of the tariff cost.

Ask the class
• Use this case to explain retaliation and a trade war.
• Why did the tariffs not simply transfer money from Chinese firms to the US government?
• Did the US current account improve? Why might it not?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Retaliation: China's counter-tariffs hit US exporters, so the protection of some US industries led to losses in others (soybeans).
  2. If the world price of Chinese goods does not fall, the tariff is passed on to US buyers: higher prices, less choice and higher input costs for US manufacturers (P, C and C in PC CRIME).
  3. Imports switched to Vietnam, Mexico and others (trade diversion), so the total US deficit did not shrink much; the current account reflects national saving and investment, not just tariffs.
  4. Farm support cost taxpayers money, an extra cost of the trade war.
  5. Judgement: short-run gains for protected industries were outweighed by wider costs, supporting Hoang's top tip (p. 467) that most economists see protection as harmful in the long run – although national security concerns about technology remain a non-economic argument that some find persuasive.
DiagramTwo linked tariff diagrams: US market for Chinese goods (tariff raises price) and US soybean exports facing a Chinese tariff (fall in export demand); or a prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix.
Scenario 4 · Intervention and equity – who bears the burden of a revenue tariff?

Revenue tariffs in a small island economy

Palau had one of the world's highest weighted mean tariffs, 34.6% (World Bank 2018, Table 29.1, p. 460). Small island economies often have limited income-tax bases and import most of what they consume. Suppose a government raises its tariff on imported cars and alcohol to fund schools.

Ask the class
• Why do small island economies rely on tariffs for government revenue?
• Why should the tariff target price-inelastic goods?
• What are the drawbacks?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Tariffs are easy to collect at a few ports, whereas income and sales taxes need administrative capacity that small economies may lack.
  2. If demand for the import is price-inelastic, quantity falls only a little, so revenue (tariff × imports) stays high; targeting luxuries bought by higher-income groups also reduces the regressive effect (p. 460).
  3. Drawbacks: higher prices, less choice and a higher cost of living; imported inputs cost more; some tariffs (e.g. on food) are regressive.
  4. Judgement: a revenue tariff on inelastic luxury or demerit goods (e.g. alcohol) may be justified, especially where there are negative externalities.
DiagramTariff diagram with steep (inelastic) demand: small fall in imports and a large revenue rectangle; compare with elastic demand.

Simulation and data activities

The Evolution of Trust (game theory) · https://ncase.me/trust

Students play the first chapters of The Evolution of Trust (about 10 minutes), noting how 'copycat' (tit-for-tat) and 'always cheat' strategies behave. Then pairs play a paper version with trade payoffs: both keep free trade 5/5; one imposes tariffs 7/2; both impose tariffs 3/3, for five rounds. Record whether each pair ended in cooperation or a trade war. Finally, discuss how the WTO's rules and dispute settlement change the payoffs.

Debrief
• Why is protection tempting for one country but harmful when both do it?
• How does the threat of retaliation encourage cooperation?
• What role does trust (and the WTO) play in keeping trade open?

Lobby day role play

Ten groups each receive one argument for protection with a real industry and country. They prepare a 90-second pitch that must include one economic mechanism and one piece of data. A panel of 'trade ministers' scores pitches for economic reasoning and must then hear a 30-second rebuttal from a 'free-trade economist' student who uses PC CRIME. The class votes on the three most convincing cases.

Debrief
• Which arguments were economic and which were political?
• Which arguments were stronger for ELDCs than for high-income countries?
• Did any pitch survive the free-trade rebuttal? Why?

World Bank Open Data / Our World in Data · https://ourworldindata.org/trade-and-globalization

Students compare trade openness and GDP per capita growth for a group of countries that liberalized (e.g. Vietnam after 1986, India after 1991) with countries that kept high barriers. They use Hoang's sub-Saharan Africa case study (openness rose from 16% to 81% of GDP between the mid-1990s and 2008, p. 462) as a starting point. Each group writes one claim and one limitation (correlation is not causation).

Debrief
• Does the data support the view that free trade promotes growth?
• What else changed at the same time as liberalization?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • All arguments for protection are equally 'economic' – national security and some job-protection claims are mainly political; infant industry and anti-dumping have a clearer economic basis.
  • Protection saves jobs overall – it may save jobs in the protected industry but cost jobs in industries that use its output as an input, and in export industries hit by retaliation.
  • Tariffs reduce a current account deficit permanently – they treat a symptom; the deficit reflects competitiveness and saving/investment, and retaliation or exchange-rate changes may cancel the effect.
  • Infant industries will automatically become competitive – without time limits and pressure to improve, they may stay inefficient and dependent on protection.
  • Only consumers lose from protection – firms that import inputs, exporters, taxpayers (for subsidies) and foreign producers in ELDCs also lose.
  • Free trade and protection are an all-or-nothing choice – real policy is about which industries, how much and for how long.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 459 (Top tip): the current account deficit is defined as imports of goods and services exceeding exports. That describes a trade deficit (balance of trade in goods and services). The current account also includes primary income and secondary income (transfers) – see Ch32. Correct this for students.
  • p. 459: the text suggests the only reason to buy a foreign currency is to pay for that country's goods or services. Demand for a currency also comes from investment (FDI, portfolio), remittances and speculation (Ch31) – point this out to avoid confusion later.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. What is an infant (sunrise) industry?

A new industry that could become competitive with time and scale but cannot yet compete with established foreign firms.

2. Define dumping.

Selling exports in a foreign market below the cost of production.

3. Give one example of protection on health and safety grounds.

US restrictions on some Chinese smartphones and appliances (p. 457), or bans on poultry imports during bird flu.

4. Why should a revenue tariff target price-inelastic imports?

Quantity falls only slightly, so tariff × imports stays high.

5. Why might ELDCs protect manufacturing?

To diversify away from volatile, price-inelastic primary exports and build a new comparative advantage.

6. What does PC CRIME stand for?

Higher Prices, less Choice, increased Costs, Retaliation, Inefficiency, Misallocation of resources, reduced Export competitiveness.

7. Explain retaliation in one sentence.

A country responds to barriers against its exports by imposing its own barriers, which can escalate into a trade war.

8. How can protection reduce export competitiveness?

Tariffs on imported inputs raise domestic firms' costs, making their exports more expensive.

9. Why are tariffs said to be regressive?

They take a larger share of the income of low-income households, who spend more of their income on traded goods.

10. Give one real example of a trade war.

The US–China trade war from 2018, or the US–EU dispute that led to the 25% tariff on Scotch whisky.

11. Name two evaluation criteria for judging protection.

Any two of: short run vs long run, affected stakeholders, type of good (externalities), size of the industry, likelihood of retaliation, political vs economic motive.

IA angle

An article in which a government justifies new trade restrictions on national security or anti-dumping grounds (e.g. restrictions on semiconductor exports, or anti-dumping duties on solar panels or steel). Commentary: analyse the argument with a tariff diagram and evaluate using PC CRIME (key concept: intervention or interdependence).

Theory of knowledge

Free trade is advocated on the grounds that it leads to greater efficiency. Yet it results in both winners and losers. Do economists have a moral responsibility toward the losers when they advocate free trade?
