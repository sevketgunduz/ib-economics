Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 16

Measuring economic activity and illustrating its variations

Syllabus 3.1 Measuring economic activity and illustrating its variationsTextbook pp. 226–242Sessions 4 HL · 3 SLConcepts economic well-being, change, sustainability, equity

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? How well does a single number such as GDP capture how well off people in a country really are?

All IB content in 3.1 is common SL/HL. Sessions 1–3 are taught to both levels. Session 4 is an HL-timetabled consolidation and extension session (GNI, real per capita and PPP calculations under Paper 3 conditions); it introduces no HL-only syllabus content, so SL students lose nothing by not attending.

Key terms and definitions

National income The total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year; it is a measure of the level of economic activity.
Output method Measures national income by adding the value of all final goods and services produced in a year, leaving out intermediate goods to avoid double counting.
Income method Measures national income by adding all factor incomes earned in producing output: wages, rent, interest and profits.
Expenditure method Measures national income by adding all spending on newly produced final output: C + I + G + (X – M).
Circular flow of income model A model showing how money flows between households and firms in exchange for factors and output, with leakages (S, T, M) leaving and injections (I, G, X) entering the flow.
Leakages (withdrawals) Money taken out of the circular flow of income: savings (S), taxes (T) and import spending (M).
Injections Money added to the circular flow of income from outside the household–firm loop: investment (I), government spending (G) and export revenue (X).
Gross domestic product (GDP) The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period, whoever owns the factors of production.
Nominal GDP GDP measured at current prices, so it has not been adjusted for inflation.
Gross national income (GNI) GDP plus net factor income from abroad; the total income earned by a country's residents and firms wherever their factors are located.
Net factor income from abroad Factor income (profits, interest, dividends, wages) earned abroad by a country's residents minus factor income earned in the country by foreign residents.
GDP deflator A price index measuring the general price level of all domestically produced output relative to a base year (= 100); it is used to convert nominal values into real values.
Real GDP / real GNI GDP or GNI adjusted for inflation, i.e. measured at constant (base-year) prices, so it shows changes in the volume of output.
Real GDP (GNI) per capita Real GDP (or GNI) divided by the population; a proxy for average income and the average standard of living.
Purchasing power parity (PPP) An exchange rate at which the same basket of goods and services costs the same in two countries; PPP-adjusted figures remove cost-of-living differences from international comparisons.
Business cycle The pattern of short-term fluctuations in real GDP (boom, peak, recession, trough, recovery) around a rising long-term growth trend.
Recession A fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters (negative economic growth).
Potential output The real GDP an economy could produce if all its resources were fully and efficiently employed; its growth is shown by the long-term trend line.
Shadow (informal) economy Economic activity that is not reported to the authorities, for example to avoid tax, so it is not recorded in official GDP.
Green GDP GDP adjusted by subtracting the estimated cost of environmental damage and resource depletion caused by production.
OECD Better Life Index (BLI) A composite measure launched by the OECD in 2011 that rates countries on 11 dimensions of material living conditions and quality of life, with weights users can choose.
Happiness Index (World Happiness Report) A UN SDSN ranking based on people's own rating of their lives on a 0–10 scale, explained by factors such as income, healthy life expectancy, social support and freedom.
Happy Planet Index (HPI) A New Economics Foundation measure of how efficiently countries deliver long, happy lives: well-being × life expectancy × an inequality adjustment, divided by ecological footprint.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Interdependence

UK car plants and the GDP–GNI gap

Toyota, Honda and Nissan have built large car plants in the UK. The cars count in UK GDP, but much of the profit is sent back to Japan. Suppose UK GDP is £2,200bn, UK residents earn £95bn of factor income abroad, and foreign owners earn £110bn in the UK.

Ask the class
• Calculate UK GNI.
• Why is GNI below GDP here?
• Which measure better shows the income available to UK residents?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Net factor income from abroad = £95bn – £110bn = –£15bn.
  2. GNI = GDP + net factor income = £2,200bn – £15bn = £2,185bn.
  3. GDP counts all production inside the UK. GNI counts income owned by UK residents, so profits sent back to Japanese parent firms are taken off.
  4. For the income UK households can actually spend, GNI is the better measure. For the level of activity (and jobs) inside the UK, GDP is better.
  5. On the circular flow, repatriated profits are a leakage abroad from the domestic flow.
DiagramCircular flow of income model: households, firms, government, financial sector and the rest of the world; show an arrow of profit income from UK firms to foreign owners as a flow abroad.
Scenario 2 · Change

Argentina: nominal growth that is not real

A country with high inflation such as Argentina can report very large rises in nominal GDP. Suppose nominal GDP rises from ARS 100tn to ARS 190tn in one year while the GDP deflator rises from 200 to 390. The population grows from 45.8m to 46.2m.

Ask the class
• Calculate real GDP in both years (base-year prices).
• What happened to real GDP per capita?
• Why is nominal GDP misleading here?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Real GDP year 1 = 100tn ÷ 200 × 100 = ARS 50tn; year 2 = 190tn ÷ 390 × 100 = ARS 48.7tn.
  2. Real GDP fell by about 2.6% although nominal GDP rose by 90%: the whole rise and more was due to prices.
  3. Real GDP per capita: year 1 = 50tn ÷ 45.8m ≈ ARS 1.09m; year 2 = 48.7tn ÷ 46.2m ≈ ARS 1.05m, a fall of about 3.4%.
  4. Only real per capita data show that the average person has less output available. Link to the business cycle diagram: this is a contraction below the trend.
DiagramBusiness cycle diagram: real GDP on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal; mark the trend line (potential output) and place the economy in the recession phase.
Scenario 3 · Economic well-being

Vietnam coffee and PPP

The market exchange rate is $1 = VND23,000. A cappuccino costs $5 in the USA and VND90,000 in Vietnam (Hoang p. 233). Vietnam's GDP per capita at market rates is about $2,700.

Ask the class
• What is the implied PPP exchange rate?
• Is the dong undervalued or overvalued?
• Would Vietnam's GDP per capita be higher or lower at PPP?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. PPP rate = VND90,000 ÷ $5 = VND18,000 per $1.
  2. At the market rate VND90,000 buys only $3.91 of US currency, so the same coffee is cheaper in Vietnam: the dong is undervalued against the market rate.
  3. Because each dollar goes further in Vietnam, converting Vietnamese GDP at the PPP rate (18,000 rather than 23,000) raises measured GDP per capita, by roughly 23,000 ÷ 18,000 ≈ 1.28 times on this one-product estimate.
  4. Evaluate: one product is not a representative basket and quality may differ (Top tip, p. 233), so real PPP data from the World Bank use hundreds of products.
DiagramNo theory diagram needed; a two-bar chart of GDP per capita at market rates vs at PPP makes the point.
Scenario 4 · Sustainability

New Zealand's well-being budget

In 2019 New Zealand began setting its budget priorities using well-being measures (mental health, child poverty, the environment) as well as GDP; Iceland and Scotland have similar plans (Hoang p. 241). Critics say well-being data are subjective and hard to compare.

Ask the class
• Why might a government look beyond GDP growth?
• What are the weaknesses of well-being measures?
• Should GDP growth be dropped as a target?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. GDP leaves out non-marketed work, leisure, income distribution and environmental damage, and counts spending on pollution clean-up as positive output.
  2. Composite measures like the BLI or the HPI include health, education, life satisfaction and ecological footprint, so they are closer to economic well-being and sustainability.
  3. However, happiness scores are self-reported, the weights in any composite index are value judgements, and data are published less often than GDP.
  4. Judgement: most economists argue for a dashboard – GDP per capita (PPP) plus distribution and environmental measures – rather than one number.
DiagramNo theory diagram; students could sketch the business cycle to show that GDP data are timely (quarterly), which is one advantage well-being indices lack.

Diagrams: how to draw them

Circular flow of income model with leakages and injections SL/HL

Fig 16.2, p. 228

  1. Draw two boxes: Households and Firms.
  2. Top loop: factors of production from households to firms, and factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) from firms to households.
  3. Bottom loop: goods and services from firms to households, and consumer spending (C) from households to firms.
  4. Add three sectors: Financial sector (S leaks out, I injected in), Government (T out, G in), Rest of the world (M out, X in).
  5. Label W = S + T + M and J = I + G + X; state that national income is in equilibrium when W = J and that O = Y = E.
Common errorsShowing money and real flows in the same direction; forgetting that imports are a leakage and exports an injection; drawing transfer payments as part of G that counts in GDP; missing labels on the arrows.

Business cycle SL/HL

Fig 16.3, p. 234

  1. Label axes: Real GDP (vertical) and Time (horizontal).
  2. Draw an upward-sloping straight line: the long-term growth trend (potential output).
  3. Draw a wave around the trend line: label the boom, peak, recession (falling real GDP), trough and recovery.

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Q16.3 (p. 230): C = $150bn, I = $60bn, G = $55bn, X = $31bn, M = $28bn, net income from abroad = –$8bn. Calculate nominal GDP. Then (Q16.6, p. 231) calculate nominal GNI.

SolutionGDP = C + I + G + (X – M) = 150 + 60 + 55 + (31 – 28) = $268bn. Net factor income is not part of GDP. GNI = GDP + net factor income from abroad = 268 + (–8) = $260bn.

Problem 2. Q16.4 (p. 230): GDP = $4,770bn, C = $3,720bn, G = $668bn, I = $524bn. Calculate net exports.

SolutionX – M = GDP – C – I – G = 4,770 – 3,720 – 524 – 668 = –$142bn (a net import position).

Problem 3. Q16.5 (p. 230): private consumption $1,205bn, government consumption $680bn, gross fixed capital formation $1,150bn, exports of goods $850bn, imports of goods $940bn, exports of services $1,100bn, imports of services $1,700bn. Calculate GDP at market prices.

SolutionX = 850 + 1,100 = $1,950bn; M = 940 + 1,700 = $2,640bn; X – M = –$690bn. GDP = 1,205 + 1,150 + 680 – 690 = $2,345bn.

Problem 4. Q16.7 (p. 232): nominal GDP $260.0bn (2019) and $262.4bn (2020); deflator 106.7 and 108.5. Calculate real GDP in both years and explain.

SolutionReal GDP 2019 = 260.0 ÷ 106.7 × 100 = $243.7bn; 2020 = 262.4 ÷ 108.5 × 100 = $241.8bn. Nominal GDP rose by 0.9% but prices rose by about 1.7%, so real output fell by about 0.8%.

Problem 5. Q16.8 (p. 233): Taiwan GDP $590bn (2018) and $605bn (2019); population 23.59m and 23.60m. Calculate the percentage change in GDP per capita.

Solution2018: 590bn ÷ 23.59m = $25,011; 2019: 605bn ÷ 23.60m = $25,636. Change = (25,636 – 25,011) ÷ 25,011 × 100 ≈ +2.5%.

Problem 6. Q16.9 (p. 233): Country G nominal GDP $228.0bn, $230.2bn, $232.4bn (2018–20); deflators 106.0, 107.8, 109.8. (a) Real GDP 2019; (b) real value of a $24,000 salary in 2020; (c) explain.

Solution(a) 230.2 ÷ 107.8 × 100 = $213.5bn. (b) 24,000 ÷ 109.8 × 100 = $21,858 in base-year prices. (c) Real GDP was $215.1bn in 2018, $213.5bn in 2019 and $211.7bn in 2020: nominal GDP grew by about 1% a year but the price level grew by about 1.7–1.9% a year, so real output fell.

Problem 7. HL extension (Session 4). Country A: C = 420, I = 110, G = 150, X = 200, M = 230, net factor income = +30 (all $bn at current prices); deflator 125; population 20m; PPP conversion factor: $1 at market rates buys 1.6 times as much in A. Calculate nominal GNI, real GNI, real GNI per capita and real GNI per capita at PPP. HL

SolutionGDP = 420 + 110 + 150 + (200 – 230) = $650bn. GNI = 650 + 30 = $680bn. Real GNI = 680 ÷ 125 × 100 = $544bn. Per capita = 544bn ÷ 20m = $27,200. At PPP = 27,200 × 1.6 = $43,520 (int. $).

Problem 8. PPP (p. 233): market rate $1 = VND23,000; a cappuccino costs $5 in the USA and VND90,000 in Vietnam. Find the PPP rate and state whether the dong is under- or overvalued.

SolutionPPP rate = 90,000 ÷ 5 = VND18,000 per $1. At the market rate the coffee costs 90,000 ÷ 23,000 = $3.91, less than $5, so the dong is undervalued by (23,000 – 18,000) ÷ 23,000 ≈ 22%.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits) are part of G in GDP – they are not, as no output is produced in return.
  • GDP and GNI are interchangeable – GDP is based on location of production, GNI on ownership of the factors.
  • A rise in nominal GDP means the economy grew – it may be entirely due to inflation.
  • A fall in the GDP growth rate is a recession – a recession needs real GDP itself to fall (negative growth) for two consecutive quarters.
  • Higher GDP per capita always means higher well-being for everyone – it is a mean and ignores distribution, leisure, the environment and the shadow economy.
  • All components of AD fall in a recession – government spending often rises through fiscal stimulus (2008, 2020).
  • PPP is the actual exchange rate – it is a notional rate; market rates can stay far from PPP for years.
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • p. 232: the China and Luxembourg per capita figures do not match the totals given. $14.2tn ÷ 1.4bn ≈ $10,140 (not $7,755), and $72.1bn ÷ 610,000 ≈ $118,200 (not $107,243). The per capita values seem to come from a different year or dataset. Correct them, or ask students to spot the mismatch.
  • p. 232: the formula is printed as 'Real GDP = Nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator'. When the deflator is an index (base = 100) it must be multiplied by 100: Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ deflator) × 100.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Name the three injections and three leakages in the circular flow.

Injections: investment, government spending, exports. Leakages: savings, taxes, imports.

2. Why should the output, income and expenditure methods give the same answer?

The value of output is what is spent on it, and that spending becomes the income of the factors that produced it, so O = Y = E.

3. Write the expenditure formula for GDP.

GDP = C + I + G + (X – M).

4. GDP is $800bn and net factor income from abroad is +$25bn. What is GNI?

$825bn.

5. Why are pensions excluded from national income?

They are transfer payments: money is redistributed but no new output is produced.

6. Nominal GDP is $330bn and the deflator is 110. What is real GDP?

330 ÷ 110 × 100 = $300bn at base-year prices.

7. Why use per capita values when comparing countries?

Totals depend on population size; dividing by population gives the average income per person.

8. Why use PPP-adjusted figures?

They remove differences in the cost of living, so a dollar of income buys the same amount in each country.

9. Define a recession.

A fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters.

10. What does the trend line on a business cycle diagram represent?

Long-term growth in potential output – what the economy can produce with full, efficient use of resources.

11. Give three reasons why GDP per capita may be a poor measure of well-being.

It ignores income distribution, the shadow economy/non-marketed work and environmental damage (also leisure, composition of output, cost of living).

12. Which index divides well-being outcomes by ecological footprint?

The Happy Planet Index (New Economics Foundation).

13. Give one weakness of the World Happiness Report ranking.

It is based on self-reported life evaluations, which are subjective and may differ across cultures.
