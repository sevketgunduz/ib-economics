Market failure – market power

Syllabus 2.11 Market failure – market power (HL only)Textbook pp. 178–216Sessions 10 HL · 0 SLConcepts efficiency, choice, interdependence, intervention, equity

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? When a few firms dominate a market – tech platforms, supermarkets, airlines – do consumers lose, and should governments break them up, regulate or fine them?

HL only (the entire chapter). It includes all market-structure diagrams, cost and revenue calculations from data, the game theory payoff matrix and concentration ratios.

Key terms and definitions

Market power HLThe ability of a firm to influence the price of its product, usually by restricting output and raising price above the level of a competitive market (P > MC).
Perfect competition HLA market structure with very many small firms, homogeneous products, perfect information and no barriers to entry or exit. Each firm is a price taker.
Price taker HLA firm with no market power that must accept the market price, so its demand curve is horizontal: P = D = AR = MR.
Monopoly HLA market structure with a single (or dominant) firm, no close substitutes and high barriers to entry. The firm is a price maker facing the downward-sloping market demand curve.
Barriers to entry HLObstacles that make it hard for new firms to enter a market, e.g. branding, legal barriers, anti-competitive practices, domination of resources and economies of scale (BLADE).
Oligopoly HLA market dominated by a few large firms with high barriers to entry, in which firms are interdependent: each firm's decisions depend on its rivals' expected reactions.
Monopolistic competition HLA market structure with many firms selling differentiated products and no significant barriers to entry. Each firm has a small degree of market power.
Marginal revenue (MR) HLThe change in total revenue from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR/ΔQ.
Marginal cost (MC) HLThe change in total cost from producing one more unit: MC = ΔTC/ΔQ.
Average cost (AC) HLTotal cost divided by output: AC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC.
Profit maximization HLProducing the output where MC = MR (with MC rising), because every unit up to that point adds more to revenue than to cost.
Economic cost HLThe total of explicit costs (payments to factors) and implicit costs (the opportunity cost of the owner's own resources).
Normal profit HLThe minimum profit needed to keep a firm in the industry in the long run. It occurs where AR = AC, i.e. zero economic profit.
Abnormal (supernormal) profit HLProfit above normal profit, where AR > AC. It attracts new firms where there are no barriers to entry.
Loss HLWhen total cost exceeds total revenue, i.e. AR < AC.
Allocative efficiency HLProducing the output where P = MC (MB = MC), so that social surplus is maximised.
Productive efficiency HLProducing at the lowest point of the average cost curve, where MC = AC.
Natural monopoly HLA market in which economies of scale are so large relative to demand that one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms, e.g. rail networks and water supply.
Collusive oligopoly HLOligopolists agree, formally (a cartel) or tacitly, to limit competition by fixing prices or sharing output, and so act like a joint monopolist.
Game theory HLThe study of strategic decision-making when the outcome for each player depends on the choices of others, often shown in a payoff matrix.
Nash equilibrium HLAn outcome in which each player's strategy is its best response to the other's strategy, so neither wants to change unilaterally. In the prisoner's dilemma it leaves both players worse off than cooperation would.
Non-price competition HLCompeting by means other than price, such as branding, advertising, packaging, quality, after-sales service and loyalty schemes.
Concentration ratio HLThe combined market share of the largest n firms in an industry (e.g. CR4). A high ratio indicates oligopoly or monopoly.
Economies of scale HLFalls in long-run average cost as the scale of production increases. Internal economies arise from the firm's own growth; external economies come from the growth of the industry.
Price discrimination HLCharging different prices to different groups of consumers for the same product, for reasons not related to cost, based on differences in PED.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Efficiency

Wheat farmers in a perfectly competitive market

Thousands of farmers sell identical wheat on a world commodity exchange. After a drought in another region, the world price rises from $250 to $320 per tonne. A typical farm has minimum AC of $250 and, at the new price, produces 1,000 tonnes where MC = MR, at an AC of $270. Two years later many new farms have switched land into wheat.

Ask the class
• What profit does the typical farm make at $320?
• What happens in the long run?
• Is the market allocatively and productively efficient in the long run?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The farm is a price taker, so its demand is horizontal at $320 = AR = MR.
  2. At MC = MR, AR ($320) > AC ($270), so abnormal profit = $50 × 1,000 = $50,000.
  3. With no barriers to entry, abnormal profit attracts new farms; industry supply shifts right and the world price falls.
  4. Entry continues until price returns to minimum AC ($250), so farms earn normal profit.
  5. In the long run P = MC (allocative efficiency) and P = min AC (productive efficiency). Weather shocks and subsidies mean real farm markets are rarely in long-run equilibrium.
DiagramPerfect competition, short-run abnormal profit then long-run adjustment (Figs 14.14 and 14.16). Two panels: industry S and D (demand up, then supply shifts right) and firm (MC, AC, D = AR = MR at $320, then at $250). Shade the profit rectangle between $320 and $270 up to 1,000 tonnes.
Scenario 2 · Equity

A pharmaceutical patent monopoly

A drug company holds a 20-year patent on a new treatment. It faces the market demand curve and produces where MC = MR, selling 2 million doses at $300 each, while the MC of a dose is $20. Generic producers could supply the same drug at about $25 once the patent expires. The firm argues that high profits fund R&D for future drugs.

Ask the class
• Why can the firm charge far above MC?
• What is the welfare cost?
• Are high profits justified?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The patent is a legal barrier to entry (the L in BLADE), so the firm is a monopolist with no close substitutes.
  2. It restricts output to where MC = MR and sets price from the demand curve, so P ($300) > MC ($20): allocative inefficiency.
  3. Compared with a competitive market (P = MC), output is lower and price higher. Consumer surplus is transferred to the firm as abnormal profit, and a welfare loss triangle appears.
  4. Against this, abnormal profits fund R&D (dynamic efficiency). Without patents, fewer new drugs might be developed.
  5. Evaluate. Governments may use price regulation, bulk purchasing or compulsory licensing in emergencies; there is an equity issue about access to medicines.
DiagramMonopoly vs perfect competition with welfare loss (Fig 14.23). Axes: Price and Quantity. Draw AR = D, MR and MC (= S of a competitive industry). Mark Pm, Qm at MC = MR and Ppc, Qpc at MC = D. Shade the welfare loss triangle between Qm and Qpc.
Scenario 3 · Interdependence

Mobile network price war

Four mobile networks control 90% of a country's market. When one cuts its unlimited-data plan from $40 to $30, the others quickly match the cut, and all four see profits fall. Analysts expect the firms to switch to competing on 5G coverage, bundled streaming services and loyalty discounts instead.

Ask the class
• Why did rivals match the cut?
• Why might firms avoid price competition?
• What does the concentration ratio suggest?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. A CR4 of 90% shows a highly concentrated oligopoly.
  2. Firms are interdependent: a price cut by one steals customers from the others, so they match it to protect market share.
  3. The result is a price war in which all firms earn lower profits, as in the low/low cell of the payoff matrix (Nash equilibrium).
  4. To avoid this, firms prefer non-price competition (coverage, bundles, branding), and prices become rigid.
  5. Evaluate. Consumers gain from the price war in the short run, but tacit collusion afterwards may keep prices high. The regulator should watch for coordination.
DiagramGame theory payoff matrix (Fig 14.28) with two firms choosing 'maintain $40' or 'cut to $30'. Fill in profits and circle the Nash equilibrium. Optionally, a collusive oligopoly diagram (Fig 14.26) to show the joint-monopoly outcome they are trying to reach.
Scenario 4 · Choice

Coffee shops in monopolistic competition

A university district has 25 independent coffee shops, each with its own style, menu and loyalty card. A new shop with a popular cold-brew menu makes large profits in its first year. Within 18 months, seven new cold-brew cafés open nearby, and the first shop's customer numbers fall back.

Ask the class
• Why can each café set its own price?
• What happens to the first shop's profits in the long run?
• Is this market efficient?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Differentiated products give each café a downward-sloping demand curve, but it is quite elastic because there are many close substitutes.
  2. In the short run, the first shop's AR > AC, so it makes abnormal profit.
  3. With low barriers to entry, new cafés copy the idea; each existing firm's demand shifts left and becomes more elastic until AR is tangent to AC: normal profit.
  4. In the long run P > MC (allocative inefficiency) and output is below minimum AC (excess capacity).
  5. But consumers gain product variety and innovation, and inefficiency is smaller than under monopoly because demand is more elastic.
DiagramMonopolistic competition: short-run abnormal profit (Fig 14.32) and long-run normal profit (Fig 14.34). Show AR and MR shifting left and becoming flatter, and AR tangent to AC at the MC = MR output.
Scenario 5 · Intervention

Blocking a supermarket merger

In 2019 the UK Competition and Markets Authority blocked the proposed merger of Sainsbury's and Asda, which would have created the largest UK grocer, with roughly 30% of the market. The CMA predicted higher prices, lower quality and less choice in many local areas. The firms argued that scale would let them cut prices by around 10% on everyday items.

Ask the class
• What were the potential benefits of the merger?
• What risks did the CMA identify?
• Was blocking the merger the right response?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. The UK grocery market is an oligopoly (the top four had over two-thirds of sales, p. 206).
  2. Benefits claimed: purchasing and logistics economies of scale that lower LRAC and might be passed on as lower prices.
  3. Risks: the combined firm's market power could allow higher prices, lower output and less choice, especially in towns where the two were the main rivals.
  4. The CMA used competition legislation to block the merger, a preventive response rather than fines after abuse.
  5. Evaluate. Blocking keeps competition, but may prevent efficiency gains. Conditions such as selling off stores are an alternative.
DiagramMonopoly vs perfect competition welfare loss (Fig 14.23) to show the risk, plus the LRAC curve (Fig 14.36) to show the claimed economies of scale.

Simulation and data activities

The Evolution of Trust · ncase.me/trust

Students play individually for about 15 minutes, then answer a worksheet linking the game to oligopoly: 'cooperate' is keeping prices high and 'cheat' is undercutting. They then pair up for a 10-round pricing game using Hoang's Fig 14.28 payoffs, record their scores and compare strategies (always cheat, tit-for-tat, always cooperate). Finally, the class discusses how repeated interaction supports tacit collusion.

Debrief
• Why is 'low price' a dominant strategy in a one-shot game?
• Why can repeated games sustain cooperation (tacit collusion)?
• What role do competition authorities play in this 'game'?

Classroom pit market (double auction) with entry

Hand out buyer cards and identical-cost seller cards for a homogeneous good. Run a 5-minute trading round and record every trade price on the board. Announce the sellers' average profit, then let extra students enter as sellers with the same costs. Run two more rounds and watch the price fall towards the sellers' average cost. Plot the average price by round.

Debrief
• Why did the price fall as sellers entered?
• What happened to each seller's profit?
• Which assumptions of perfect competition did the game meet, and which did it not?

Course Simulator – Monopoly vs perfect competition

Project the tool with linear demand and constant MC. Students predict the monopoly price and quantity, then check them. They record consumer surplus, profit and welfare loss under each structure in a table. Then they flatten the demand curve to represent monopolistic competition and note the smaller welfare loss. Finally they enter the Hoang-style numbers from the calculation section to verify the hand calculations.

Debrief
• Which part of the lost consumer surplus becomes profit, and which part is lost to society?
• Why does a more elastic demand reduce the welfare loss?

Cartel (OPEC) quota game

Groups of four act as oil producers. Each round they secretly write 'quota' or 'extra'. The teacher reveals a profit table: if all keep quota, each earns 10; one cheater earns 16 while the others earn 7; with two or more cheaters, everyone earns 5–6. Play six rounds with a negotiation break after round 3 and a surprise 'fine' for cheating in round 5.

Debrief
• Why did cartels break down?
• How did communication and fines change behaviour?
• How does this compare with the BMW/VW/Daimler and TV-tube cartels (pp. 201, 203)?

Worked calculations

Problem 1. Q14.1 Tandy Toys (p. 188): price $20, materials $8 per unit, rent $7,000, salaries $8,000, output 5,000 units. Calculate total cost and profit. HL

SolutionTVC = 8 × 5,000 = $40,000; TFC = 7,000 + 8,000 = $15,000; TC = $55,000. TR = 20 × 5,000 = $100,000. Profit = 100,000 − 55,000 = $45,000.

Problem 2. Q14.2 (p. 189): at 10 units TVC = $2,000 and TC = $4,545; at 15 units TVC = $2,850 and TC = $5,395. Calculate FC, AFC, AVC, AC and MC. HL

SolutionFC = 4,545 − 2,000 = $2,545. AFC = 2,545/10 = $254.50 and 2,545/15 = $169.67. AVC = $200 and 2,850/15 = $190. AC = $454.50 and 5,395/15 = $359.67. MC (per unit between 10 and 15) = (5,395 − 4,545)/5 = $170.

Problem 3. Q14.9 Crispian's Candies (p. 211): FC $4,800, AVC $1.50, output 4,000 units, price $4. Calculate AC, break-even output and profit. HL

SolutionTC = 4,800 + 1.50 × 4,000 = $10,800; AC = 10,800/4,000 = $2.70. Break-even: 4Q = 4,800 + 1.5Q, so Q = 4,800/2.50 = 1,920 units. Profit = 16,000 − 10,800 = $5,200.

Problem 4. Economic cost (p. 190): university fees are $35,000 and the student gives up a $25,000 salary. What is the economic cost? And a cinema earns $60,000 from 7,500 tickets (p. 187): what is AR? HL

SolutionEconomic cost = explicit $35,000 + implicit $25,000 = $60,000. AR = TR/Q = 60,000/7,500 = $8, which equals the ticket price.

Problem 5. A monopolist faces the data: Q 1–6; P $10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. TC (including FC of $6) is $9, 11, 14, 18, 24, 32 for Q = 1–6. Calculate TR, MR, MC and profit, and find the profit-maximizing output and the abnormal profit per unit. HL

SolutionTR = 10, 18, 24, 28, 30, 30; MR = 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 0. MC (from TC at Q = 0 of $6) = 3, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8. Profit = 1, 7, 10, 10, 6, −2. MC = MR = 4 at Q = 4, where profit is at its maximum of $10 (equal to Q = 3, because the 4th unit adds zero profit). At Q = 4, AR = $7 and AC = 18/4 = $4.50, so abnormal profit per unit = $2.50 and total = $10. Revenue is maximised where MR = 0 (Q = 5–6).

Problem 6. Monopoly welfare loss: demand P = 100 − Q, MR = 100 − 2Q, constant MC = AC = $20. Compare perfect competition with monopoly and calculate the welfare loss. HL

SolutionPerfect competition: P = MC gives P = $20, Q = 80; CS = ½ × 80 × 80 = 3,200. Monopoly: MR = MC gives 100 − 2Q = 20, Q = 40, P = $60. CS = ½ × 40 × 40 = 800; abnormal profit = (60 − 20) × 40 = 1,600; welfare loss = ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) = 800. Check: 800 + 1,600 + 800 = 3,200.

Problem 7. Price taker (Table 14.2, p. 192): lemonade sells at $5 a cup. Show TR, AR and MR for 1–3 cups. HL

SolutionTR = 5, 10, 15; AR = TR/Q = $5 at each level; MR = ΔTR/ΔQ = $5. So P = AR = MR = $5, the horizontal demand curve of a price taker.

Problem 8. Game theory (Fig 14.28, p. 204): identify each firm's dominant strategy and the Nash equilibrium. HL

SolutionIf Blue prices high, Green earns $60m by pricing low vs $50m high; if Blue prices low, Green earns $30m low vs $20m high. So low is dominant for Green, and by symmetry for Blue. Nash equilibrium: both low, $30m each, which is worse than the $50m each they would get by both pricing high. This is why firms have an incentive to collude, and each has an incentive to cheat.

Problem 9. Concentration (p. 206): Industry A has firm shares of 30%, 25% and 10%; Industry B has 25%, 20% and 20%. Calculate CR3 and the HHI for the top three firms. HL

SolutionCR3: A = 65%, B = 65%. HHI (top three): A = 30² + 25² + 10² = 900 + 625 + 100 = 1,625; B = 25² + 20² + 20² = 625 + 400 + 400 = 1,425. A is more concentrated because its market power is held by a bigger leading firm, even though the CR3 is the same. (A full HHI would add the squared shares of all remaining firms.)

Problem 10. UK supermarkets (approximate 2019 shares): Tesco 27%, Sainsbury's 15%, Asda 15%, Morrisons 10%. Calculate the CR4 and interpret it. HL

SolutionCR4 = 27 + 15 + 15 + 10 = 67% (Hoang reports over 69%, p. 206). A CR4 above about 60% indicates a concentrated oligopoly in which firms are interdependent.

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • A monopolist can charge any price it wants. It controls price OR quantity, not demand; a higher price means fewer sales (pp. 184, 212).
  • Normal profit means zero profit. It means zero economic profit: accounting profit that just covers the opportunity cost of the owner's resources (p. 190).
  • Profit is maximised where AC is lowest, or where TR is highest. Profit is maximised where MC = MR.
  • The monopoly price is read off the MR curve. Output comes from MC = MR; price is read from the AR (demand) curve.
  • Economies of scale lower total costs. They lower long-run average cost; total cost still rises with output (p. 212).
  • Nash equilibrium is the best outcome for both firms. It is each firm's best response to the other, which may leave both worse off than cooperating.
  • Large firms are always bad for consumers. Economies of scale, R&D and natural-monopoly efficiency can benefit consumers (p. 215).
  • Cost and price mean the same thing. Cost is what the firm pays to produce; price is what the buyer pays (pp. 188, 191).
Textbook errata: correct these in class
  • Hoang p. 213 states that the UK CMA blocked a merger of 'Asda and Tesco' in 2019. The merger the CMA blocked in April 2019 was between Sainsbury's and Asda (Walmart's UK subsidiary). Correct this when using the example.
  • Hoang's HHI figures for Industries A and B (p. 206) use only the top three firms' shares. A full HHI sums the squared shares of all firms, so point out to students that these are partial indices.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Give the four characteristics of perfect competition.

Very many firms, homogeneous products, perfect information, free entry and exit (so each firm is a price taker).

2. What does BLADE stand for?

Branding, Legal barriers, Anti-competitive practices, Domination of resources, Economies of scale.

3. State the profit-maximizing rule.

Produce where MC = MR (with MC rising).

4. TFC = $500, TVC at 50 units = $1,000. What is AC?

TC = $1,500, so AC = 1,500/50 = $30.

5. What is the condition for abnormal profit?

AR > AC.

6. Why is the demand curve horizontal for a perfectly competitive firm?

It is a price taker: it can sell any quantity at the market price, and none above it.

7. What are the long-run equilibrium conditions under perfect competition?

MR = MC = AR = AC: normal profit, P = MC and production at minimum AC.

8. Why does a monopolist create a welfare loss?

It restricts output to where MC = MR and charges P > MC, so units that consumers value above their cost are not produced.

9. What makes a monopoly 'natural'?

Economies of scale are so large that LRAC is still falling where it meets market demand, so one firm can supply at the lowest cost.

10. Why do cartel members have an incentive to cheat?

At the high cartel price, each firm could raise its own profit by secretly selling more, since P > its MC.

11. In Hoang's payoff matrix, what is the Nash equilibrium?

Both firms charge a low price and earn $30m each.

12. Calculate the CR3 when the top shares are 40%, 20% and 15%.

75%.

13. Why is monopolistic competition allocatively inefficient in the long run?

P > MC at the MC = MR output, and firms have excess capacity (output below minimum AC).

14. Name three government responses to abuse of market power.

Legislation and regulation (merger control, price caps), government ownership (nationalisation), and fines.

15. Give one advantage of large firms with market power.

Economies of scale lower average costs, and abnormal profit can fund R&D and innovation.

Theory of knowledge

Microeconomic theory is based on the assumption of rational consumer choice and rational self-interest. Yet the principle of collective self-governance suggests that people also behave cooperatively. What assumptions do economists make about the roles of reason and emotion? Are these assumptions justified?
