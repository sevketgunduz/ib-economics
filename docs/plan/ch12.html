Market failure – public goods

Syllabus 2.9 Market failure – public goodsTextbook pp. 165–170Sessions 2 HL · 2 SLConcepts intervention, efficiency, equity, choice

When are markets unable to satisfy important economic objectives – and does government intervention help? If nobody can be made to pay for street lights, flood defences or national defence, who will provide them?

Entirely common SL/HL. The IB requires no diagram and no calculation for public goods.

Key terms and definitions

Public good A good that is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, so the free market will under-provide it or not provide it at all, e.g. national defence or street lighting.
Non-rivalrous One person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others, so the marginal cost of an extra user is zero.
Non-excludable Once the good is provided, it is impossible or too costly to stop people who have not paid from benefiting.
Rivalrous One person's consumption reduces the quantity available for others, e.g. a sandwich or a fish from the sea.
Excludable Non-payers can be prevented from using the good, e.g. through a ticket, paywall or fence.
Private good A good that is both rivalrous and excludable, so the market can provide it through the price mechanism, e.g. a smartphone.
Pure public good A good that is fully non-rivalrous and fully non-excludable, e.g. national defence.
Quasi-public good A good with some but not all public-good characteristics, or only partly, e.g. a road that becomes rivalrous when congested, or a beach.
Free rider problem People can benefit from a non-excludable good without paying, so they do not reveal their willingness to pay and private firms cannot earn revenue from it.
Collective consumption good Samuelson's (1954) original term for a public good: each person's use does not reduce anyone else's.
Direct provision The government produces the public good itself and pays for it from taxes, e.g. national defence or police.
Contracting out The government pays private firms to produce a good or service it has chosen to provide, e.g. refuse collection or road maintenance. The service is still publicly funded.
Optimal provision Providing a public good up to the output where MSB = MSC. If MSB > MSC the good is under-provided; if MSC > MSB it is over-provided.
Government failure When government intervention leads to a less efficient outcome, e.g. over- or under-providing a public good because true valuations are unknown.
Opportunity cost of provision The next best use of the tax revenue spent on a public good, such as more hospitals or lower taxes.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Intervention

Flood defences for a river town

A town of 40,000 people on a river floods on average once every ten years, causing about $30 million of damage each time. A flood barrier would cost $120 million and protect every home in the town. A private firm offers to build it if residents pay $3,000 each.

Ask the class
• Is the flood barrier a public good?
• Why would the private offer fail?
• How should the government decide whether to build it?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Protection is non-rivalrous (one more protected home does not reduce protection for others) and non-excludable (the firm cannot protect some homes and not their neighbours).
  2. Each resident has an incentive to free-ride and hope others pay, so the firm cannot collect enough to cover its costs and the market fails to provide the barrier.
  3. Expected damage avoided is roughly $3 million a year, so over the barrier's life the social benefit may exceed $120 million.
  4. The government can provide it directly or contract out construction, funded by taxes. It should build if the MSB is at least the MSC.
  5. Evaluate. Benefits are hard to measure, there is an opportunity cost, and there are equity issues about whether national taxpayers should pay for a local good.

Scenario 2 · Choice

Wikipedia and open-access knowledge

Wikipedia receives billions of page views a month but is funded by donations from a small share of users (Hoang p. 168). Each spring it runs banners asking readers to give a few dollars. Most readers never donate, yet the site keeps running.

Ask the class
• Is Wikipedia a public good?
• How does the free rider problem show up here?
• Why has the market not failed completely?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Wikipedia is non-rivalrous (one reader does not reduce content for others, and the marginal cost is close to zero) and non-excludable (there is no paywall).
  2. Most users free-ride, so donations are far below the value users receive.
  3. It survives because of volunteer contributors, a minority of donors with altruistic motives, and low costs per user.
  4. This shows that economic theory predicts under-provision, not always zero provision, and that behaviour is not always purely self-interested.
  5. Link to TOK: are economic assumptions about self-interest justified?
DiagramNo IB-required diagram. Place Wikipedia on the rivalry/excludability grid and compare it with Netflix (a club good).
Scenario 3 · Efficiency

Contracting out refuse collection

A city council currently collects household rubbish with its own workers at a cost of $18 million a year. A private firm bids $15 million for a five-year contract with the same service level. Unions warn that pay and service quality could fall, and residents worry about missed collections.

Ask the class
• Why might the council contract out?
• What are the risks?
• Is refuse collection a pure public good?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Refuse collection is a quasi-public good. Collection is partly excludable (it could be charged per bin), but it also creates positive externalities for public health, so councils provide it for everyone.
  2. Contracting out uses competitive bidding to lower costs. Here it saves $3 million a year that can be spent elsewhere.
  3. Risks: quality falls if the contract is poorly monitored, it costs money to write and enforce contracts, workers may get worse terms, and the firm could fail.
  4. The service remains publicly funded; only production changes hands.
  5. Evaluate. It works well when the output is easy to measure and the contract is enforced.
DiagramNo diagram required; a stakeholder table (council, taxpayers, residents, workers, private firm) with gains and losses.
Scenario 4 · Sustainability

Crowded public beaches in Bali

Public beaches in Bali are open to everyone without charge. On quiet weekdays one extra visitor takes nothing away from others, but in peak season Kuta beach becomes crowded and littered. Hoang notes that tourists can wear out public beaches (p. 168). The local government is considering an entry fee for foreign tourists and paying a private firm to clean the beach.

Ask the class
• Is a public beach a pure public good?
• Why does the good change character at peak times?
• Evaluate an entry fee plus contracted-out cleaning.
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Off-peak, the beach is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, so it behaves like a public good.
  2. At peak times one more visitor reduces space and quality for others, so it becomes rivalrous. With no exclusion it now behaves like a common pool resource, and over-use and litter follow (a tragedy of the commons).
  3. It is therefore a quasi-public good.
  4. An entry fee makes the beach partly excludable and raises revenue; cleaning could be contracted out to a specialist firm and paid for with the fees.
  5. Evaluate. A fee may be hard to enforce on open coastline and may hurt equity for locals (so exempt them). Contracting out needs clear performance standards.
DiagramNo IB-required diagram. Use the rivalry/excludability grid with an arrow showing the beach moving from the public-good box to the common-pool box at peak times.

Simulation and data activities

Veconlab – Public goods (voluntary contributions) game · veconlab.econ.virginia.edu

Set up the Voluntary Contributions game with groups of four and a multiplier below the group size (e.g. 0.5 per token to each member). Students choose each round how many tokens to keep and how many to contribute to the group account. Run eight rounds and show the falling average contribution. Then run four rounds with a 'punishment' option or a government 'tax' that forces everyone to contribute, and compare earnings.

Debrief
• Why did contributions fall over time?
• Which strategy maximises your own payoff, and which maximises the group's?
• How does compulsory taxation solve the free rider problem, and what does it cost?

Class fireworks fund (envelope game)

Give each student five tokens, which can be swapped for a small reward at the end. Announce a class 'fireworks' treat that happens only if the envelope total reaches a target, and everyone benefits whether or not they gave. Students give secretly. Reveal the total, then repeat after discussion. Link the result to non-excludability and free riding.

Debrief
• Did the class reach the target? Why or why not?
• What would happen if the teacher could exclude non-contributors?
• What does this tell us about how governments must fund public goods?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Public goods are goods provided by the government. Governments also provide education and healthcare, which are not public goods; the definition rests on non-rivalry and non-excludability (pp. 166, 168).
  • Public goods are free goods. They use scarce resources and have an opportunity cost, so they are economic goods (Q12.3, p. 170).
  • Merit goods and public goods are the same. Both create external benefits, but merit goods are rivalrous and excludable (p. 168).
  • Every good with positive externalities is a public good. Private schooling and private healthcare have positive externalities but are private goods (p. 166).
  • Common pool resources are public goods. CPRs are rivalrous; public goods are not.
  • The free rider problem means the good is never provided. The market under-provides; donations, advertising or volunteers may provide some.

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. What are the two characteristics of a public good?

Non-rivalrous and non-excludable.

2. What is the free rider problem?

People benefit from a good without paying, so firms cannot earn revenue and do not supply it.

3. Why is national defence a pure public good?

Protecting one more person costs nothing extra, and no resident can be excluded from protection.

4. Is education a public good? Explain briefly.

No. It is rivalrous (class places are limited) and excludable (fees can be charged); it is a merit good.

5. What is a quasi-public good? Give an example.

A good with only some or partial public-good characteristics, e.g. a road that becomes rivalrous when congested.

6. What is contracting out?

The government pays a private firm to produce a publicly funded service, e.g. refuse collection.

7. State one advantage of contracting out.

Competitive bidding can lower costs and bring in specialist skills.

8. State one disadvantage of direct provision.

Opportunity cost of taxes and risk of government failure, e.g. inefficiency or providing the wrong quantity.

9. Why are public goods economic goods, not free goods?

They use scarce resources, so producing them has an opportunity cost.

10. At what output is a public good optimally provided?

Where MSB = MSC.

11. Why is it hard for government to know the MSB of a public good?

People have an incentive to understate how much they value it, and benefits are spread across many people.
