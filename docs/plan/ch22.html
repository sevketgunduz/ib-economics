Unit 3 · Macroeconomics · Hoang Chapter 22

Potential conflict between macroeconomic objectives

Syllabus 3.3 Macroeconomic objectives – potential conflict between macroeconomic objectivesTextbook pp. 316–324Sessions 3 HL · 1 SLConcepts choice, sustainability, equity, change, economic well-being

Why does economic activity vary over time and why does this matter? Can a government have fast growth, full employment, stable prices, a clean environment and a fair income distribution all at once – or must it choose?

Common SL/HL: conflicts between low unemployment and low inflation (in words), high growth and low inflation, high growth and environmental sustainability, high growth and equity. HL only: the AD/AS diagram of the unemployment–inflation trade-off, and the short-run and long-run Phillips curves (pp. 317–320).

Key terms and definitions

Conflicting macroeconomic objectives A situation in which achieving one macroeconomic objective makes it harder to achieve another, so policymakers face trade-offs (e.g. low unemployment versus low inflation).
Demand-pull inflation A rise in the general price level caused by aggregate demand growing faster than aggregate supply, often as the economy approaches full employment.
Cost-push (wage) inflation A rise in the general price level caused by higher costs of production, such as higher wages in a tight labour market, which shifts SRAS to the left.
Phillips curve HLA curve showing an inverse relationship between the rate of unemployment and the rate of (wage) inflation, based on A.W. Phillips' 1958 study of UK data for 1861–1913.
Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC) HLA downward-sloping curve showing a short-run trade-off: lower unemployment can be achieved only with higher inflation, and vice versa, given inflation expectations.
Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) HLA vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment showing that, in the long run, there is no trade-off between unemployment and inflation.
Natural rate of unemployment (NRU) The rate of unemployment when the economy is at full-employment output; it consists of frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment and excludes cyclical unemployment.
Stagflation The simultaneous occurrence of rising inflation and falling real output with rising unemployment, usually caused by a negative supply shock.
Supply shock A sudden, unexpected event (e.g. oil crisis, pandemic, natural disaster) that changes costs of production and shifts SRAS; a negative shock shifts SRAS left.
Inflation expectations HLThe rate of inflation that workers and firms anticipate; when real wages fall, workers demand higher nominal wages, which shifts the SRPC upwards.
Accelerating inflation HLEver-rising inflation that results from repeated attempts to hold unemployment below the natural rate using demand-side policy.
Sustainable economic growth Growth in real GDP that can be maintained over time without causing high inflation or depleting resources for future generations, e.g. when AS rises with AD.
Negative externality of production A cost of production borne by third parties who are not part of the transaction, such as the harm from carbon emissions or river pollution.
Equity in income distribution A fair (not necessarily equal) distribution of income; fast growth may widen income gaps if its gains go mainly to high-income and wealth-owning households.
Progressive tax A tax in which the marginal and average tax rate rise as income rises, so higher earners pay a larger proportion of income; it can help combine growth with equity.
Zero-hours contract An employment contract in which the employer does not guarantee any minimum number of working hours.
Green GDP A measure of output that adjusts GDP for the depletion of natural resources and environmental damage caused by production.

Scenario-based explanations

Scenario 1 · Choice – policymakers must prioritise one objective over another.

Energy shock in Europe, 2022

In 2022 wholesale gas prices in the euro area rose several-fold after supply from Russia was cut. Euro-area inflation reached about 10% in late 2022, while forecasters cut growth expectations sharply. Unemployment stayed close to 6.5% but firms warned of lay-offs in energy-intensive industry. The ECB had to decide whether to raise interest rates.

Ask the class
• Is this demand-pull or cost-push inflation?
• What happens on an SRPC diagram (HL)?
• Why does this create a policy dilemma for the central bank?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Higher gas prices raise costs for almost all firms, so SRAS shifts left – cost-push inflation.
  2. The price level rises and real GDP falls (or grows more slowly), so there is upward pressure on unemployment: stagflation (Fig 22.3).
  3. Step 3 (HL): On a Phillips curve the economy does not move along SRPC1; SRPC1 shifts outward to SRPC2, so every unemployment rate now comes with higher inflation (Fig 22.5).
  4. If the ECB raises rates to fight inflation, AD falls and unemployment rises further; if it keeps rates low, inflation may stay high and become built into expectations. One tool cannot fix both problems (p. 321).
DiagramAD/AS: SRAS1 shifts left to SRAS2; PL1 rises to PL2 and Y1 falls to Y2. HL: SRPC1 shifts right to SRPC2 with axes Inflation rate (%) and Unemployment rate (%).
Scenario 2 · Sustainability

India: growth versus clean air

India's real GDP grew by roughly 6–7% a year in the 2010s and it became the world's third-largest emitter of CO₂. Many Indian cities regularly exceed WHO air-quality limits, and the Yamuna River near the Taj Mahal is heavily polluted by industrial and household waste (p. 322). The government has set a target of net-zero emissions by 2070 and is expanding solar capacity.

Ask the class
• Why does fast growth tend to harm the environment?
• Can growth be made compatible with sustainability?
• Who bears the costs of the pollution?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Rapid growth means more production, energy use and transport, much of it coal-powered, generating negative production externalities.
  2. Third parties (residents, farmers downstream, future generations) bear health and clean-up costs not paid by the producers, so growth in GDP overstates the gain in well-being.
  3. The conflict can be reduced by carbon taxes, regulation, the 3 Rs and investment in renewables; growth becomes 'greener' as output per unit of emissions falls.
  4. Evaluation: clean technology is costly for a lower-middle-income country; the trade-off is sharper in the short run than in the long run.
DiagramPPC or LRAS shifting right to show growth; optionally a negative production externality diagram (MSC above MPC) to show the welfare cost.
Scenario 3 · Equity

Growth and the billionaire gap

Forbes data show the combined wealth of the world's ten richest people rose by 74% between 2017 and 2019 to $743.8bn, while real GDP grew about 7.5% in the USA and 6.4% in the EU over the same period (p. 323). Their combined wealth was larger than the GDP of Switzerland or Turkey.

Ask the class
• Why can growth widen inequality?
• What policies allow growth with equity?
• Does it matter if the rich get richer faster, as long as the poor are not worse off?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Much of the gain from growth goes to owners of capital (shares, property, technology platforms), whose asset values rise faster than wages.
  2. Low-skilled workers on zero-hours contracts or minimum wages (p. 324) gain little, so the income and wealth gap widens even though average income rises.
  3. A progressive tax system, transfer payments and spending on education can spread the gains without stopping growth (p. 324).
  4. Evaluation: very high tax rates may reduce incentives and investment; the right balance is a normative judgement.
DiagramLorenz curve moving further from the line of equality as growth favours the top quintile (links to Chapter 23).
Scenario 4 · Change

Two central bankers (HL)

Country X has unemployment of 5%, which economists estimate to be the natural rate, and inflation of 2%. A new government wants unemployment down to 3% before the next election and pressures the central bank to cut interest rates. Workers' unions have signalled they will demand pay rises matching any increase in inflation.

Ask the class
• What happens in the short run if rates are cut?
• What happens in the long run?
• What would be a better way to reduce unemployment permanently?
Step-by-step explanation
  1. Lower rates raise AD; firms expand output and hire, so the economy moves up SRPC1 from A (5%, 2%) to B (3%, say 4%).
  2. Workers notice that prices have risen faster than expected, real wages fall, and they demand higher nominal wages (W1 to W2).
  3. Higher wage costs shift SRAS left; firms lay off workers and unemployment returns to 5%, but inflation stays at 4%. The SRPC has shifted up to SRPC2: point C (Fig 22.7).
  4. Repeating the policy only accelerates inflation. Supply-side policies (training, better job matching) lower the NRU and shift the LRPC left (Fig 22.8).
DiagramLRPC vertical at NRU = 5%; SRPC1 and SRPC2; points A, B and C with arrows.

Simulation and data activities

Course Simulator – AD/AS (Keynesian & LRAS)

Project the tool in Keynesian mode. Shift AD right in small steps and ask students to record real GDP and price level in a table, then convert output into an estimated unemployment rate (for example, each 1% of output gap = 0.5 points of unemployment). Plot the pairs of inflation and unemployment on paper to generate their own SRPC. Next, shift SRAS left and repeat, showing the new, higher set of points. HL students use the neoclassical LRAS mode to show that AD shifts at Yf only raise the price level.

Debrief
• Why did the points form a downward-sloping curve when only AD changed?
• Why did the curve move when SRAS shifted?
• What does the vertical LRAS imply about the long-run Phillips curve?

FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) · https://fred.stlouisfed.org

Students download US unemployment (UNRATE) and CPI inflation (CPIAUCSL, percent change from a year ago) as annual data. In Google Sheets they build scatter plots for 1961–69, 1970–82 and 2009–19, colouring each decade differently. They draw a trend line for each period and compare slopes and positions. They then annotate the 1970s oil shocks and the 2021–22 inflation surge.

Debrief
• In which decade does a stable SRPC appear?
• What explains the shift in the 1970s?
• Does the 2010s data suggest the Phillips curve has 'flattened'? Why might that be?

Our World in Data · https://ourworldindata.org/co2-and-greenhouse-gas-emissions

Pairs pick two countries (for example, India and the UK, or China and Sweden) and use the 'CO₂ emissions vs GDP' chart to see whether emissions and GDP have 'decoupled'. They note the years in which GDP rose while emissions fell. Each pair writes one sentence evaluating whether growth and sustainability conflict in their country.

Debrief
• Which countries have decoupled growth from emissions?
• Is decoupling partly because production moved abroad?
• What policies helped?

Misconceptions and textbook errata

  • Growth always causes inflation – in fact growth driven by rising AS (or when there is spare capacity) can occur with stable or falling prices (p. 321).
  • The Phillips curve is an AD/AS diagram with different labels – it has inflation rate (not price level) and unemployment rate (not real GDP) on its axes.
  • Stagflation is a movement along the SRPC – it is a shift of the SRPC caused by a leftward shift of SRAS.
  • The LRPC is at zero unemployment – it is vertical at the natural rate, which includes frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment.
  • Demand-side policy can permanently reduce unemployment below the NRU – in the long run this only raises inflation (accelerating inflation).
  • Equity means equal incomes for all – equity is about fairness, and growth with equity usually means a fairer share of gains, not equality.
  • Monetary policy can cure recession and inflation at the same time – one instrument cannot hit two conflicting targets (p. 321).

Checklist questions (exit tickets)

1. Why can low unemployment lead to inflation?

Higher employment raises incomes and AD (demand-pull), and a tight labour market pushes up wages (cost-push).

2. Under what condition can high growth be achieved with low inflation?

When AS (LRAS) increases at a similar pace to AD, e.g. through productivity gains, or when there is spare capacity.

3. Give two ways fast growth can harm the environment.

More emissions and air pollution from production and transport; depletion of resources and waste such as plastics in rivers and oceans.

4. Why may growth worsen income equity?

The gains may go mostly to owners of capital and high-skilled workers, while low-paid workers see little rise in real income.

5. (HL) What is on the axes of a Phillips curve?

Inflation rate (%) on the vertical axis and unemployment rate (%) on the horizontal axis.

6. (HL) What causes a movement along the SRPC?

A change in AD, which changes output and unemployment and the rate of inflation in opposite directions.

7. (HL) What causes the SRPC to shift outwards?

A negative supply shock or a rise in inflation expectations that raises nominal wage demands.

8. (HL) Why is the LRPC vertical?

Once workers adjust their expectations, nominal wages rise with prices, so unemployment returns to the NRU whatever the inflation rate.

9. Define the natural rate of unemployment.

Unemployment at full-employment output: the sum of frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment.

10. (HL) What shifts the LRPC left?

Supply-side policies that reduce the NRU, such as training, better job information and labour-market flexibility.

11. What is stagflation and what usually causes it?

Rising inflation with falling output and rising unemployment; usually a negative supply shock such as the 1970s oil crisis.

12. Name one policy that reconciles growth with equity.

A progressive income tax system (or transfer payments, or investment in education).

13. Why did economists doubt the Phillips curve in the 1970s?

Oil shocks produced high inflation and high unemployment together, which the original curve could not explain.
