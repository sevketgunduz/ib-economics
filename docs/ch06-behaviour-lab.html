Do people and firms really behave like the models say?

Economic models assume consumers maximise utility and firms maximise profit. Test those assumptions on yourself: take part in real behavioural experiments, design nudges, and see why firms often aim for something other than maximum profit.

    04

    Bounded rationality, self-control and selfishness

    Bounded rationality: limited time, information and brainpower, so people settle for “good enough”. Bounded self-control: people struggle to act in their long-run interest. Bounded selfishness: people care about fairness and others, not just themselves.

    A. Self-control: now or later?

    Answer both questions quickly.

    Q1. Would you rather have $100 today or $110 in one month?

    Q2. Would you rather have $100 in 12 months or $110 in 13 months?

    B. Selfishness: the ultimatum game

    You have $10 to split with a stranger. If they accept your offer, you both keep the money; if they reject it, you both get nothing. A purely selfish, rational stranger would accept even $1. How much will you offer?

    05

    Choice architecture and nudges

    A nudge changes how a choice is presented, without banning options or changing prices much. The three syllabus types are default choice (what happens if you do nothing), restricted choice (fewer options) and mandated choice (you must decide in advance). Design the school canteen below.

    Evaluating nudges

    ✓ StrengthsCheap to introduce · keep freedom of choice · work with how people really decide · can be large (pension auto-enrolment) · politically easier than taxes or bans
    ✗ LimitationsEffects can be small or fade over time · weak against addiction and strong habits · can be manipulative or unethical (“dark patterns”) · who decides what is “good” for people? · firms use nudges for profit, not welfare

    EAST framework (UK Behavioural Insights Team): make the desired behaviour Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely.
